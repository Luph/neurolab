# Kenya's geothermal steam-sales model: GDC's Menengai PISSAs, KenGen's Olkaria VI steam-supply PPP and the Olkaria III contrast

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest verified events: Globeleq's Menengai II financial close, announced 2 January 2024; Sosian's Menengai III grid supply, August 2023. Later commissioning of Menengai II and OrPower 22 was not verified.)

Cross-references: facts/geothermal-risk-facilities.md (GRMF and other exploration risk facilities in East Africa, Indonesia and Turkey; read it for the grant side); facts/sarulla.md (Indonesia, where the developer holds the whole resource and power chain); facts/lake-turkana.md (KPLC as offtaker and Kenyan contingent liabilities).

## Summary

Kenya splits geothermal development at the wellhead. A state entity takes the exploration and drilling risk and sells steam to private independent power producers (IPPs), which build and run the power plants and sell electricity to Kenya Power (KPLC). There are two state steam sellers:

- the Geothermal Development Company (GDC), created under the Energy Act 2006, at Menengai and other new fields;
- KenGen, the listed, majority state-owned generator, at Olkaria.

At Menengai, GDC signed Project Implementation and Steam Supply Agreements (PISSAs) with three IPPs of 35 MW each:
- Sosian Menengai Geothermal Power (Menengai III, grid supply from August 2023);
- Quantum Power East Africa, later Globeleq (Menengai II, USD 117 million, financial close announced January 2024);
- OrPower 22.

The tariff is split into a generation component (capped at USD 0.05 per kWh) and a steam component of USD 0.02 per kWh. KPLC pays the steam component through to GDC on net energy delivered. The African Development Fund backed a partial risk guarantee of approximately USD 11.27 million that covers both KPLC non-payment and GDC failure to deliver steam. At Olkaria, KenGen tendered a 140 MW "Olkaria VI" PPP in 2019-2020 on a BOOT basis: KenGen holds 25% of the SPV, supplies steam under a 25-year steam supply agreement, and KPLC buys power under a 25-year PPA. Five groups were shortlisted in May 2020. Both models contrast with Olkaria III (Ormat's OrPower 4), where the private developer developed the field itself, after KenGen supplied exploration data and some production wells.

## Verified facts

1. Kenya's Energy Act 2006 created GDC (alongside the Energy Regulatory Commission, KETRACO and the Rural Electrification Authority) to take on upstream geothermal risk: exploration, appraisal and production drilling, and the steam gathering system. Private IPPs then develop the power plants under what is called the PISSA model, after the Project Implementation and Steam Supply Agreement under which the IPP buys steam from GDC. A Climate Investment Funds and AfDB case study states that this model shifts exploration and resource risk away from the private investors. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Menengai Phase I: GDC owns and operates the steam field. Three IPPs each build, own and operate a 35 MW plant, signing a PISSA with GDC and a PPA with KPLC. They are Sosian Menengai Geothermal Energy Ltd, Quantum Power East Africa (QPEA) Menengai Ltd, and a consortium, OrPower Twenty-Two Ltd. They were selected after a tender in which 12 firms expressed interest. KETRACO built a substation and a roughly 7 km line to the 132 kV grid. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Payment structure (Box 5 of the case study):
   - generation component: capped at USD 0.05 per kWh;
   - steam component: USD 0.02 per kWh, paid to GDC by KPLC as a pass-through on net power delivered to the grid;
   - the IPP pays GDC for steam used for its own parasitic load (about 2 MW), described at USD 0.035 per kWh;
   - the overall tariff is capped at USD 0.085 per kWh, against the feed-in tariff ceiling of USD 0.088 per kWh for 35-70 MW geothermal plants over 20 years;
   - the agreed Menengai tariff is described as USD 0.07 per kWh, of which USD 0.02 is steam;
   - part of the tariff is scalable (20% for the first 12 years).

   GDC's steam price is below the true cost of steam development. [confidence: high (as reported by the case study); the scalable-portion detail is truncated in the source] [source: 1]
4. Credit support: at the government's request, the African Development Fund (through AfDB) provided a partial risk guarantee (PRG) of approximately USD 11.27 million. A commercial bank issues letters of credit, at the request of KPLC and GDC, which each IPP can draw on if KPLC fails to pay under the PPA or if the IPP cannot generate because GDC defaults under the PISSA. KPLC and GDC must reimburse the bank. The Kenyan Treasury indemnifies AfDB for any payment under the PRG, and the guarantee period is 15 years. The government's letter of support covers both the PISSA and the PPA. Negotiations on the security package concluded in June 2017, and the tender for the L/C bank was launched in October 2017. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. Concessional debt: in 2016 the project obtained USD 29.7 million of Clean Technology Fund concessional lending through AfDB for two IPP projects (USD 15 million per plant). The case study says this raised IRRs by 2 to 4 percentage points. The IPPs typically financed with about 30% equity. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. Resource confirmation was a gating issue: "most private investors will not consider providing finance until about 70 percent of the power capacity available in the wells has been drilled". Three reservoir studies estimated 150 MW, 133 MW and 90 MW. GDC's 2017 assessment confirmed a sustainable 105 MW plus a 20% reserve margin for the 25-year PPA period. OrPower 22 awaited a JICA-financed independent steam study in early 2018. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. Counterfactual cost: the case study, citing GDC (2014) and CPI (2015), estimates that tariffs would need to rise by 60 to 75%, to between USD 0.14 and 0.17 per kWh, if an IPP carried drilling and steam field development costs. [confidence: high (as a cited estimate)] [source: 1]
8. Sosian Menengai Geothermal Power Ltd's 35 MW plant began supplying the grid in August 2023 as the first Menengai IPP. The Energy Cabinet Secretary cited a power price of 6.9 US cents (about KES 9.6) per kWh. GDC drilled the wells, built the steam gathering system and sells steam to the three IPPs. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
9. Globeleq (then 70% British International Investment and 30% Norfund) announced financial close on 2 January 2024 for its 35 MW Menengai plant (Menengai II), at a total project cost of USD 117 million. The senior lenders were AfDB, Finnfund and the Eastern and Southern African Trade and Development Bank (TDB). It has a 25-year PISSA with GDC and a 25-year PPA with KPLC. The EPC contractor is Toyota Tsusho and its subsidiary CFAO Kenya, and commercial operation was expected in 2025. GDC envisages developing Menengai in phases toward about 465 MW, and the first 105 MW phase uses a steam gathering system of about 25 km. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
10. Olkaria VI PPP (KenGen as steam seller):
    - November 2019: KenGen sought qualifications for a 140 MW Olkaria PPP on a BOOT basis;
    - an SPV under Kenyan law would finance, design, build, commission, operate and maintain the plant;
    - KenGen would supply steam under a 25-year steam supply agreement, and KPLC would buy power under a 25-year PPA;
    - KenGen had an option to invest cash for a 25% interest in the SPV;
    - May 2020: five groups were shortlisted (Ormat Technologies; ITOCHU; Sumitomo; Enel Green Power; and Engie Energie Services with Toyota Tsusho, Kyuden International and DL Koisagat Tea Estate), under the PPP Act 2013, with an RFP planned for June 2020 and transfer to KenGen at the end of the PPA. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
11. The earlier (2013) structure for the same project, as described in an AfDB/SREP concept note: a 30-year concession, a 25-year PPA, and KenGen equity of up to 49%. The preferred bidder was to build the steam gathering system (estimated at USD 80 million) and transfer it to KenGen on commissioning. Financing was envisaged at 30/70 equity to debt. There would be no sovereign guarantee of KPLC's PPA obligations, which the note identified as a bankability barrier. [confidence: high (as a 2013-14 plan, superseded)] [source: 7]
12. Olkaria III contrast: Olkaria III (139 MW by 2018), owned by Ormat's OrPower 4, was Kenya's only privately operated geothermal plant as of 2018. Field development was financed mainly privately. KenGen provided exploration data and 8 MW of production wells. Financial close took from 1998 to 2009, and the first 84 MW were commissioned in 2013. The security package (2007) comprised a KPLC letter of credit and a government "letter of comfort", together with MIGA political risk cover. The tariff was about USD 0.09 per kWh. [confidence: medium-high (case study citing CPI 2015)] [source: 1]

## Financing and structure details

- Contract set per Menengai IPP:
  - PISSA with GDC: 25 years, steam priced per kWh delivered;
  - PPA with KPLC: 25 years;
  - government letter of support;
  - AfDB/ADF PRG through a standby L/C, covering both PPA non-payment and steam non-delivery, for 15 years;
  - CTF concessional loans (USD 15 million each for two plants) with commercial and DFI senior debt.
- Menengai II (Globeleq): USD 117 million cost, with AfDB, Finnfund and TDB senior lenders. Loan amounts per lender were not verified.
- Olkaria VI (KenGen): BOOT SPV with KenGen holding 25%, a 25-year steam supply agreement with KenGen and a 25-year PPA.
- Risk allocation: the state seller holds resource risk (and the reinjection and make-up well programme, in principle). The IPP holds plant construction, plant performance and O&M risk. KPLC credit risk and steam non-delivery risk are partly guaranteed by AfDB.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Right: the CIF and AfDB case study (source 1) attributes Menengai's private participation to the separation of resource risk, plus a security package that guaranteed both counterparties (KPLC as power buyer and GDC as steam seller), plus small amounts of concessional debt. Together these let the projects work within the feed-in tariff cap.
- Slow: Phase I PISSAs dated from 2014, but the first plant reached the grid only in August 2023, and the second reached financial close only in January 2024. The case study cites resource-confirmation uncertainty (reservoir estimates of 90 to 150 MW) and lengthy negotiation of the security package as delivery challenges (source 1).
- Interface risk: in a steam-sales model the IPP depends on a public steam seller's drilling, make-up wells and gathering system. This is why the PRG covers GDC default as well as KPLC default.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 72 (hydropower and geothermal): use Menengai to teach the steam-field and power-plant split (fw:geothermal-staging). The public entity carries exploration and drilling success risk, and the private IPP finances only the surface plant against a PISSA plus PPA. Show the tariff decomposition: generation USD 0.05 plus steam USD 0.02 per kWh, with the steam passed through by KPLC. Show the guarantee covering both counterparties, a two-sided interface that lenders insist on. Contrast it with Olkaria III, a fully private field, and Sarulla (facts/sarulla.md), where the developer holds the whole resource and power chain. Quantify the policy choice using the 60 to 75% tariff uplift estimated for an IPP that carries drilling.

## Do not state

- Do not state that KenGen currently sells steam to any operating private IPP at Olkaria. No operating KenGen-to-IPP steam sale was verified. Olkaria VI's award, financial close or construction status is unverified (some web sources conflate it with KenGen's own Olkaria I Unit 6).
- Do not state commercial operation dates for Globeleq's Menengai II or OrPower 22. Both were reported as expected in 2025-2026 but not verified. Ownership changes (including reports of a Kaishan acquisition of OrPower 22) are also unverified.
- Do not state per-lender amounts for Menengai II, or the size of PRG drawings, if any.
- Do not present the case study's USD 0.035 per kWh parasitic-load rate or the "scalable portion" terms as final contract terms. The source text is partly truncated.
- Do not state the 6.9 US cents per kWh as the contractual tariff. It was quoted by a minister in a news report.
