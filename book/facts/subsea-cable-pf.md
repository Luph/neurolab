# EASSy (Eastern Africa Submarine System) and WIOCC: a DFI-financed subsea telecom cable

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: WIOCC website descriptions viewed October 2026; latest dated event, the EASSy and SEACOM cable cuts off KwaZulu-Natal, 12 May to 3 June 2024)

Cross-references: facts/t-dfis.md (DFI mandates and instruments generally); facts/t-insurance-market.md (marine and property insurance context). No other sheet covers subsea cables.

## Summary

EASSy is a submarine fibre-optic cable along Africa's east coast, from South Africa to Sudan, with onward interconnection to Europe and Asia. It entered service in July 2010. African telecom operators planned it from an MOU signed by 28 operators in December 2003. It was built under a split structure, which is the reason it is taught in project finance:

- some operators invested directly as consortium members;
- the operators that could not fund their share alone pooled their interests in a special purpose vehicle, the West Indian Ocean Cable Company Ltd (WIOCC), incorporated in Mauritius. WIOCC borrowed from a group of development finance institutions (DFIs): IFC, the European Investment Bank, the African Development Bank, the Development Bank of Southern Africa, AFD/Proparco and KfW.

IFC put total project cost at USD 235 million and signed its own loan in November 2007. WIOCC became the largest shareholder in the EASSy consortium. It later grew into a pan-African wholesale "carriers' carrier" with fibre-pair interests in Meta-led 2Africa and Google's Equiano, data centres and terrestrial fibre. Its DFI relationships continued: an IFC USD 20 million expansion loan (signed 2017), an IFC COVID-19 facility loan (2020) and a USD 30 million IFC equity investment (2022). The case shows how DFI project debt can make a consortium cable bankable when several members are small, state-owned or below investment grade. It also shows the open-access condition DFIs attach, and the outage and repair risk that applies to any cable.

## Verified facts

1. Twenty-eight telecom operators from East and Southern Africa signed an MOU for EASSy in December 2003. IFC described the sponsors as predominantly well-established African carriers, an approximately equal mix of government-owned and private institutions. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. In July 2006 the DFIs reached consensus and proposed up to USD 170 million of private project-finance debt to complement operator equity. The participating institutions included the African Development Bank, the European Investment Bank and the World Bank Group. Additional concessional funding from governments and donors was mentioned as a possible gap filler. [confidence: medium (trade press report of the proposal)] [source: 3]
3. IFC's Summary of Proposed Investment for project 25340, "EASSy Cable", gives:
   - total project cost of USD 235 million, covering both the SPV and the consortium operators;
   - an IFC loan of USD 32.7 million (originally estimated at up to USD 30 million);
   - co-financiers EIB, AfDB, DBSA, AFD/Proparco and KfW;
   - IFC board approval on 2 August 2007, signing on 21 November 2007 and investment completion on 12 June 2008. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. IFC described the structure as two groups of operators. "SPV Operators" invest through a Mauritius-incorporated SPV, West Indian Ocean Cable Company Ltd, which receives DFI financing. "Consortium Operators" invest directly. The SPV was to be a commercial entity that "will manage, market and sell or lease its capacity" to SPV operators and other authorised third parties, on competitive, non-discriminatory (open-access) terms. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
5. At appraisal, IFC described an approximately 8,500 km route from South Africa to Sudan with landing stations in eight countries: South Africa, Mozambique, Madagascar, Tanzania, Kenya, Somalia, Djibouti and Sudan. Initial equipped capacity was 20 Gbit/s, with design capacity of 320 Gbit/s. [confidence: high (as appraised)] [source: 1, 2]
6. WIOCC now describes EASSy as a 10,000 km system with nine landing stations, in Sudan, Djibouti, Somalia, Kenya, Tanzania, Comoros, Madagascar, Mozambique and South Africa. It gives total capacity of 10 Tbit/s, says the system provides backhaul for at least 12 landlocked countries, and calls WIOCC "the largest shareholder in the EASSy consortium". The gap between the appraisal and current figures (route, landings, capacity a few hundred times higher) reflects later scope and repeated upgrades of terminal equipment. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
7. EASSy went live on 16 July 2010, with commercial service from 30 July 2010. Alcatel-Lucent was the supply contractor (contract signed 2007). [confidence: medium (encyclopedic secondary source; consistent with IFC timing)] [source: 6]
8. WIOCC says it was established in 2008 and is founded and owned by 10 major African telecom operators. It names IFC, Proparco, the Emerging Africa Infrastructure Fund and Ninety One among its funders and supporters. IFC's 2016 disclosure described it as jointly owned by 14 African operators, including Djibouti Telecom, Telecomunicações de Moçambique, Telkom Kenya, Uganda Telecom and Zanzibar Telecom. [confidence: high (shareholder count changed over time)] [source: 5, 7]
9. IFC project 38381 ("WIOCC Investment") was an A loan of up to USD 20 million within a USD 56 million programme, funded otherwise by internal cash. It was approved on 2 November 2016, signed on 1 May 2017 and invested on 25 May 2018. Uses included buying additional capacity, upgrading WIOCC's capacity on EASSy, interconnecting with other fibre systems and buying network equipment. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
10. On 28 September 2022 IFC announced a USD 30 million equity investment in WIOCC Group. It followed a USD 20 million IFC loan in 2020 under IFC's COVID-19 facility. IFC described itself as "one of the founding lenders in 2007". IFC named WIOCC as a strategic investor in EASSy, 2Africa, Equiano and WACS. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
11. WIOCC reports approximately USD 950 million invested in digital infrastructure assets, more than 200,000 km of subsea systems, more than 115,000 km of terrestrial fibre, and fibre-pair ownership on Equiano and 2Africa. These are company-reported figures. [confidence: high (as company claims)] [source: 4, 5]
12. On 12 May 2024 the EASSy and SEACOM cables were damaged off KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. A dragging ship anchor was suspected. Repairs were completed by 3 June 2024. Burundi, Malawi, Mozambique, Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania and Uganda relied mainly on these two cables for access to hyperscale cloud services in South Africa. No country suffered a total outage because traffic was rerouted, although the rerouting congested alternative routes. [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

- Total cost: USD 235 million (IFC, covering both the SPV and the consortium operators).
- Owners of the system: a consortium (club) of operators under a construction and maintenance agreement. WIOCC is the largest member. The agreement name and terms were not verified, and "C&MA" is the general industry term.
- Borrower of DFI debt: WIOCC (Mauritius SPV), on-selling and leasing capacity to its operator shareholders and third parties.
- DFI lenders: IFC (USD 32.7 million), EIB, AfDB, DBSA, AFD/Proparco and KfW. The amounts for lenders other than IFC were not verified.
- Conditions: open access and non-discriminatory capacity sales (IFC disclosure).
- Later funding: IFC USD 20 million A loan (2017), IFC USD 20 million COVID-19 loan (2020), IFC USD 30 million equity (2022). WIOCC names the Emerging Africa Infrastructure Fund, Proparco and Ninety One as funders.
- Revenue model: wholesale sales of capacity, either as indefeasible rights of use (IRUs) with upfront payment plus O&M charges, or as leased capacity. This is the general model. WIOCC's specific contract mix and revenues were not verified.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Right: the SPV structure let smaller and state-owned operators join a USD 235 million system with non-recourse DFI debt rather than corporate balance sheets. IFC's appraisal framed the open-access SPV as the means to competitive bandwidth pricing (source 1). WIOCC's later growth, and its fibre-pair stakes in 2Africa and Equiano, show the SPV becoming a durable corporate platform that DFIs kept financing (sources 5, 7, 8).
- Risk realised: a single anchor strike on two parallel cables in May 2024 cut capacity for seven countries for about three weeks (source 9). Lender cases for cables need repair-ship arrangements, route diversity and the business-interruption effect of outages in their downside cases.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 82 (digital infrastructure): use EASSy to show how a consortium cable is financed. The system is owned by a club, while the bankable borrower is an SPV that aggregates the shares of weaker members and on-sells capacity. Contrast it with hyperscaler-led systems such as 2Africa and Equiano, where content companies fund most of the cost from their balance sheets and carriers like WIOCC buy fibre pairs. Use the evolution from a 20 Gbit/s initial capacity to 10 Tbit/s to show why cable lenders size debt on the dry-plant life (often about 25 years) while revenue comes from repeated terminal-equipment upgrades. Use the 2024 cut to build a downside case around repair time and diversity.

## Do not state

- Do not state a figure of USD 70.7 million of total DFI debt, or that IFC lent USD 18.2 million. These figures circulate in secondary sources, but IFC's own disclosure says USD 32.7 million, and the totals could not be reconciled.
- Do not state individual loan amounts for EIB, AfDB, DBSA, KfW or AFD. The AfDB project page blocked automated access (HTTP 403), and no EIB primary figure was found.
- Do not state the World Bank "partially funded" EASSy as cable debt. Any World Bank involvement was through regional connectivity programmes, and the details were not verified.
- Do not state an exact ownership percentage for WIOCC in EASSy, or the current list of WIOCC shareholders.
- Do not state the dry-plant design life, IRU pricing or WIOCC revenues as facts about EASSy. They are generic industry norms, unverified for this system.
- Do not state the cause of the 2024 cut as established. It was "suspected" anchor dragging.
- For 2Africa and PEACE, no financing data was verified in this research. Do not attribute DFI or ECA financing to them.
