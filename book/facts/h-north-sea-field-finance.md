# North Sea field financings in the 1970s (BP Forties, 1972) and the shift to limited recourse

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Petroleum and Submarine Pipe-lines Act 1975, Royal Assent 12 November 1975; a 1980 journal article is cited for context)

## Summary

The UK North Sea in the 1970s is usually cited as the place where limited-recourse lending against a single field's future cash flows moved from US onshore production payments into large offshore developments. It was the bridge between oil and gas lending and modern project finance. The best-documented anchor is BP's 1972 financing of the Forties field. The International Finance Corporation's 1999 history of project finance records it as a milestone in which BP raised $945 million from a syndicate of 66 banks to develop Forties. The IFC also characterises project finance before the 1970s as "previously confined mainly to [the] natural resource sector". The UK state shaped the financing environment around these deals:
- the Oil Taxation Act 1975 (Royal Assent 8 May 1975) created a new tax, petroleum revenue tax, on profits from licensed petroleum;
- the Petroleum and Submarine Pipe-lines Act 1975 (12 November 1975) set up the British National Oil Corporation;
- the same Act let the Secretary of State lend up to GBP 50 million to oil companies, and guarantee up to a further GBP 600 million of their borrowing, to facilitate development;
- it also authorised up to GBP 350 million to make good Bank of England losses on loans and guarantees to the Burmah Oil Company.

A 1980 study of British clearing banks and North Sea financing concluded that the banks changed their lending techniques to meet the oil industry's needs, and that development had not been held back for lack of funding. Many popular accounts add further details: a sterling figure for Forties, a named forward-purchase vehicle, and precise recourse terms. These could not be verified against primary sources for this sheet and are listed under "Do not state".

## Verified facts

1. **Forties financing.** The IFC's 1999 "Milestones in Project Financing" (Box 2.1) lists, for the 1970s: "U.K.: British Petroleum raised $945m from a syndicate of 66 banks to develop its Forties field in the North Sea (1972)." [confidence: medium; single institutional source, consistent with the commonly cited sterling figure but not independently confirmed here] [source: 1]
2. **Where project finance stood before the 1970s.** The same IFC box describes the 1970s as the period when foundations for project financing were laid in the power sector, having "previously" been "confined mainly to [the] natural resource sector". [confidence: high, as IFC's characterisation] [source: 1]
3. **Petroleum revenue tax.** The Oil Taxation Act 1975 (c. 22) received Royal Assent on 8 May 1975. Its long title is "An Act to impose a new tax in respect of profits from substances won or capable of being won under the authority of licences granted under the Petroleum (Production) Act 1934" (and the Northern Ireland equivalent). The new tax is petroleum revenue tax. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
4. **BNOC and state finance powers.** The Petroleum and Submarine Pipe-lines Act 1975 (c. 74) received Royal Assent on 12 November 1975. It established the British National Oil Corporation. It also authorised "loans and guarantees in connection with the development of the petroleum resources of the United Kingdom" and payments in respect of certain Bank of England guarantees and loans. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
5. **Section 42 of the 1975 Act.** Where expedient "for the purpose of facilitating or maintaining the development of the petroleum resources of the United Kingdom", the Secretary of State, with Treasury approval, may:
   - make loans to an oil company; or
   - guarantee principal and interest on loans made to an oil company by another person.

   Outstanding loans are capped at GBP 50 million, and guarantees (plus unrecovered guarantee payments) at a further GBP 600 million. Each loan or guarantee must be reported to both Houses of Parliament. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
6. **Section 43 (Burmah Oil).** The Secretary of State may pay the Bank of England up to GBP 350 million in aggregate to make good losses it suffers from guarantees given for loans to the Burmah Oil Company Limited or its subsidiaries, or from loans the Bank made to them. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
7. **Banks adapted their lending.** A 1980 article by R. G. Baird reviewed North Sea financing for both oil-company exploration and development and onshore suppliers. Its abstract says it examined "the changes induced" in the British clearing banks' "lending techniques and habits by the demands of the oil industry". It concluded that "financial provision has been adequate" and that neither the pace of oil development nor British industry's offshore participation suffered "for lack of ready funding". [confidence: high, as to the article's stated conclusions] [source: 6]

## Timeline

- 1972: BP raises $945 million from 66 banks for the Forties field development (IFC). [source: 1]
- 8 May 1975: Oil Taxation Act 1975 (petroleum revenue tax) receives Royal Assent. [source: 2]
- 12 November 1975: Petroleum and Submarine Pipe-lines Act 1975 receives Royal Assent. It establishes BNOC, gives the government loan and guarantee powers (GBP 50 million and GBP 600 million), and provides for the Burmah Oil indemnity to the Bank of England (up to GBP 350 million). [sources: 3, 4, 5]
- June 1980: Baird's article on clearing banks and North Sea financing is published in Managerial and Decision Economics, vol. 1. [source: 6]

## Financing and structure details

- **Verified.** The Forties financing involved a syndicate of 66 banks and USD 945 million (IFC's figure). [source: 1]
- **Not verified here.** The borrower entity, the currency mix, the tenor, the precise recourse terms (including any completion support from BP), and the use of a forward-purchase or "production payment" vehicle. See "Do not state".
- **State backstop in place by late 1975.** The government had statutory power to lend and to guarantee third-party loans to oil companies to keep development moving. The Bank of England had separately supported Burmah Oil, which had North Sea interests; that link is not verified here. [sources: 4, 5]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **IFC (1999).** IFC treats the North Sea, with BP Forties as the marker, as part of the 1970s foundation of modern project finance. Limited-recourse techniques, previously mainly used for natural resources, were scaled up for very large capital programmes. [source: 1]
- **Baird (1980).** Baird concluded that the UK banking system adapted its techniques successfully and that funding was adequate. [source: 6]
- **The statutes.** By 1975 the UK government was willing to stand behind North Sea development finance. It did so through loan and guarantee powers and the Burmah Oil indemnity, which shows how far sovereign policy and fiscal regimes shaped field economics and bankability. PRT changed after-tax field cash flows, which lenders had to model. [sources: 2, 4, 5]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 3 (How project finance evolved).** Use Forties as the bridge from US production payments to large-scale limited-recourse field development finance:
  - a single field, very large capex, a bank syndicate of dozens of lenders, and repayment tied to field output;
  - the lesson of the era: once reserves are engineered and quantified, lenders will take development and price risk on a defined asset, but only inside a stable fiscal and legal framework;
  - the 1975 tax and BNOC legislation shows how quickly that framework can move, so host-government terms become a credit variable (forward reference to political and regulatory risk in Part III).

  Keep the deal specifics at the verified level: 1972, 66 banks, USD 945 million.

## Do not state

- Do not give a GBP figure (often quoted as about GBP 360–370 million) as a verified fact. It was not confirmed in a primary or institutional source here. If used, present it only as a conversion of IFC's USD figure, and mark it approximate.
- Do not name a forward-purchase company (for example "Norex") or describe the deal as a "production payment" or "forward purchase" structure as verified fact. Neither was confirmed.
- Do not state the Forties loan tenor, pricing, completion guarantee terms, or that it was "the first" non-recourse loan in Europe. None was verified.
- Do not state the dates of the Forties discovery, first oil (often given as 1975) or the royal inauguration as verified. They are widely reported, but they were not confirmed in a primary source for this sheet.
- Do not assert which specific fields, if any, used the section 42 guarantee power, or the details of the Burmah Oil rescue beyond what section 43 states.
