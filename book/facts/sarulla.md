# Sarulla geothermal (Indonesia): resource risk, JBIC/ADB

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Ormat Technologies' Form 10-K for fiscal 2025, filed 26 February 2026, reporting Sarulla's remediation drilling plans)

## Summary

Sarulla is a 330 MW geothermal complex in Tapanuli Utara, North Sumatra, Indonesia. It has three units of about 110 MW each: SIL at the Silangkitang field, and NIL 1 and NIL 2 at the Namora-I-Langit field. At completion in 2018 it was among the largest single-contract geothermal developments in the world.

Unocal began exploring the field in 1993 but stopped in 1998 after the Asian financial crisis, and the rights passed to the state utility PLN. A consortium of Medco, Ormat and Itochu won PLN's re-tender in the mid-2000s, and Kyushu Electric joined in 2008. The project rests on four contracts:

- a 30-year Joint Operation Contract (JOC) with concession holder Pertamina Geothermal Energy (PGE);
- a 30-year Energy Sales Contract (ESC) with PLN, with both contracts executed (as amended) on 4 April 2013;
- a 20-year guarantee from Indonesia's Ministry of Finance;
- an approximately USD 1.17 billion limited-recourse loan package for a project of approximately USD 1.6 billion.

Loan documents were signed on 28 March 2014, and financial close followed on 16 May 2014. JBIC and the Asian Development Bank (ADB) were lead structuring banks. JBIC lent directly and guaranteed six commercial banks against political risk. ADB lent from its own resources and from the Clean Technology Fund (CTF) and the Canadian Climate Fund.

The three units reached commercial operation between March 2017 and May 2018. The drilling programme overran in time and cost. Since then, generation has fallen because of wellfield problems at one of the plants and equipment failures. In 2022 the borrowers agreed a remediation framework with the lenders, and more make-up drilling is planned from 2026. Sarulla is therefore a live example of geothermal resource risk persisting into operations.

## Financing and structure details

- **Senior debt (approximately USD 1.17 billion):**
  - JBIC direct loan, USD 492 million;
  - JBIC-guaranteed commercial tranche (six banks), USD 329 million;
  - ADB ordinary capital, USD 250 million;
  - CTF, USD 80 million, administered by ADB;
  - Canadian Climate Fund, USD 20 million, administered by ADB.
- **Tenor and pricing.** About 4 years of construction plus a 16-year term, 20 years in total. The debt is mostly LIBOR-linked, with up to USD 0.96 billion swapped at 3.4565%.
- **Sovereign and offtake support.** The structure combines a 30-year ESC with PLN, a 30-year JOC with PGE (which keeps title to the concession), and a 20-year Ministry of Finance guarantee.
- **Sponsor support.** Lenders required a contingent equity facility and contingencies. Ormat's filings list an "Ormat Equity Support Deed" dated 28 March 2014 among the finance documents.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right: reaching close after two decades.** Ormat's authors (Wolf and Gabbay, GRC 2015) credit several factors. Unocal's earlier exploration data and the reservoir modelling reduced resource uncertainty. The JBIC and ADB anchor role, with JBIC political risk cover for banks, brought in commercial lenders. Sovereign support came from the MoF guarantee. Lenders insisted on contingent equity, DSCR headroom and a debt tenor well inside the 30-year contract.
- **Problem: resource and drilling risk was not fully retired at COD.** Ormat reported drilling delays and cost overruns during construction (FY2017 10-K). It later reported falling generation from wellfield issues and equipment failures at one plant, requiring a lender-approved remediation and make-up drilling programme (FY2023 and FY2025 10-Ks). Geothermal output depends on reservoir behaviour that drilling and simulation cannot fully predict. Lenders' protection therefore lies in reserve accounts, make-up well budgets and the ability to approve recovery plans.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 72 (hydropower and geothermal).** Sarulla shows how geothermal project finance handles resource risk:
  - inherited exploration data and reservoir simulation before close;
  - drilling as a construction-period risk, with overruns that did happen;
  - lenders sizing debt against a long, sovereign-backed sale contract while keeping tenor shorter than the contract;
  - resource decline after COD that forces a lender-approved remediation and make-up drilling programme.

  Pair it with the technology choice (combined-cycle steam plus binary units with full reinjection) to show how plant design responds to reservoir chemistry and sustainability.
