# Upstream decommissioning security (UK DSAs and Australia's financial assurance)

As of: 2026-10-03 (UK OPRED guidance "Assessing the financial capability of offshore oil and gas companies to deliver decommissioning obligations", April 2019, still linked from the GOV.UK decommissioning page updated 25 September 2026; Finance Bill measure on decommissioning relief agreements, 26 November 2025; Australian OPGGS Act 2006 compilation dated 24 August 2026)

## Summary

Offshore decommissioning costs arrive at the end of field life, when revenues have dwindled and the late-life owner may be the weakest. Regulators therefore spread liability widely and require security. In the UK, the Petroleum Act 1998 lets the Secretary of State serve section 29 notices, which require submission and execution of a decommissioning programme. These can be served on current and former licensees and on parent or associated companies, and the notice is usually not withdrawn when a seller exits. Under section 38(4) the Secretary of State can also require financial security. In practice, co-venturers agree decommissioning security agreements (DSAs) among themselves, usually on the industry template. They post letters of credit, cash or on-demand bonds from A-rated institutions, sized by a formula equal to post-tax decommissioning cost plus a risk margin, less the post-tax value of remaining reserves. Parent company guarantees are expressly not acceptable as DSA security. Decommissioning Relief Deeds (DRDs), introduced by the Finance Act 2013, let security be posted on a post-tax basis because they fix the tax relief available. From 26 November 2025, DRD payments cannot be made by reference to the Energy Profits Levy. Australia's Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage Act 2006 requires titleholders to maintain financial assurance at all times, in forms from insurance to a mortgage. The Act also lets the responsible Commonwealth Minister direct former titleholders, related bodies corporate, and others who benefited from or influenced the operations to plug wells and remediate (often called trailing liability). For RBL and acquisition lenders, these rules mean decommissioning is senior in substance. It consumes borrowing base and liquidity through letters of credit, and it survives a sale.

## Verified facts

United Kingdom (jurisdiction: UK Continental Shelf; regulator OPRED within DESNZ; tax HMRC)
1. Section 29 notices under the Petroleum Act 1998 may be given to persons with interests listed in section 30(1) (installations) and section 30(2) (pipelines). They require the recipient to submit a decommissioning programme when called for. Withdrawal is at the Secretary of State's discretion under section 31(5). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. OPRED runs routine financial checks three or four times a year for every UKCS field with an approved field development plan, and also when interests are bought or sold. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Company risk classification uses the share of decommissioning cost as a percentage of net worth (shareholder funds minus intangibles): 0–50% low risk, 51–70% medium risk, 71% and above high risk. OPRED then classifies the whole section 29 group. A non-UK-registered company may be discounted. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. OPRED has two mitigation powers. It can extend section 29 notices to parent or associated companies (section 30(1)(e) and section 30(2)(c)), and it can require security under section 38(4), "normally... in the form of a decommissioning security agreement". It can also demand financial information under section 38. On asset sales, "in the vast majority of cases the section 29 notice is unlikely to be withdrawn from the selling party". [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. For new fields developed by weaker companies, OPRED may require security against premature decommissioning, may suspend it after satisfactory production, and may reinstate it near end of life. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. Acceptable DSA security, where the Secretary of State requires it, is cash, irrevocable standby letters of credit from a prime bank, or on-demand bonds from prime banks or FSMA-regulated insurers. Issuers must be established in an EU or OECD country with a UK office and rated A- (S&P/Fitch) or A3 (Moody's) or better. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. Parent company guarantees "are not an acceptable form of decommissioning security as they do not meet the criteria of being irrevocable and on-demand". [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. The DSA should be on a full-field basis, covering each party's share of post-tax decommissioning cost (where a DRD exists and there is a relevant tax history). It typically includes a risk factor of about 10–30% added to the cost estimate, and an allowance for post-tax future revenues where production is predictable. Salvage value is allowed only for FPSO-type facilities. After the main removal works, ongoing security of 1–3% of total decommissioning cost covers site clear-up. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. Security is normally renewed annually, two months before the next period. Failure to renew is a default: the letter of credit or bond is drawn and the cash is placed in a regulated trust fund. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. Cost and reserve estimates used for the security calculation must be redone at least every three years (annually if timing requires), verified by an OPRED-approved independent expert. The DSA must stand alone from the joint operating agreement. A template DSA is published by the industry body (Oil & Gas UK, now Offshore Energies UK). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. DRDs (Finance Act 2013) guarantee the amount of relief that would have been available at the enactment date of 17 July 2013. Where one party's default imposes costs on another, they give that party relief at 30% (ring-fence corporation tax) and 20% (supplementary charge). This lets companies give decommissioning security on a post-tax rather than gross basis. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
12. A Finance (No. 2) Bill measure inserts section 80(4A)–(4B) into FA 2013. No payment is to be made under a decommissioning relief agreement by reference to the Energy (Oil and Gas) Profits Levy, for expenditure incurred on or after 26 November 2025. [confidence: high as draft legislation published by HMRC; check Royal Assent status] [source: 3]

Australia (jurisdiction: Commonwealth offshore areas; OPGGS Act 2006)
13. Section 571 requires a petroleum titleholder, at all times while the title is in force, to maintain financial assurance sufficient to meet costs, expenses and liabilities arising from the petroleum activity and from compliance with the Act. Examples include remediation directions. Regulations may make acceptable financial assurance a condition of NOPSEMA's acceptance of an environment plan. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
14. Permitted forms (without limitation, and combinable) are insurance, self-insurance, a bond, a cash deposit with a financial institution, an indemnity or other surety, a letter of credit, and a mortgage. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
15. Section 586A lets the responsible Commonwealth Minister direct plugging of wells, conservation of resources and making good of seabed damage. Directions can go to the registered holder, a related body corporate, any former registered holder, a person who was a related body corporate of a former holder while the title was in force, or a person the Minister determines. In making that determination the Minister considers financial benefit from the operations, the ability to influence compliance, and acting jointly with a holder. Section 587A covers titles that have ceased to be in force. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
16. Chapter 5A of the Act requires approval of a change in control of a registered titleholder (sections 566B–566K). [confidence: high] [source: 4]

## Financing and structure details

- **UK DSA mechanics.** Trustee-held security, a formula-based amount (post-tax cost × (1 + risk factor) − post-tax value of remaining reserves), annual LC renewal, draw on failure to renew, and joint and several liability with pro rata first-loss sharing on default. [source: 1]
- **Interaction with RBLs.** DSA letters of credit are typically issued under the borrower's RBL letter of credit sublimit, so they consume capacity. This is inferred from the structures in t-rbl.md and not separately verified as a market statistic.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The UK approach treats the late-life owner as the default risk. It refuses parent company guarantees because the parent is likely to be distressed at the same time as the subsidiary, and it keeps sellers liable. That explains why buyers of mature assets must show decommissioning capacity, and why sellers often retain obligations or take security. [source: 1]
- Australia's expansion of directions to former holders and related parties reflects a policy choice to reach whoever benefited from the operations. [source: 4]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 65.** Use the UK DSA formula to teach security sizing: post-tax cost plus a 10–30% risk factor, less the net value of remaining reserves, with security rising as reserves deplete. Explain why PCGs are rejected and how trust-held, on-demand instruments protect against co-venturer insolvency. Contrast with Australia's broad financial assurance and trailing-liability powers.
- **Chapter 75.** Show how decommissioning liability affects upstream finance. LCs consume RBL capacity, section 29 liability survives a sale, and acquisition finance must price the decommissioning tail and DRD tax relief, including the 2025 EPL carve-out.
