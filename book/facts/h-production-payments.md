# Oil and gas production payments in the US (1930s–1970s): mechanics and tax history

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: the Tax Reform Act of 1969, signed 30 December 1969, with a 1976 technical amendment; a 2012 filing is cited only to show the later volumetric production payment form)

## Summary

A mineral production payment is a right to a set share of the output of an oil, gas or other mineral property, or to the cash proceeds of that output, until a stated sum (usually with interest) has been paid out. It is payable only from production, if and when production occurs. The US Senate Finance Committee described it in 1969 as secured by an interest in the minerals, lasting for less than the expected life of the property, and usually bearing interest. The assignee looks only to the oil and not to the owner's general credit. That makes it the clearest US ancestor of the limited-recourse, cash-flow-based lending that project finance later generalised. Production payments appear in Supreme Court tax litigation from the 1930s. Thomas v. Perkins (1937) concerned a $395,000 oil payment payable out of one quarter of a Texas lease's oil, with no personal obligation on the operator. The tax treatment of production payments drove their structure. Before 1969 an owner could sell ("carve out") a production payment to a bank and report the cash as current income. A buyer of a property could also finance the purchase with a "retained" production payment, which the seller then sold to a financial institution (the "A-B-C transaction"). Either way the debt was effectively repaid with pre-tax dollars. The Senate Finance Committee reported carved-out payments of $214 million in 1965 and $540 million in 1966, and A-B-C transactions of $1.85 billion in 1966. Congress responded in the Tax Reform Act of 1969 by adding Internal Revenue Code section 636. From 7 August 1969 it treats carved-out production payments as mortgage loans and retained ones as purchase-money mortgage loans. This ended most of the tax arbitrage, but the economic idea survived: lending against a defined slice of future production, with recourse only to the reserves.

## Verified facts

1. **Definition used by Congress.** In its 1969 report the Senate Finance Committee defined a mineral production payment as "a right to a specified share of the production from a mineral property (or a sum of money in place of the production) when that production occurs". It added that the payment is secured by an interest in the minerals, the right lasts for a period shorter than the expected life of the property, and the payment usually bears interest. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. **Three forms (pre-1969).** The same report described three forms. In a *carved-out* production payment, the owner sells a portion of future production, usually for cash and "quite often" to a financial institution. In a *retained* production payment, the owner sells the working interest but reserves a production payment. In an *A-B-C transaction*, owner A sells the property to B and reserves an interest-bearing production payment for a major portion of the price, then sells that payment to C. C was usually a financial institution, or perhaps a tax-exempt organisation. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. **Why Congress called them loans.** The committee said all three were "similar, in fact, to a loan transaction" secured by a mortgage on the property, with the "borrower" not personally liable. It compared the A-B-C deal to selling an apartment building subject to a mortgage that is then sold to a third party. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. **Pre-1969 tax effects** (as described by the committee):
   - **Carve-outs:** advancing income without the related expense let taxpayers get around limits based on net or taxable income. The chief one was the 50% taxable-income limitation on percentage depletion; others were the foreign tax credit limitation and loss and credit carryover limits.
   - **A-B-C transactions:** the buyer B effectively paid off what was in substance a loan out of before-tax dollars. The production payment in a typical case could be as much as 90% of the well's income.
   - **The lender C:** took cost depletion on the payments it received.

   [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. **Market size reported to Congress:**
   - reported carved-out production payment transactions totalled $214 million in 1965 and $540 million in 1966, a revenue loss of $70 million in 1966;
   - reported A-B-C transactions totalled $1.85 billion in 1966;
   - coal properties had recently been sold subject to retained production payments of approximately $800 million, although such payments had been "almost unknown" in coal a few years earlier;
   - the committee estimated the combined revenue loss from A-B-C transactions and carve-outs at $200–350 million a year.

   [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. **Pre-1969 loan treatment where guaranteed.** Before the 1969 Act, a production payment was already treated as a loan if its payout was "in any manner guaranteed": in a carve-out, by the person who created it; in an A-B-C deal, by B. The economic-interest (non-loan) treatment therefore depended on the payment being truly non-recourse. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. **Statutory change.** IRC section 636 was added by Pub. L. 91-172 (the Tax Reform Act of 1969), title V, §503(a), on 30 December 1969. It provides:
   - **§636(a):** a production payment carved out of mineral property is treated "as if it were a mortgage loan on the property" and does not qualify as an economic interest. There is an exception for carve-outs for exploration or development where the creator would not otherwise realise gross income.
   - **§636(b):** a production payment retained on a sale is treated as a purchase-money mortgage loan.
   - **§636(c):** a production payment retained by a lessor in a lease is treated, as far as the lessee is concerned, as a bonus payable in instalments.

   [confidence: high] [source: 2]
8. **Effective date.** Section 636 applies to production payments created on or after 7 August 1969. Payments created before 1 January 1971 under a binding contract entered into before 7 August 1969 are excepted, and a taxpayer could elect earlier application. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
9. **Thomas v. Perkins, 301 U.S. 655 (decided 1 June 1937).**
   - **Facts:** the owners of undeveloped Texas oil and gas leases assigned them for $10 cash plus $395,000 "to be paid out of the oil produced and saved". The payment was one quarter of all oil produced until the sum was paid. The instrument stated that the payment was "payable out of oil only, if, as and when produced" and was not a personal obligation of the assignee.
   - **Holding:** the Supreme Court held that the assignors had withheld that oil from the grant. The amounts paid to them were therefore not gross income of the operator.

   [confidence: high] [source: 3]
10. **Commissioner v. P. G. Lake, Inc., 356 U.S. 260 (decided 14 April 1958).**
    - **Facts:** in 1950 Lake, an oil and gas producer, assigned its president an oil payment right of $600,000 plus 3% annual interest, payable out of 25% of the oil from its working interest in two leases. The assignment was in cancellation of a $600,000 debt. The right paid out in a little over three years. In the companion O'Connor case, the purchaser of a $10,000,000 oil payment borrowed $9,990,350 at 3.5% interest, secured only by a deed of trust on the oil payment.
    - **Holding:** the Court held that the consideration for carved-out oil (and sulphur) payments was ordinary income subject to depletion, not capital gain. It reasoned that the lump sum was "essentially a substitute for what would otherwise be received at a future time as ordinary income".

    [confidence: high] [source: 4]
11. **Later form: the volumetric production payment (VPP).** Chesapeake Energy's 10-K for 2011 (filed 29 February 2012) defines a VPP as a limited-term overriding royalty interest with five features:
    - it entitles the purchaser to scheduled production volumes from specific lease interests;
    - it is free of future production costs and capital expenditure;
    - it is "nonrecourse to the seller (i.e., the purchaser's only recourse is to the reserves acquired)";
    - it transfers title to the reserves;
    - the seller keeps the remaining reserves.

    The company used VPPs as one of several asset-monetisation tools. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

## Financing and structure details

- **Parties.** These are the mineral owner or operator (the "A" or the carving-out owner), the purchaser of the working interest ("B"), and the holder of the production payment ("C"). The Senate report says C was usually a financial institution. [source: 1]
- **Payment mechanics** (as seen in Thomas v. Perkins):
  - the payment was a fixed fraction of production (one quarter) until a fixed dollar sum was reached;
  - pipeline purchasers paid the payment holder directly under division orders signed by all interested parties;
  - the operator had no personal obligation;
  - the payment bore none of the development expenses.

  [source: 3]
- **Interest.** Interest was usually built in, either explicitly (the 3% in P. G. Lake) or through the payment amount. [sources: 1, 4]
- **Credit.** Recourse ran only to the minerals. A guarantee by the creator (or by B) converted the arrangement into a loan for tax purposes even before 1969. [source: 1]
- **Lender appetite.** In O'Connor the payment's pay-out was regarded as so assured that its purchaser borrowed 99.9% of the price against it alone. The Court cited this to show that the cash flows could be predicted with "considerable accuracy". [source: 4]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **The Senate Finance Committee's analysis (1969).** The instrument's economics were a non-recourse secured loan. Tax law, however, treated it as a transfer of an economic interest in the minerals, so the tax treatment differed sharply from that of an ordinary mortgage loan. Taxpayers used the gap to accelerate income, to get around depletion and credit limits, and to repay acquisition debt with untaxed income. The committee concluded there was "no reason" a production payment borrower should repay with tax-free dollars when borrowers in other industries could not. [source: 1]
- **The Supreme Court's analysis (1958).** In P. G. Lake the Court looked through form to substance. It called the oil payment assignments "transparent devices" whose essence was the present value of future ordinary income. [source: 4]
- **Lesson.** The tax change removed the arbitrage. It did not remove the commercial logic of lending against identified, engineered reserves with recourse limited to production. That logic carried into reserve-based lending and, later, VPPs (see fact 11).

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 3 (How project finance evolved).** Open the history with production payments. They show the core project finance idea decades before the term was common: a lender takes repayment only from the output of a defined asset, sized against engineering estimates of that output. Two points are worth drawing out. First, the structure's popularity in the 1950s and 1960s owed as much to tax as to credit logic. Second, Congress's 1969 decision to recharacterise these deals as loans is an early example of a theme the book returns to: legal form, tax and accounting treatment often shape structures as much as risk allocation does. Link forward to the Chapter 2 comparison of reserve-based lending with project finance.

## Do not state

- Do not claim that Texas or Dallas banks "invented" project finance, or name a specific first production payment loan or bank. No primary source was verified for either claim.
- Do not give specific 1930s–1950s market volumes. The only verified volume figures are the 1965–1966 Treasury-reported figures quoted by the Senate Finance Committee.
- Do not say that production payments disappeared after 1969. They continued in other forms, including VPPs. Equally, do not assert how common they were in the 1970s, which was not verified.
- Do not describe the Thomas v. Perkins outcome as "the oil payment holder was taxed as the owner" without care. The holding is that the amounts were not gross income of the operator (assignee).
- Do not state typical interest rates, advance rates or tenors for historical production payment loans. Beyond the two Lake-case figures (3% and 3.5%/4%), none were verified.
