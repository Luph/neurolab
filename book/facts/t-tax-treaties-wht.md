# Tax treaties and withholding taxes for project structures: OECD and UN Models (Articles 5, 10–13), the MLI and the principal purpose test, offshore indirect transfers

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: OECD list of MLI signatories and parties, status as of 15 September 2026)

## Summary

A cross-border project usually pays dividends, shareholder-loan interest and fees from the host country to holding companies and lenders abroad. The host country's domestic withholding tax (WHT) on those payments can be cut by a double tax treaty, but only for a resident of the treaty partner that beneficially owns the income and passes the treaty's anti-abuse tests.

Two model conventions shape almost all treaties:

- **OECD Model (2017 version).** Caps source-country tax on dividends at 5% for a company holding at least 25% of the payer's capital for 365 days, and at 15% otherwise. Caps interest at 10%. Leaves royalties to the residence state alone.
- **UN Model (2021 version).** Uses the same structure but leaves every rate blank for bilateral negotiation. Lets the source state tax royalties and, through Article 12A, fees for technical services. Adds two capital-gains rules that matter to resource and infrastructure projects: Article 13(6), on alienation of state-granted rights to natural resources, and Article 13(7), on "offshore indirect transfers" of shares deriving more than half their value from such assets.

The BEPS **Multilateral Instrument (MLI)** amends existing treaties in bulk. Its Article 7(1) inserts a **principal purpose test (PPT)**: treaty benefits are denied if obtaining them was one of the principal purposes of an arrangement, unless granting them accords with the treaty's object and purpose. As of 15 September 2026 the OECD lists 107 numbered jurisdictions as signatories or parties. By this sheet's count of that list, about 91 had ratified. The United States has not signed.

Treaty shopping through conduit holding companies is therefore much harder than before 2017. Substance in the holding company, a genuine business rationale and beneficial ownership must be shown. The MLI's effect also depends on whether both treaty partners have ratified it and which options each chose.

## Verified facts

### Model conventions: rates and thresholds

1. **OECD Model, dividends (Article 10(2)).** Source-state tax on dividends is capped at:
   - 5% for "direct investment" dividends, where the beneficial owner is a company holding directly at least 25% of the payer's capital;
   - 15% for all other ("portfolio") dividends.

   As stated in the UN Model Commentary's description of the OECD Model. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. **365-day holding period (2017).** In 2017 both Models added a requirement that the 25% holding be held throughout a 365-day period that includes the payment date. Changes of ownership that result directly from a corporate reorganisation, such as a merger or divisive reorganisation, are disregarded. The change follows the BEPS Action 6 final report. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. **UN Model, dividends.** The UN Model 2021 Article 10(2) leaves both percentages blank ("to be established through bilateral negotiations"). It uses the same 25% / 365-day test for the direct-investment rate. Before the 2017 update the UN threshold was 10%. The Committee raised it to 25% in 2017 "in line with" the OECD Model. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. **Interest (Article 11(2)).** The OECD Model caps source-state tax on interest at 10% of the gross amount. The UN Model leaves the rate to bilateral negotiation. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. **Royalties (Article 12).** The OECD Model gives the residence state the exclusive right to tax royalties beneficially owned by its residents. The UN Model omits the word "only" and adds paragraph 2, letting the source state tax royalties at a negotiated rate. The UN Commentary notes that several OECD members have recorded reservations to exclusive residence taxation of royalties. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. **Fees for technical services (UN Article 12A).** The UN Model lets the source state tax "fees for technical services" (payments for managerial, technical or consultancy services) at a negotiated rate on the gross amount. This applies even without a permanent establishment. The OECD Model has no equivalent. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. **Construction permanent establishment (Article 5(3)).**

   | | OECD Model | UN Model |
   |---|---|---|
   | When a building site or construction or installation project is a PE | Lasts more than 12 months | Lasts more than six months |
   | What it covers | Building site, construction or installation project | Also assembly projects and "supervisory activities in connection therewith" |
   | Other | — | Services through personnel are a PE if present more than 183 days in any 12-month period (Article 5(3)(b)); the Commentary says six months could be cut to not less than three months in special cases |

   [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. **Land-rich shares (Article 13(4)).** UN Model Article 13(4) "corresponds to paragraph 4 of the OECD Model". The source state may tax gains on shares or comparable interests (including partnership or trust interests) that, at any time in the 365 days before alienation, derived more than 50% of their value directly or indirectly from immovable property situated there. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. **Substantial shareholdings (UN Article 13(5)).** The UN Model also allows source taxation of gains on shares of a resident company where the alienator held at least a negotiated percentage of capital at any time in the preceding 365 days. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

### UN Model: natural-resource rights and offshore indirect transfers

10. **Article 13(6): resource rights.** The source state may tax gains from alienating a right granted under its law that allows use of resources naturally present in, and under the jurisdiction of, that state. The Commentary's examples include:
    - fishing quotas;
    - rights to fell timber and to extract water;
    - rights to explore for oil, gas or minerals;
    - rights to install wind or tidal-stream turbines in part of the state's territory;
    - rights to use radio spectrum.

    Contractual rights between private parties are not covered. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. **Article 13(6) does not reach share sales.** Paragraph 6 applies only where the right itself is alienated, not an "indirect transfer" through the sale of shares in the company holding it. Such sales may instead fall within paragraphs 4, 5 or 7, or anti-abuse rules such as Article 29(9). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. **Article 13(7): offshore indirect transfers (OITs).** The source state may tax gains on shares or comparable interests in a local or offshore entity where both conditions hold:
    - (a) the alienator held at least a negotiated percentage of capital at any time in the 365 days before alienation;
    - (b) the interests derived more than 50% of their value, directly or indirectly, from property whose direct sale that state could have taxed under the other paragraphs of Article 13.

    The Commentary records that a "medium-sized minority" of Committee members opposed the paragraph. It notes collection difficulties when one non-resident sells to another. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. **When paragraphs 6 and 7 were added.** The UN Model's introduction says paragraphs 6 and 7 of Article 13 were added in the 2021 revision, together with Article 12B (income from automated digital services, adopted at the Committee's twenty-second session in April 2021). It cites the Platform for Collaboration on Tax's 2020 toolkit, *The Taxation of Offshore Indirect Transfers*, for the concerns behind them. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

### MLI and the principal purpose test

14. **PPT text.** MLI Article 7(1): a benefit under a Covered Tax Agreement "shall not be granted" for an item of income or capital if it is reasonable to conclude, having regard to all relevant facts and circumstances, that obtaining the benefit was one of the principal purposes of any arrangement or transaction that resulted directly or indirectly in it. The exception is where granting the benefit in the circumstances would accord with the object and purpose of the relevant provisions. The UN Model 2021 contains the same rule as Article 29(9). [confidence: high] [source: 2, 1]
15. **Minimum standard.** The MLI frames the PPT (alone or with a limitation-on-benefits clause) as the means of meeting "the minimum standard for preventing treaty abuse under the OECD/G20 BEPS package". [confidence: high] [source: 2]
16. **MLI Article 8 (dividend transfer transactions).** Reduced treaty rates for corporate shareholders apply only if the ownership conditions are met throughout a 365-day period that includes the payment date. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
17. **MLI Article 9 (land-rich entities).**
    - Article 9(1) applies the treaty's value threshold "at any time during the 365 days preceding the alienation" and extends it to comparable interests such as partnership or trust interests.
    - A party may opt for Article 9(4), the 50%-of-value test matching the OECD Model.
    - Parties may reserve against parts of Article 9.

    [confidence: high] [source: 2]
18. **Entry into force (Article 34).** The MLI enters into force for each ratifying signatory on the first day of the month after three calendar months from deposit of its instrument. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
19. **Entry into effect (Article 35(1)).** For a given treaty, the MLI has effect:
    - for withholding taxes, on amounts where the event giving rise to the tax occurs on or after 1 January of the next calendar year beginning on or after the latest of the dates on which the MLI entered into force for the treaty partners;
    - for other taxes, for taxable periods beginning on or after six months (or a shorter agreed period) after that latest date.

    Parties may adjust this by notification (Article 35(2)–(3)). [confidence: high] [source: 2]

### Beneficial ownership

21. **Conduits.** The OECD Commentary on Article 10, reproduced in the UN Commentary, says three kinds of recipient are not the beneficial owner:
    - an agent or nominee;
    - a conduit company acting as a fiduciary or administrator;
    - more generally, a recipient whose right to use and enjoy a dividend is constrained by a contractual or legal obligation to pass it on to another person. Such an obligation may be found in the legal documents or inferred from facts and circumstances.

    The OECD's conduit-companies report concludes that a conduit "cannot normally be regarded as the beneficial owner" if it has very narrow powers. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

23. **Indicative ranges.** On these US data:
    - treaty interest rates typically run 0–15%;
    - direct-investment dividend rates 5–15%;
    - portfolio dividend rates 10–25%;
    - against a 30% statutory default.

    These are one country's treaties as of May 2023. Developing host countries' treaties, which usually follow the UN Model, tend to keep higher source rates. Always check the specific treaty, its protocols and the MLI position of both states. [confidence: medium (range drawn from one table)] [source: 4, 1]

### Indirect transfers in practice: India and Vodafone (secondary source)

24. **Vodafone in India.**
    - Vodafone agreed in February 2007 to buy Hutchison's approximately 67% controlling stake in Hutchison Essar for approximately USD 11.1 billion, completed 8 May 2007, by acquiring an offshore holding company.
    - India's 2012 retrospective amendment taxing such indirect transfers led Vodafone to bring arbitration under the India–Netherlands bilateral investment treaty in 2014.
    - A Permanent Court of Arbitration tribunal ruled for Vodafone on 25 September 2020, finding a breach of fair and equitable treatment.
    - India's Taxation Laws (Amendment) Act, 2021 withdrew retrospective demands for indirect transfers made before May 2012, with refunds without interest conditional on withdrawal of litigation.

    [confidence: medium (encyclopedic secondary source; details should be checked against the award and the Act)] [source: 5]

## Financing and structure details

Not a transaction. Structural points that follow from the verified rules:

- **Holding-company location.** A sponsor holding project equity through a treaty jurisdiction can cut dividend WHT to 5% only if it holds at least 25% for 365 days (facts 1–2, 16). Every holding vehicle must also show beneficial ownership (fact 21) and survive the PPT (fact 14). A holding company with no staff or decision-making, that passes dividends straight on, is the classic conduit.
- **Shareholder loans.** Interest WHT relief under Article 11 (0–15% in the US table, fact 22) is one reason sponsors fund projects with shareholder loans. Interest deductibility is then limited separately (see t-interest-limitation), and pricing is tested under transfer pricing (see t-transfer-pricing).
- **Exit.** A sale of shares in an offshore holding company is not automatically outside the host country's tax net. Under treaties following UN Article 13(7), or domestic indirect-transfer rules, gains may be taxed where the value derives mainly from local assets (facts 10–13). Licence-holding renewable and resource projects are directly in view: the Commentary names wind and tidal-turbine rights, exploration rights and spectrum.
- **EPC contractors.** Under the UN Model six-month construction-PE threshold, a foreign contractor's site work can create a taxable presence sooner than under the OECD 12-month rule. UN Article 12A can also tax technical-services fees at source without a PE (facts 6–7).
- **MLI timing.** For withholding taxes the MLI bites from 1 January after both treaty partners' entry-into-force dates (fact 19). In a 2026 structure, check whether both states have ratified and which articles each adopted.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Why the UN Model differs.** The UN Commentary records developing-country members' view that the source country should have the exclusive, or at least primary, right to tax interest, because that is where the capital is put to use. Some developed-country members held that the investor's home country should tax, to promote capital mobility. [source: 1]
- **Why Article 13(7) exists.** The Commentary explains it: without it, gains from selling shares of a non-resident entity whose value lies in assets in the source state (other than immovable property) are taxable only in the residence state. Many developing countries consider they should be able to tax such OITs. The opposing minority stressed administrative and collection challenges. [source: 1]
- **Vodafone as a cautionary tale.** India's attempt to tax an offshore indirect transfer retrospectively ended in an adverse investment-treaty award and legislative withdrawal (fact 24). The lesson for structurers is that the rule should be prospective and treaty-based. For lenders and buyers, it shows that indirect-transfer tax is a live exit risk, and that bilateral investment treaties can protect against retrospective changes. [source: 5]

## Teaching angles by chapter

**Chapter 67 (Tax structuring).**

- Build Case P's holding chain hop by hop. At each payment, ask four questions: domestic WHT rate; treaty rate; does the recipient beneficially own the income and pass the PPT; does the MLI apply to this treaty yet.
- Use the OECD 5%/15%/10% figures and the IRS table to show indicative ranges, and stress that the specific treaty governs.
- Close with exit: Article 13(4) and UN 13(6)–(7) mean a share sale abroad may still be taxed in the host country.

**Chapter 54 (Governing law, disputes and investment protection).**

- Contrast double tax treaties, which allocate taxing rights, with bilateral investment treaties, which protect against unfair treatment.
- Vodafone v India (2020, under the India–Netherlands BIT) shows a tax measure being challenged as a breach of fair and equitable treatment. Teach that choosing the holding jurisdiction affects both tax and investment protection, and that the PPT asks whether a structure's principal purposes included obtaining treaty benefits.

**Chapter 63 (Refinancing, repricing and secondary sales).**

- In a partial sale of a project stake, the buyer and seller must model indirect-transfer tax exposure in the host country: land-rich and resource-rights rules, OIT clauses and domestic law. They also need to decide who bears it (price adjustment, tax indemnity, withholding by the buyer where the law requires).
- The 365-day tests in MLI Articles 8–9 and Article 13(4) matter when stakes are restructured shortly before a sale or dividend.

## Do not state

- That the OECD Model's 5%/15%/10% rates are "the" treaty rates anywhere. They are model caps; actual treaties vary (see fact 22).
- A precise current count of MLI parties without the date and source. This sheet counts about 91 from the 15 September 2026 list. The OECD's own headline counts may define "covered jurisdictions" differently.
- That the MLI's PPT applies to every treaty of a signatory. It applies only to Covered Tax Agreements that both parties listed, from the Article 35 effective dates, and subject to reservations.
- Any content of a 2025 or later update to the OECD Model or UN Model (for example new Commentary on remote work or further UN articles). Not verified here.
- The exact wording or rate of UN Article 12B (automated digital services). It is in the 2021 Model but was not checked line by line here.
- The Indian Supreme Court's 2012 Vodafone judgment date and holding, and exact sums under the 2021 Act. These come from a secondary source; check the judgment and the Act.
- Any statement on how specific courts have applied beneficial ownership (for example the UK Indofood case or the CJEU Danish cases of 2019). The primary texts were not retrieved.
- The recommendations of the Platform for Collaboration on Tax's 2020 toolkit on offshore indirect transfers. The toolkit itself was not retrieved; only the UN Model's citation of it.
