# DFI and multilateral landscape; MIGA; preferred creditor status; A/B loans

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest data point covered: MIGA's website figure for guarantees issued in FY26, the fiscal year ended 30 June 2026)

## Summary

Development finance institutions (DFIs) lend, invest and guarantee with a development mandate. They fall into two groups:
- **Multilateral DFIs and MDBs,** owned by many governments. Examples are the World Bank Group, EBRD, EIB, ADB, AfDB, IDB Invest and the Islamic Development Bank.
- **Bilateral DFIs,** owned by one government. Examples are the US DFC, the Dutch FMO, Germany's DEG, France's Proparco and the UK's BII. The European association EDFI groups 15 bilateral DFIs.

In project finance their value lies less in their money than in three things they carry with them:
- **preferred creditor status (PCS),** a market and official-sector convention under which MDB loans are serviced in priority during a country's foreign-exchange crisis and are excluded from general sovereign reschedulings;
- **the A/B loan structure,** which lets commercial banks share that status by participating in a loan of record held by the MDB;
- **guarantees,** above all those of MIGA, the World Bank Group's political-risk and credit-enhancement arm.

MIGA was founded on 12 April 1988 and has 182 members. Since 2024 it has housed the World Bank Group Guarantee Platform, which brings together World Bank, IFC and MIGA guarantees. The platform's goal is to raise annual group guarantee issuance to USD 20 billion by 2030. In FY2024 the Group issued USD 10.3 billion of guarantees.

IFC created the B loan in 1959. Its syndication program dates from 1957 and had mobilised over USD 97 billion for more than 1,630 projects by FY2021. IFC states that no IFC loan, including the portions held by B-loan participants, has been included in a general country debt rescheduling.

## Verified facts

**The landscape**
1. The World Bank Group has five institutions: IBRD (loans to middle-income and creditworthy low-income governments), IDA (interest-free loans and grants to the poorest countries' governments), IFC (private sector), MIGA (guarantees of foreign direct investment against non-commercial risks) and ICSID (conciliation and arbitration of investment disputes). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. IFC was founded in 1956. As of FY2021 it was owned by 185 countries and rated triple-A, and reported more than USD 321 billion invested since its founding. In FY2021 it committed USD 23.3 billion in long-term investments, including mobilisation, and had a committed portfolio of USD 64.1 billion. [confidence: high, as of FY2021] [source: 1]
3. EDFI, the association of European bilateral DFIs, describes itself as promoting the joint interests of 15 bilateral DFIs. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
4. The US International Development Finance Corporation (DFC) was established in 2019 and was "granted significant expanded authorities in 2025". It reports more than USD 40 billion in its global investment portfolio and active investments in more than 100 countries. [confidence: high for establishment and portfolio statement; medium for the scope of the 2025 changes, which were not reviewed] [source: 3]
5. The CRR (EU Regulation 575/2013) as enacted in 2013 assigned a 0% risk weight to exposures to listed MDBs, including:
   - IBRD and IFC;
   - the Inter-American Development Bank, ADB and AfDB;
   - EBRD and EIB;
   - MIGA and the Islamic Development Bank.

   Other MDBs are treated like institutions. The list has since been amended, so check the current CRR text. [confidence: high for the 2013 text] [source: 4]

**A/B loans, parallel loans, PCS**
6. IFC's syndicated lending program, established in 1957, is described by IFC as the oldest and largest among MDBs. As of FY2021 (to 30 June 2021) IFC reported:
   - over USD 97 billion mobilised for over 1,630 projects;
   - a syndicated loan portfolio under management of USD 15.6 billion;
   - USD 26 billion syndicated in the previous five years. [confidence: high, as of FY2021] [source: 1]
7. IFC created the B loan structure in 1959. IFC says B-loan participants benefit from the same preferred creditor treatment as IFC's own loans. It says this treatment is recognised by regulators, rating agencies (including under Basel II and III) and private insurers. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
8. **B loan mechanics.** There is one loan agreement with the borrower, covering the A loan (IFC's own account) and the B loan, and IFC is lender of record and administers the whole loan. Participants enter a participation agreement with IFC and share project risk fully with IFC. The participation structure lets participants benefit from IFC's privileges and immunities. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. **PCS features of B loans,** as listed by IFC:
   - preferred access to foreign exchange in a country FX shortage;
   - exclusion from general country debt reschedulings;
   - no mandatory new-money obligations in such reschedulings.

   IFC also states four further points:
   - recognition has been consistent in Pakistan, Russia and Argentina;
   - bank regulators exempt B loans from mandatory country-risk provisioning;
   - rated transactions can pierce the sovereign ceiling;
   - PCS is a recognised country-risk mitigant under Basel II. [confidence: high as IFC's own statements] [source: 1]
10. **Basel II capital treatment of B loans.** IFC states that under the standardised approach banks may apply the borrower's local-currency rather than foreign-currency rating. Under advanced IRB they may reflect the country-risk mitigation through lower country-risk weighting. IFC cites footnotes 20 and 31 of the June 2004 Basel II framework. [confidence: high as IFC's statement] [source: 1]
11. **B-loan participant voting thresholds** (share of total B loan, per IFC's 2022 brochure):

    | Threshold | Decisions |
    |---|---|
    | 100% | Changes to money terms; waivers or amendments of conditions precedent |
    | 67% | Acceleration after payment default; release of security; changes to guarantees or support arrangements |
    | 51% | Change-of-control provisions; financial covenants |
    | Consultation only | Non-financial covenants |

    IFC shares with participants all information it receives from borrowers. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. **B-loan participant eligibility.** An eligible participant must not be incorporated or resident in the borrower's or project's country, and must not be an export credit, governmental or multilateral agency. It should have an international investment-grade rating, though non-investment-grade or unrated institutions may be considered case by case. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. **IFC syndication products by investor type:**
    - B loans: commercial banks, voting rights, tenor generally shorter than the A loan;
    - parallel loans: DFIs and sovereign entities, separate loans under a common terms agreement, tenor generally matching the A loan;
    - credit insurance: insurers, unfunded risk participation;
    - MCPP (Managed Co-Lending Portfolio Program): institutional investors, passive portfolio participation with full delegation to IFC after mandate. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. **IFC syndication volumes FY2019-FY2021:**

    | Fiscal year | B loans | Parallel loans | MCPP |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | FY2019 | USD 1,699m (22 deals) | USD 3,472m (29) | USD 566m (16) |
    | FY2020 | USD 1,062m (19) | USD 2,892m (32) | USD 1,036m (29) |
    | FY2021 | USD 1,227m (29) | USD 1,941m (28) | USD 480m (15) |

    Average final maturities were 5-7 years for B loans and 8-11 years for parallel loans. Average margins ranged from 319 to 399 bp across the two loan types. These are indicative of IFC's emerging-market private-sector book, FY2019-21. Drivers are country risk, sector, tenor and the global rate and spread cycle. [confidence: high as reported] [source: 1]
15. B-loan participants may also buy political risk insurance on their B loans, and IFC B loans may benefit from MIGA war and civil disturbance cover. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

**MIGA and the World Bank Group Guarantee Platform**
16. MIGA was established by an international convention on 12 April 1988 as a legally separate and financially independent member of the World Bank Group, with initial capital stock of USD 1 billion and 29 original members. The World Bank Board of Governors endorsed the MIGA Convention in September 1985. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
17. MIGA now has 182 members: 154 developing and 28 industrialised countries. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
18. MIGA's history page records issuance figures for recent fiscal years (July-June):
    - FY2021: USD 5.2 billion across 40 projects;
    - FY2022: USD 4.9 billion across a record 54 projects;
    - FY2023: a record USD 6.4 billion across 40 projects in 29 countries, including its largest single guarantee to date, USD 1.85 billion for climate-related financing in Mexico;
    - FY2024: the World Bank Group issued USD 10.3 billion in guarantees, of which USD 8.2 billion came from MIGA, USD 1.4 billion from IFC and USD 0.7 billion from the World Bank. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
19. The World Bank Group Guarantee Platform brings World Bank and IFC guarantee products into MIGA, with the goal of raising annual guarantee issuance to USD 20 billion by 2030. MIGA says it has housed the platform since 2024 and that the platform provides guarantees from the balance sheets of MIGA, IFC and the World Bank. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7, 8]
20. MIGA's website headline states "$25 billion in guarantees issued in FY26". [confidence: medium; the page does not say whether the figure is MIGA alone or platform-wide] [source: 7]
21. **MIGA political-risk products:**
    - currency inconvertibility and transfer restriction (depreciation not covered);
    - expropriation, including creeping and partial;
    - war and civil disturbance;
    - breach of contract;
    - non-honoring of sovereign financial obligations and of SOE financial obligations.

    It also offers credit guarantees (partial/full credit guarantees for loans and bonds, policy-based guarantees, project-based loan and payment guarantees), "capital optimization" products, and trade finance guarantees. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]
22. **Breach of contract** covers a government's breach or repudiation of a contract with an investor, such as a concession or PPA, and in some cases SOE obligations. The investor must first invoke the contract's dispute mechanism. MIGA pays in two situations:
    - **denial of recourse:** government interference prevents an award;
    - **non-payment of an award.**

    MIGA may make a provisional payment pending the dispute. Compensation is capped at the guaranteed amount. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
23. **Non-honoring cover** pays when a sovereign or SOE fails to pay under an unconditional and irrevocable financial payment obligation or guarantee. No arbitral award is required. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
24. **Transfer restriction cover** compensates in the hard currency (dollar, euro or yen) specified in the contract of guarantee. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
25. **Tenor.** MIGA issues guarantees for up to 15 years, occasionally 20 years, with a minimum of three years. Loan cover usually matches the loan tenor. Investors may cancel after the first three years. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
26. **Paris alignment.** MIGA committed to align 85% of new projects with the Paris Agreement from 1 July 2023 and 100% from 1 July 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

**Illustrative precedent**
27. Pakistan's Hub Power Project used the World Bank's first partial risk guarantee: USD 240 million, alongside a parallel USD 120 million JEXIM guarantee, covering certain political risks for commercial lenders. It also drew on CDC (now BII) loans and equity. See facts/hub-power.md. [confidence: high] [source: 12]

## Financing and structure details

A typical DFI-anchored emerging-market project financing combines several layers:
- **IFC (or another MDB) A loan:** long tenor, for own account.
- **B loan:** commercial banks participating through IFC as lender of record. Usually shorter tenor, priced to the market, and covered by PCS and treated favourably for country-risk provisioning.
- **Parallel loans:** other DFIs (bilateral DFIs, EIB and others) lend directly under a common terms agreement. They do not share the A lender's privileges contractually but sit pari passu.
- **MCPP or insurer participations:** institutional money on IFC's tenor, through unfunded risk participation or credit insurance.
- **MIGA or World Bank guarantees:** protect against political and sovereign-contract risk, such as an off-taker's breach of a PPA or a transfer restriction. For banks, a MIGA guarantee can let the covered portion take MIGA's risk weight where regulation allows.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- IFC's own position is that PCS has been consistently honoured, in Pakistan, Russia and Argentina, and that no IFC loan, B-loan portions included, has been included in a general country debt rescheduling (source 1). This is the main reason commercial banks accept B-loan pricing and documentation.
- PCS is a convention, not a treaty right of the lender. Its strength rests on sovereigns' need for continued MDB access and on official-creditor practice. Present it as an empirically robust practice, not a legal guarantee.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 29 (The lenders):**
  - Map the lender universe: MDBs, bilateral DFIs, ECAs, banks and institutions.
  - Explain DFI additionality and mandate, and how DFIs change the price and availability of everyone else's money.
  - Teach the A/B mechanics: lender of record, participation agreement, voting thresholds, eligibility.
  - Use Case P to show how adding a B loan widens the bank market for a frontier-country project.
- **Ch 34 (Blended, concessional and local-currency finance):**
  - Position World Bank and MIGA guarantees as the bridge between public balance sheets and private lenders.
  - Use the Guarantee Platform's USD 20 billion-by-2030 goal and the FY2024 split (USD 10.3bn total) to show scale and direction of travel.
  - Show how parallel loans with DFIs and concessional windows sit beside commercial B loans in a common terms agreement.
- **Ch 60 (Political risk and its protection):**
  - Walk through MIGA's covers and how each pays: breach of contract requires the dispute process (denial of recourse or non-payment of award), while non-honoring does not.
  - Teach the 15-to-20-year tenor limits and the three-year minimum.
  - Explain the "multilateral halo", deterrence through World Bank Group relationships with the host state, using Hub Power as the archetype of World Bank partial risk guarantees.

## Do not state

- Do not state current (FY2025 or FY2026) IFC syndication totals or B-loan pricing. Verified figures are FY2019-FY2021 only.
- Do not attribute the FY26 USD 25bn figure to MIGA alone or to the whole platform without checking MIGA's FY26 annual report.
- Do not state the scope of the DFC's 2025 expanded authorities, such as its exposure cap or equity powers. It was not verified.
- Do not state that PCS is a legal right enforceable against sovereigns, or that MDB loans are never restructured in any form. IFC's claim concerns general country debt reschedulings.
- Do not give the date CDC was renamed British International Investment, or BII's current size. They were not verified here.
- Do not quote rating-agency criteria on PCS. They were not reviewed.
- Do not treat the 2013 CRR MDB list as the current list.
