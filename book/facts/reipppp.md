# South Africa's Renewable Energy Independent Power Producer Procurement Programme (REIPPPP)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: 23 December 2024, announcement of Bid Window 7 preferred bidders)

## Summary

REIPPPP is South Africa's competitive tender programme for grid-connected renewable power from independent power producers (IPPs). It replaced a 2009 feed-in tariff (REFIT) scheme that never produced a signed contract: after a legal opinion commissioned by National Treasury concluded that feed-in tariffs amounted to non-competitive procurement, the Department of Energy (DOE) launched REIPPPP with a Request for Proposals in August 2011. The programme was run by a dedicated DOE IPP Office set up with National Treasury's PPP Unit and the Development Bank of Southern Africa, staffed largely by external advisors. Its design features have become a reference model for emerging-market renewables tenders: a single-stage tender with strict qualification thresholds (land rights, environmental consents, grid cost letters, measured resource data, and bank letters confirming committed financing); bid bonds of ZAR 100,000 per MW, doubled at preferred-bidder stage; evaluation on price (70 points) and economic development (30 points) in the early windows; and a set of standardized, non-negotiable contracts released with the RFP: a 20-year rand-denominated, CPI-indexed Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) with Eskom as buyer, an Implementation Agreement (IA) with the DOE that in effect gave a sovereign guarantee of Eskom's payments, and Direct Agreements giving lenders step-in rights. Over the first four bid windows (2011–2015) it awarded 92 projects totaling about 6,300 MW from more than 300 bids, raised about ZAR 193 billion of funding (about 65 percent project-finance debt, mostly from the five big South African banks), and drove average solar PV tariffs down by about two-thirds between Bid Windows 1 and 3. The programme then stalled for several years before resuming with Bid Window 5 (2021); Bid Windows 6 (2022) and 7 (2024) were constrained by lack of grid capacity, awarding only solar PV and far less than the capacity offered.

## Verified facts

1. In 2009 South Africa explored renewable feed-in tariffs; NERSA approved a REFIT policy in 2009 with tariffs designed to give a 17 percent real after-tax return on equity. A March 2011 NERSA consultation paper proposed sharply lower tariffs, and a legal opinion commissioned by the DOE and National Treasury concluded that feed-in tariffs amounted to non-competitive procurement. Not a single megawatt had been signed under REFIT when it was abandoned. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. In August 2011 the DOE issued the first REIPPPP Request for Proposals; a compulsory bidders' conference drew over 300 organizations. NERSA subsequently terminated the REFITs. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. The programme's capacity is set by ministerial determinations under the Integrated Resource Plan (IRP 2010–2030, which planned 17.8 GW of solar and wind by 2030); NERSA licenses new capacity within those determinations. Eskom is the country's "single buyer" and PPA counterparty. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The DOE IPP Office was established by the DOE with National Treasury's PPP Unit as a dedicated project team with arm's-length autonomy from normal government procedures. The DBSA provided ZAR 80 million for transaction advisors and capacity building, and National Treasury allocated ZAR 100 million; over 100 advisors from 13 firms were engaged. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
5. The RFP contained general requirements, qualification criteria and evaluation criteria, and included a standard PPA, IA and Direct Agreement. The PPA was signed by the IPP and Eskom, denominated in rand, with a 20-year term from commercial operation date. The IA, signed by the IPP and the DOE, effectively provided a sovereign guarantee of payment by requiring the DOE to make good Eskom payment defaults, and also imposed the IPP's economic development obligations. The Direct Agreements gave lenders step-in rights. All three were non-negotiable. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
6. The sovereign guarantees in the IAs were approved by National Treasury's Fiscal Liability Committee; no reserve or contingent liability fund was set aside per transaction, and the guarantees were provided even though Eskom then had an investment-grade rating. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. Bids were due within three months of the RFP, and financial close was to follow within six months of preferred-bidder announcement. Bidders had to submit bank letters showing committed financing, which Eberhard, Kolker and Leigland describe as outsourcing due diligence to the banks and addressing the "low-balling" problem of auctions. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. Bidders paid a registration fee of ZAR 15,000 and posted bid guarantees of ZAR 100,000 per MW of nameplate capacity, doubled (ZAR 200,000 per MW) on appointment as preferred bidder. Winning project companies pay a development fee of 1 percent of total project cost. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
9. Qualification included proof of land rights, environmental authorizations (costing about ZAR 2–6 million and up to 24 months per project), a grid cost estimate letter, at least 12 months (365 consecutive days) of on-site wind data for wind projects, financial capability tests for finance providers, and minimum ownership thresholds: at least 40 percent South African participation, at least 12 percent ownership by black South Africans and at least 2.5 percent by local communities. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
10. In Bid Windows 1–4, bids that passed qualification were scored 70 points on price and 30 points on economic development (jobs, local content, ownership, management control, preferential procurement, enterprise development and socio-economic development). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
11. Bid Window 1 caps were set close to the 2009 REFIT levels: ZAR 1.15/kWh for wind and ZAR 2.85/kWh for PV and CSP. Round 1 average bid prices were ZAR 1.14/kWh for wind, ZAR 2.76/kWh for PV and ZAR 2.69/kWh for CSP, i.e. close to the caps, which Eberhard and colleagues attribute to limited competition in the first round. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. Bid Window 1: 53 bids for 2,128 MW received by November 2011; 28 preferred bidders for 1,416 MW (investment close to USD 6 billion). Main agreements were signed on 5 November 2012, all closing together; the first project came online in November 2013. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. Bid Window 2: 79 bids for 3,233 MW received in March 2012; 19 projects selected (about 1,044 MW), with agreements signed in May 2013. Bid Window 3: 93 bids for 6,023 MW received in August 2013; 17 preferred bidders for 1,456 MW announced in October 2013. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. Over the first three windows, average nominal solar PV tariffs fell by 68 percent and wind tariffs by 42 percent. Eberhard and Naude report solar PV prices fell by about two-thirds from BW1 to BW3 and by 25 percent from BW3 to BW4. In BW4(a) the average wind price was ZAR 0.62/kWh (lowest ZAR 0.56) and the average PV price ZAR 0.79/kWh (lowest ZAR 0.77), which the authors compare with Eskom's average cost of supply of ZAR 0.76/kWh for 2015/16 and describe as about half the cost of power from new Eskom power stations. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
15. Across the first four bid windows (including BW4 and expedited rounds), the programme procured 6,328 MW from 92 projects selected from over 300 bids. Total funding was about ZAR 193 billion: external project-finance debt ZAR 125.6 billion (65 percent), equity 23 percent and corporate finance 12 percent; 79 projects used project finance and 13 corporate finance. Typical gearing was 70–80 percent debt. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
16. Local banks provided about 68 percent of external debt, led by Absa, Nedbank, RMB, Standard Bank and Investec; the DBSA and Industrial Development Corporation provided about 13 percent. Foreign shareholding of up to 60 percent was permitted; by the IPP Office's 2015 data, foreign equity in BW1–4 was ZAR 35.2 billion, about 56 percent of equity. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
17. The programme stalled between about 2015 and 2019 before new windows resumed; Eberhard and Naude's 2016 review does not describe the cause, but other reporting attributes it to Eskom's refusal to sign PPAs with the BW4 preferred bidders. [confidence: medium] [source: 4]
18. Bid Window 5 preferred bidders (25 projects, about 2,583 MW) were announced in October 2021. As at December 2022, 19 BW5 projects (1,759 MW: 975 MW solar PV, 784 MW wind) had signed, with R34.3 billion of investment and a weighted average solar PV tariff of ZAR 454.23/MWh. [confidence: high for December 2022 figures; medium for October 2021 totals] [source: 4, 5]
19. Bid Window 6 (December 2022) attracted 4,116 MW of wind bids, but no wind project was awarded because of grid constraints in the Western, Eastern and Northern Cape; five solar PV preferred bidders were appointed for 860 MW at a weighted average tariff of ZAR 490.48/MWh, 8 percent above BW5 solar, the first increase in the programme's history, with R12.1 billion of investment. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
20. Bid Window 7 was released on 14 December 2023 for up to 5,000 MW (1,800 MW solar PV, 3,200 MW onshore wind); 48 bids totaling over 10.2 GW were received on 15 August 2024. On 23 December 2024 the Minister of Electricity and Energy announced eight solar PV preferred bidders totaling 1,760 MW, at prices of ZAR 420.74–492.20/MWh, with R31.4 billion investment. Bids were scored 90 points on price and 10 on economic development. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

## Timeline

- 2009: NERSA approves REFIT; no contracts signed.
- March 2011: NERSA consultation on lower feed-in tariffs; legal opinion that FITs are non-competitive procurement.
- August 2011: REIPPPP BW1 RFP issued.
- November 2011: BW1 bids (53 bids, 2,128 MW); BW2 announced.
- 5 November 2012: BW1 agreements signed (28 projects, 1,416 MW).
- March 2012 / May 2013: BW2 bids received / BW2 agreements signed (19 projects).
- August / October 2013: BW3 bids / 17 preferred bidders (1,456 MW).
- November 2013: First REIPPPP project online.
- 2014–2015: BW3.5 (CSP) and BW4 (two batches, BW4(a) and BW4(b)) procured.
- About 2015–2019: Programme stalled.
- October 2021: BW5 preferred bidders announced.
- 8 December 2022: BW6 results (860 MW solar only; wind blocked by grid).
- 14 December 2023: BW7 released.
- 23 December 2024: BW7 preferred bidders (1,760 MW solar PV).

## Financing and structure details

- Contract set (standard, non-negotiable): PPA (IPP–Eskom, 20 years from COD, rand-denominated, CPI-indexed); Implementation Agreement (IPP–DOE, sovereign backing of Eskom payment obligations, economic development obligations); Direct Agreements (lenders' step-in). [source: 1, 2]
- Credit support: sovereign guarantee through the IA, approved by the Fiscal Liability Committee; no political risk insurance needed given South Africa's credit standing in 2011–2014. [source: 1]
- Bid security: ZAR 100,000/MW at bid; ZAR 200,000/MW at preferred bidder; released at COD or on failure at evaluation. [source: 1]
- Development fee: 1 percent of total project cost. [source: 1, 2]
- Funding of BW1–4: about ZAR 193 billion; 65 percent external debt, 23 percent equity, 12 percent corporate finance; local commercial banks about 68 percent of debt; DFIs (DBSA, IDC) about 13 percent. [source: 2]
- Simultaneous closing: BW1 closed all projects on one day, standardizing terms and limiting foreign-exchange risk, but stretching banks and advisors. [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Right: competitive tenders beat feed-in tariffs on price. Eberhard and Naude (2016) conclude that competitive tenders produce better price outcomes than unsolicited negotiations or feed-in tariffs, provided they include standardized PPAs, credit enhancement and security mechanisms ensuring bidders can deliver at the bid price. [source: 2]
- Right: process design. Eberhard, Kolker and Leigland (PPIAF, 2014) credit a dedicated, well-advised IPP Office, credible deadlines, non-negotiable documents, standardized financial data, and fully underwritten bids that removed incentives to low-ball and renegotiate. [source: 1]
- Right: economic development criteria built political support, though the same authors describe them as controversial, confusing and costly for bidders. [source: 1]
- Wrong or at risk: the programme depended on an ad hoc IPP Office and on Eskom's willingness and ability to contract; the 2015–2019 stall and the grid constraints of BW6–7 show off-taker and transmission capacity became the binding constraints. The PPIAF authors warned that a deteriorating Eskom could lead to calls on the sovereign guarantee. [source: 1, 6]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 47 (Bidding, acquisitions and infrastructure funds): Use REIPPPP's rounds to show how bid prices fall as competition rises (BW1 bids at the caps, then about two-thirds lower for PV by BW3) and how bid bonds, committed-finance letters and fixed documents control the winner's curse. Discuss how bidders price a non-negotiable PPA and why the secondary market of pension funds and insurers emerged after BW1.
- Ch 57 (The public-sector case for PPPs): A procurement-program design case: dedicated PPP unit, standardized documents, competitive tender replacing an administratively set tariff, sovereign guarantee as a contingent liability approved by a fiscal committee, and economic development objectives scored at 30 percent (later 10 percent). Use the stall and grid constraints to show that program success depends on the off-taker and transmission planning, not only procurement design.
- Ch 70 (Onshore wind and solar): The canonical example of how a standardized, bankable PPA, sovereign credit support and qualification rules (measured wind data, grid letters, environmental consents) let lenders finance many projects quickly at 70–80 percent gearing. Show the tariff path (BW1 ZAR 2.76/kWh PV to about ZAR 0.42–0.49/kWh in BW7, nominal) and the reversal in BW6 when grid scarcity, not technology cost, set the price.

## Do not state

- That Eskom signed the delayed BW4 PPAs on a specific date with a specific number of projects and investment total (commonly reported as April 2018, 27 projects, about ZAR 56 billion); not verified in this research.
- That the stall was caused solely by Eskom; the cause is reported but contested and not covered by the academic sources used here.
- The 30 GW / 300,000 jobs IRP figures from Wikipedia; not verified against the IRP.
- Programme-wide totals "as of 2026" (for example 9,910 MW across 127 projects) taken from Wikipedia; not verified against an official IPP Office report.
- Exact BW4 preferred-bidder announcement dates; not verified.
- That the IA is a "guarantee" in legal form; sources say it "effectively" provided a sovereign guarantee of Eskom's payment obligations.
- Present-day institutional names without dating them: the DOE became part of the Department of Mineral Resources and Energy (2019) and electricity matters later moved to the Ministry of Electricity and Energy (by 2024); confirm the current department before stating.
- BW5 weighted average tariff for all technologies (Wikipedia gives R473/MWh); only the solar PV figure (R454.23/MWh) is verified.
