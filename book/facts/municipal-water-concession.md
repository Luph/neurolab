# Metro Manila water concessions (1997): Manila Water (East Zone) and Maynilad (West Zone)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest data covered: PSE Edge market data for Maynilad Water Services, Inc., 2 October 2026; Maynilad listed 7 November 2025)

Cross-references: facts/argentina-2002.md covers the macro collapse that also hit Buenos Aires utility concessions. Aguas Argentinas was not researched for this sheet.

## Summary

In 1997 the Philippine government privatised the operations of the Metropolitan Waterworks and Sewerage System (MWSS), then one of Asia's least efficient water utilities. With IFC as lead adviser, it split Metro Manila into two zones and let two parallel 25-year concessions, awarded on the lowest average tariff bid. No bidder could win both. The East Zone went to Manila Water Company (Ayala Corporation with International Water, UK–US). The West Zone went to Maynilad Water Services (Benpres Corporation with Lyonnaise des Eaux). Tariffs initially fell 74% in the east and 43% in the west. The concessionaires took over in August 1997, just as the Asian financial crisis began. The two zones then diverged sharply, which makes this a natural experiment in currency risk. Manila Water was profitable from 1999, cut non-revenue water (NRW) from 63% to about 30% by 2006, and listed in 2005. Maynilad had about 90% of its concession fees denominated in foreign currency, plus large FX borrowings. The peso devaluation of mid-1997 to early 2001 broke its finances. It stopped paying concession fees and major loans in March 2001, served a termination notice in February 2003 and sought bankruptcy protection in November 2003. A court-approved Debt and Capital Restructuring Agreement (DCRA) in 2005 converted unpaid fees into an 84% MWSS stake. MWSS drew the full USD 120 million performance bond after a Supreme Court decision, and the government then sold the stake to new owners. Tariffs carried explicit FX pass-through charges (CERA and FCDA). In December 2021 Congress granted both companies 25-year legislative franchises, conditional on listing at least 30% of their shares within five years. Maynilad listed on the Philippine Stock Exchange on 7 November 2025.

## Verified facts

1. Background: in 1995 three-quarters of homes in the eastern half of Manila lacked 24-hour service and only 8% had a sewer connection. Almost two-thirds of water produced was lost. The 1995 Water Crisis Act set the framework for privatisation. In late 1995 the government hired IFC as lead transaction adviser. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Structure: two geographically separate, vertically integrated 25-year concessions for water and sewerage, with the East Zone holding about 40% of the population and the West about 60%. No single bidder could win both. A Regulatory Office was created within MWSS to monitor and enforce the agreements. The operators took full commercial and investment risk. Targets included 24-hour supply within five years, national water standards, universal water coverage within 10 years, and 83% sewerage and sanitation coverage within 25 years. The concessionaires had rights to raw water from the Angat dam but were responsible for future bulk supply. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Award: four prequalified consortia, each with a Filipino company and an international operator, bid. The concessions went to the lowest average tariff bids. Winners: Manila Water (Ayala Corporation and International Water, UK-USA) for the East Zone, and Maynilad (Benpres Corporation and Lyonnaise des Eaux, France) for the West Zone. Concessions were awarded in January 1997, and operations began in August 1997. Rates initially fell 74% in the east and 43% in the west. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
4. Manila Water's performance:
   - NRW fell from 63% (1997) to 29.9% (2006);
   - 24-hour availability rose from 26% of customers (1997) to 98% (2007);
   - households served rose from 325,000 (1997) to 938,000 (Q1 2007);
   - profitable since 1999;
   - IPO on the Philippine Stock Exchange in 2005, raising about USD 96 million;
   - PHP 17.5 billion (about USD 338 million) invested from 1997 to 31 December 2005.

   In 2007 Ayala held about 30.4%, United Utilities 11.8%, Mitsubishi 7.9% and IFC 7.4%, with the public holding about 37.9%. IFC invested USD 75 million. Manila Water's 2006 return on equity was about 22% (World Bank). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
5. Tariff design (East Zone, effective 7 October 2006): a Basic Charge plus the Currency Exchange Rate Adjustment (CERA), a fixed PHP 1.00/m³ surcharge, plus the Foreign Currency Differential Adjustment (FCDA), which recovers FX losses on MWSS and Manila Water loans used for capex and concession fees (then 1.10% of Basic). Sewer-connected customers paid a 50% surcharge. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
6. Maynilad's collapse (World Bank, 2006):
   - the large peso devaluation between mid-1997 and early 2001 "had the most significant impact" on its difficulties. With 90% of its concession fees in foreign currency and large FX loans for capital works, it could not cover concession fees and debt service from operating revenues;
   - it suspended concession fees and major loan repayments in March 2001, stated an intention to terminate in December 2002, served a termination notice in February 2003, and petitioned for bankruptcy protection in November 2003;
   - NRW rose from 61% (1998) to 69% (2004);
   - it never made a profit up to 2006. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. Restructuring:
   - in March 2004 the company, sponsors, lenders and MWSS agreed a restructuring plan. MWSS withdrew in June 2004 after a Supreme Court decision allowed it to draw the full USD 120 million performance bond rather than the USD 50 million in the earlier deal;
   - a revised DCRA was agreed in April 2005 and approved in June 2005 by the Quezon City Regional Trial Court acting as rehabilitation court. The DCRA was to run from June 2005 to 2013, by which time all existing obligations were to be repaid;
   - part of the unpaid fees converted into an 84% MWSS equity stake in Maynilad, which the Department of Finance was selling (pre-qualification complete by November 2006);
   - the performance bond was paid in early 2005. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
8. MWSS's own finances: all its revenue came from concession fees, so Maynilad's non-payment forced MWSS into very expensive borrowing. The World Bank prepared a project to support MWSS's financial rehabilitation, repaid through a ring-fenced "Special Project" tariff adjustment under DCRA clause 18.8. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
9. Franchises (December 2021):
   - Republic Act No. 11600 (Maynilad, West Zone and Cavite) and Republic Act No. 11601 (Manila Water, East Zone and Rizal) were both approved on 10 December 2021;
   - each grants a 25-year franchise, operating under a concession from MWSS;
   - each presumes Regulatory Office tariffs approved by the MWSS Board to be "valid and reasonable" unless set aside;
   - each requires the grantee to offer at least 30% of its outstanding capital stock to Filipino citizens on a Philippine exchange within five years, failing which the franchise is revoked "ipso facto". [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
10. Maynilad listed on the Philippine Stock Exchange (ticker MYNLD) on 7 November 2025. PSE data at 2 October 2026: 7,617,997,300 listed shares, a 28.97% free float, a 40% foreign ownership limit and a market capitalisation of about PHP 122.1 billion. PSE's company description, citing the 2025 SEC Form 17-A, says Maynilad was incorporated on 22 January 1997. It also says it received its 25-year franchise on 22 January 2022 and at 31 December 2025 served 1,563,344 billed connections covering 94.8% of the West Zone's population. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

## Timeline

- 1995: Water Crisis Act; IFC hired as adviser.
- January 1997: concessions awarded. 22 January 1997: Maynilad incorporated.
- August 1997: operations begin; the Asian financial crisis starts.
- 1999: Manila Water first profitable year.
- March 2001: Maynilad suspends concession fees and major loan payments.
- February 2003: Maynilad termination notice.
- November 2003: bankruptcy petition.
- March 2004: first restructuring plan; MWSS withdraws in June 2004 after the Supreme Court bond ruling.
- Early 2005: USD 120 million performance bond paid to MWSS.
- April–June 2005: DCRA agreed and approved by the rehabilitation court.
- 2005: Manila Water IPO (about USD 96 million).
- November 2006: Department of Finance completes pre-qualification for the sale of the MWSS stake in Maynilad.
- 10 December 2021: RA 11600 and RA 11601 approved.
- 7 November 2025: Maynilad listed on the PSE.

## Financing and structure details

- Concession fees: MWSS's entire revenue came from concession fees, and about 90% of Maynilad's concession fees were denominated in foreign currency (reflecting MWSS's foreign-currency obligations; the exact fee formula was not read). [source: 3]
- FX pass-through: CERA (fixed) and FCDA (formula-based) let operators recover currency losses through tariffs, subject to the Regulatory Office. [source: 2]
- Security package: Maynilad's USD 120 million performance bond was the main credit support available to MWSS on default. The Supreme Court allowed it to be drawn in full. [source: 3]
- Development finance: IFC was adviser, then a lender and shareholder in Manila Water (USD 75 million). The World Bank (IBRD) supported MWSS and both concessions. The Global Partnership on Output-Based Aid (GPOBA) gave USD 2.8 million for connections for the poor. [source: 1, 2]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- World Bank (2006): devaluation and FX-denominated concession fees and loans were the main cause of Maynilad's failure. It also lists poor service levels and rising NRW in the West Zone, and notes that NRW is the hardest thing to control "when the flow of funds is uncertain". [source: 3]
- The two-zone design gave the regulator a built-in performance benchmark, as IFC intended. The divergence between the zones is the clearest result of that design. [source: 1, 3]
- Note that the IFC case study is self-reported by the adviser. [source: 1]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 81 (Social infrastructure, water, desalination and waste-to-energy):** Use Manila as the classic currency-mismatch case. Revenues were in pesos, but concession fees and capex debt were in dollars. Show how the FCDA pass-through tried to fix this through tariffs, and why tariff pass-through is only as good as the regulator's willingness to approve it in a crisis. Compare the zones: same contract, same city, same regulator, different FX exposure and operator performance. Use the performance bond, the rehabilitation court and the DCRA's cash waterfall to show workout tools. End with the 2021 franchise laws as the state's later attempt to put concessions on a statutory footing and require public listing.
