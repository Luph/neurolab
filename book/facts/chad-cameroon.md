# Chad–Cameroon Petroleum Development and Pipeline Project

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: August 2026, Savannah Energy regulatory filing confirming ICC arbitration claims against Chad remain pending)

## Summary

The Chad–Cameroon Petroleum Development and Pipeline Project developed three oil fields near Doba in southern Chad and a 1,070 km buried export pipeline to an offshore floating storage and offloading vessel off Kribi, Cameroon. An ExxonMobil-led consortium (ExxonMobil 40 percent as operator, Petronas 35 percent, Chevron 25 percent after Shell and Elf withdrew in 1999) financed the oil fields entirely with equity and financed the pipeline companies, TOTCO (Chad) and COTCO (Cameroon), with equity, IFC A and B loans, and loans from US Ex-Im and COFACE. The World Bank approved its participation on 6 June 2000; its IBRD loans financed the two governments' minority equity stakes in the pipeline, and its condition for support was Chad's 1999 Petroleum Revenue Management Law, which earmarked most direct oil revenues for priority poverty sectors, set aside 10 percent in a Future Generations Fund, and routed revenues through a London escrow account under the oversight of a joint government–civil society committee (the Collège). Oil flowed in July 2003, more than a year early, and the project was a technical and financial success. But as oil prices rose and armed rebellion grew, Chad amended the law in December 2005 to abolish the Future Generations Fund and add security to the priority sectors. The World Bank suspended disbursements and froze the escrow account in January 2006, reached interim and July 2006 agreements, and in September 2008 ended its involvement after Chad missed the new spending targets and prepaid USD 65.7 million of World Bank loans. The World Bank's Independent Evaluation Group (IEG) rated the overall program outcome unsatisfactory, principally because of lack of government ownership. The story continued: Chad bought Chevron's interests in 2014, and in March 2023 nationalized the upstream and pipeline interests that ExxonMobil had sold to Savannah Energy, which is pursuing ICC arbitration.

## Verified facts

1. In 1988 Chad granted a consortium of Esso (now ExxonMobil), Shell and Elf a 10-year exploration permit (later extended) and a 30-year lease to develop three oil fields near Doba and export the crude. Shell and Elf withdrew in 1999 and were replaced by Petronas (35 percent) and Chevron (25 percent); ExxonMobil remained operator with 40 percent. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The World Bank Group agreed in late 1994 to work with the consortium and the two governments. The consortium and interested commercial banks made clear that a multilateral development agency had to participate to mitigate the adverse conditions. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. The project covered about 300 wells, field facilities, and a 1,070 km buried pipeline from the Doba fields to a floating storage and loading facility about 11 km off the Cameroon coast. The pipeline and floating facility were built and operated by Tchad Oil Transportation Company (TOTCO) and Cameroon Oil Transportation Company (COTCO), in which the governments took minority equity stakes. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. Appraisal cost was USD 3.72 billion (field system USD 1,521 million; export system USD 2,202 million). Actual cost to 2005 was USD 4,817 million (field system USD 2,648 million; export system USD 2,169 million). IEG later cited an actual cost of USD 6.5 billion through 2008. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
5. The oil fields were financed entirely by consortium equity. The export system financing plan at appraisal was: commercial loans USD 600 million, IFC A and B loans USD 400 million, a USD 400 million capital markets bond, consortium equity USD 685 million, Chad government equity USD 48 million and Cameroon government equity USD 70 million. Actual: commercial (ECA) loans USD 400 million, IFC loans USD 200 million, no bond, consortium equity USD 1,450 million, Chad USD 49 million, Cameroon USD 70 million. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. IFC provided USD 200 million: an A loan of USD 100 million (USD 13.9 million to TOTCO, USD 86.1 million to COTCO) and a B loan of USD 100 million in the same split, with 18 commercial banks participating in the B loan. US Ex-Im and COFACE each provided USD 200 million. The expected USD 400 million bond did not materialize; the gap was largely funded by consortium equity. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. IBRD lent Chad USD 39.5 million (approved; IEG reports USD 37.2 million) and Cameroon USD 53.4 million as "enclave" loans to fund the governments' pipeline equity, interest during construction and other charges; EIB lent Chad EUR 20.3 million and Cameroon EUR 35.7 million for the same purpose, and EIB also lent EUR 88 million to the consortium. Chad was to acquire an 11 percent share in TOTCO and 5 percent in COTCO, and Cameroon 10 percent in COTCO; these shares were later reduced as the financing plan was restructured (actual: Chad 8 percent of TOTCO and 3 percent of COTCO; Cameroon 5 percent of COTCO). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
8. World Bank financing was less than 3 percent of total project cost; total World Bank Group financing (including IFC and three IDA capacity-building credits) was USD 337.6 million, around 9 percent of the original estimate and 5 percent of the USD 6.5 billion actual cost through 2008. Over 90 percent of financing came from the consortium and commercial banks. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
9. Three IDA capacity-building credits accompanied the project: Chad Management of the Petroleum Economy (approved 27 January 2000, about USD 17.5 million), Chad Petroleum Sector Management Capacity Building (6 June 2000, about USD 23.7 million) and Cameroon Petroleum Environment Capacity Enhancement (6 June 2000, about USD 5.8 million). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. Chad's Petroleum Revenue Management Law (Law 001/PR/99) was promulgated on 11 January 1999. It allocated 10 percent of royalties and dividends to a Future Generations Fund; of the remainder, 80 percent of royalties and 85 percent of dividends to priority poverty-reduction sectors (health, social services, education, infrastructure, rural development, environment and water), 5 percent of royalties to the producing region, and the remaining 15 percent to general expenditure until the end of 2007. Revenues were to pass through an offshore escrow account in London, and spending from the special petroleum accounts required approval of the Collège de Contrôle et de Surveillance des Ressources Pétrolières. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. The law's coverage was limited to direct revenues (royalties and dividends) from the Doba fields; the World Bank's own 2006 overview says the geographic and revenue scope "could have been wider." Signature bonuses and indirect revenues were outside the earmarking. [confidence: high for the scope limitation; see fact 12 for the bonus episode] [source: 1, 5]
12. In 2000 Chad used part of a USD 25 million payment from the consortium for arms purchases. Arbogast (2009) states that President Déby used much of the USD 25 million Chevron/Petronas payment for arms; academic accounts put the amount at USD 4.5 million and report that Déby agreed to repay it from the budget. [confidence: medium; the use for arms is widely documented, the precise amount and payers vary by source] [source: 3, 6]
13. Chad was projected at appraisal to receive about USD 1.8 billion over a 24-year operating period (about USD 75 million a year), based on an oil price of USD 15.25 per barrel. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. The 1999 Environmental Management Plan ran to 19 volumes; an independent External Compliance Monitoring Group (ECMG) issued a Certificate of Compliance in October 2004, and an International Advisory Group of five members, led by a former Prime Minister of Senegal, advised the World Bank and both governments. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
15. The World Bank Inspection Panel investigated two requests. In the Chad case (request March 2001, report July 2002) it found compliance with most policies but only partial compliance with the environmental assessment policy (OD 4.01), for lack of a regional or cumulative impact assessment. In the Cameroon case (request September 2002, report May 2003) it found partial compliance on environmental assessment and indigenous peoples (OD 4.20). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
16. Pipeline construction peaked at about 13,400 workers in November 2002. The pipeline was completed in July 2003; the official first oil ceremony was in October 2003; first royalties reached the London escrow account in November 2003; the first transfer to Chad (USD 32 million) was in July 2004; lenders declared project completion in October 2004. First oil came more than a year ahead of the initial December 2004 target. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
17. By end-December 2005 cumulative petroleum revenues were USD 426 million, of which USD 307 million had been transferred to Chad and USD 36.2 million placed in the Future Generations Fund. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
18. On 29 December 2005 Chad's National Assembly amended the law. The amendments abolished the Future Generations Fund (and released its USD 36.2 million to the treasury), added justice, territorial administration and security to the priority sectors, raised the share of direct oil revenue usable for non-priority spending from 13.5 to 30 percent, and extended the law to all oil fields. The revised law was enacted on 11 January 2006. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
19. On 6 January 2006 the World Bank suspended disbursements on its Chad portfolio (except a regional locust operation), which under the escrow agreement also froze the escrow account in London. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
20. On 26 April 2006 the World Bank and Chad reached an interim agreement under which Chad would apply 70 percent of direct revenue to priority sectors excluding security; a memorandum of understanding followed on 13 July 2006, under which the government committed to allocate 70 percent of its budget to priority poverty programs. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
21. Chad missed the new allocation targets by a significant margin. In August 2008 the World Bank persuaded Chad to prepay the outstanding IBRD loan and the balances of two IDA credits (the first such IDA prepayment in Bank history, per IEG); Chad paid USD 65.7 million in September 2008, and on 9 September 2008 the World Bank announced it could not continue to support the project. IFC continued to monitor E&S aspects. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
22. IEG's Program Performance Assessment Report (Report 50315, 16 September 2009) rated the overall program outcome unsatisfactory despite the technical and financial success of the pipeline project, citing lack of government ownership as the principal reason. It rated the main pipeline project "moderately satisfactory" and the three IDA capacity-building projects "unsatisfactory." It also found that World Bank Group involvement produced stronger E&S protection and higher priority-sector allocations than would otherwise have occurred, and that the World Bank was right to support the program in 2000. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
23. Chevron sold its 25 percent interest in the Doba fields and its 21 percent interest in the pipeline system to the Republic of Chad for approximately USD 1.3 billion, closing on 13 June 2014. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
24. ExxonMobil completed the sale of its Chad and Cameroon upstream and pipeline interests to Savannah Energy in December 2022 (announced consideration USD 407 million). On 23–24 March 2023 Chad nationalized the assets, including the 40 percent Doba interest and the related pipeline interests, saying the final terms differed from those presented to it. [confidence: high] [source: 8, 9]
25. Savannah subsidiaries commenced ICC arbitrations against Chad in 2023. In the COTCO-related case (ICC Case 27914, Paris seat, Cameroonian law), Savannah Midstream Investment Limited seeks about USD 397 million; an emergency arbitrator granted interim measures on 28 July 2023. Savannah's August 2026 filing reported the claims unchanged; no final award had been disclosed as of that filing. [confidence: medium] [source: 10, 11]

## Financing and structure details

- Field system (Chad upstream): 100 percent consortium equity (actual USD 2,648 million to 2005). [source: 1]
- Export system (pipeline and FSO, TOTCO and COTCO), actual to 2005, USD million: ECA-supported commercial loans 400 (US Ex-Im 200, COFACE 200); IFC 200 (A loan 100, B loan 100 with 18 banks; each split 13.9 TOTCO and 86.1 COTCO); consortium equity and sponsor loans 1,450; Chad equity 49; Cameroon equity 70. Total export system 2,169. The planned USD 400 million bond was not issued. [source: 1]
- Government equity funding: IBRD enclave loans (Chad USD 39.5 million approved, Cameroon USD 53.4 million) plus EIB loans (Chad EUR 20.3 million, Cameroon EUR 35.7 million). [source: 1, 2]
- Revenue control architecture: Law 001/PR/99 allocation rules; offshore escrow account in London (account bank not named in the World Bank sources); the Collège oversight committee; cross-default between the law and the World Bank legal agreements, which is why amending the law triggered suspension and the escrow freeze. [source: 1, 3]
- Arbogast (2009) describes the structure as concentrating the lenders (World Bank, IFC, ECAs) and the governments' equity at the pipeline, the export choke point, so that a host-government action would damage the governments' own investments and their relationships with their most important development lenders. [source: 3]

## What went wrong or right, and why

IEG (2009) concluded that the pipeline was a physical, technical and financial success, helped by high oil prices, but that the fundamental objective of reducing poverty and improving governance in Chad was not achieved; the oil windfall coincided with renewed civil conflict and worse governance. It identified lack of government ownership, with repeated violations of the agreements, as the principal cause, and added that no alternative design or closer supervision would have achieved the objectives without government commitment, although a more flexible design might have produced better results. [source: 2]

The World Bank's 2006 overview, written during the crisis, listed design lessons: the revenue law's scope could have been wider; saving and stabilization principles should have been stressed; the law should have given flexibility for legitimate shocks and should have included an enforcement mechanism for the Collège's findings; capacity building started too late and moved far slower than private construction, which finished early. [source: 1]

Arbogast (2009), an executive professor at the University of Houston's Bauer College of Business writing in the Texas Journal of Oil, Gas, and Energy Law, analyzes the financing as contract political risk mitigation. He argues that the World Bank's involvement and the revenue management plan, although they failed as development tools, insulated the private sponsors: Chad's pressure fell on the World Bank and the revenue plan rather than on the consortium's contracts. [source: 3]

Pegg and other academic critics characterize the project as a failed experiment in using lender conditionality to direct oil revenues to the poor; these are scholarly positions, not findings of fact. [source: 6]

The post-2014 history (Chevron's exit by sale to the state, then the 2023 nationalization of the ExxonMobil interests after their sale to Savannah) shows the obsolescing bargain playing out once the World Bank had left and the debt had been repaid. [source: 7, 8, 9]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 50 (Environmental and social risk and standards): Use the project for multilayered independent monitoring (ECMG, International Advisory Group, Inspection Panel) and for the gap between E&S compliance and development outcome: the ECMG certified EMP compliance in October 2004, yet IEG rated the program unsatisfactory. Teach the Inspection Panel findings on cumulative impact assessment and indigenous peoples as the two places reviewers most often find shortfalls in pipeline E&S work.
- Chapter 60 (Political risk and its protection): This is the textbook case of the multilateral halo and its limits. Teach how lenders and host-government equity were concentrated at the pipeline choke point (Arbogast), how IBRD-financed government equity aligned the states with the project, and how the protection lapsed once Chad prepaid the World Bank in 2008; then show the obsolescing bargain completing in the 2014 buyout of Chevron and the 2023 nationalization and ICC arbitrations.
- Chapter 75 (Upstream and midstream oil and gas): Show the classic split between an equity-financed upstream and a project-financed midstream export system, with TOTCO and COTCO as separate pipeline companies in two jurisdictions, ECA and IFC A/B debt, and a planned bond replaced by sponsor equity. Use the cost growth from USD 3.72 billion to USD 4.8 billion by 2005 (and USD 6.5 billion by 2008), and first oil a year early, to discuss how field-development cost risk sits with sponsor equity while the pipeline lenders take a narrower risk.

## Do not state

- Do not state the exact amount of the bonus used for arms as definitive; sources give "much of" USD 25 million (Arbogast) and USD 4.5 million (academic accounts). Attribute any figure.
- Do not state that Citibank held the escrow account unless a primary source is found; the World Bank overview says only "an escrow account based in London."
- Do not state the pipeline length split between Chad and Cameroon (commonly said to be about 890 km in Cameroon); the IEG text is ambiguous.
- Do not state loan tenors, margins or ECA cover terms; they are not in the verified sources.
- Do not say the World Bank "cancelled" the project or that the pipeline stopped operating in 2008; the World Bank exited after prepayment and the pipeline kept operating.
- Do not state that ExxonMobil's December 2022 sale price was paid in full or adjusted; only the announced USD 407 million is verified.
- Do not state any ICC final award or outcome in the Savannah cases; none had been disclosed as of August 2026. Do not rely on the trade-press characterization that Chad "won" the arbitration in May 2024; the verified event is a procedural order refusing an account freeze.
- Do not state Savannah's total claim figure (USD 1.22 billion appears in secondary reporting of its 2023 annual report) without checking the company's filing.
- Do not state current ownership percentages of TOTCO or COTCO.
