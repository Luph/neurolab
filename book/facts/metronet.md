# Metronet and the London Underground PPP

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: June 2010, completion of Transport for London's purchase of Tube Lines, which ended the London Underground PPP; core Metronet coverage runs to the NAO report of 5 June 2009 and the Public Accounts Committee report of February 2010)

## Summary

Between December 2002 and April 2003, London Underground Limited (LUL) let three 30-year public-private partnership (PPP) contracts to maintain, renew and upgrade the Tube's track, signals, trains and stations. Each contract went to an infrastructure company ("infraco"): Tube Lines (Jubilee, Northern and Piccadilly lines, owned by Ferrovial/Amey and Bechtel) and two Metronet companies, Metronet BCV (Bakerloo, Central, Victoria and Waterloo and City) and Metronet SSL (sub-surface lines). Metronet was owned in equal shares by five firms that were also its main contractors: Atkins, Balfour Beatty, Bombardier, EDF Energy and Thames Water. The infracos were paid a fixed monthly infrastructure service charge with performance adjustments, and price, scope and funding were to be reset every seven and a half years with an independent PPP Arbiter ruling on whether costs were "economic and efficient". The public sector guaranteed at least 95% of the infracos' borrowing. Metronet ran large cost overruns (an estimated GBP 750 million by 2006 and about GBP 2 billion by 2007), the Arbiter in July 2007 allowed it only GBP 121 million of the GBP 551 million it sought in an extraordinary review, its lenders stopped funding, its shareholders declined to inject more, and it entered administration on 18 July 2007. The Department for Transport paid GBP 1.7 billion so that TfL could meet the debt guarantee, and the National Audit Office (NAO) estimated the direct loss to the taxpayer at between GBP 170 million and GBP 410 million. The NAO attributed the failure mainly to Metronet's poor corporate governance and leadership, which left it unable to manage a supply chain dominated by its own shareholders. TfL absorbed Metronet in 2008 and bought out Tube Lines in 2010, ending the PPP.

## Verified facts

1. The PPP comprised three 30-year contracts. NAO (June 2004) valued planned spending at a present value of GBP 15.7 billion over 30 years (GBP 9.7 billion in the first seven and a half years). Price, scope and funding were to be reviewed every seven and a half years. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The NAO found "limited assurance" that the price of the three PPPs was reasonable; prices rose after competitive bidding, adding GBP 590 million to the 30-year cost; shareholders stood to earn nominal returns of 18% to 20% a year at bid performance (about a third higher than recent PFI deals); and because repayment was at least 95% guaranteed by the public sector, private borrowing still cost about GBP 450 million more than direct government borrowing. Work started two years later than originally planned. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Metronet's two contracts (BCV and SSL) started in April 2003; the Metronet contracts were the larger part of the PPP, covering nine of the Underground's 12 lines (Guardian) and described as worth GBP 17 billion. Tube Lines' contract had been let earlier (December 2002). [confidence: high for April 2003 and line coverage; medium for the December 2002 Tube Lines date and the GBP 17 billion figure, which are press figures] [source: 2, 3, 8]
4. Metronet's five equal shareholders were Atkins, Balfour Beatty, Bombardier (successor to Adtranz), EDF Energy (successor to SEEBOARD) and Thames Water. Under a "tied" or "closed" supply chain, work under the contracts was carried out by the shareholders. Tube Lines, owned by Ferrovial (via Amey) and Bechtel, let work by competitive tender. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4, 5]
5. Metronet's shareholders had invested GBP 350 million in total (about GBP 70 million each). [confidence: medium; press figure, consistent with five equal shareholders] [source: 5, 8]
6. In its first annual review (November 2006) of Metronet's performance from April 2003 to March 2006, the Office of Rail Regulation, whose chairman Chris Bolt was also the PPP Arbiter, found that neither Metronet infraco had performed in line with the required standard, criticised its management structure and found some spending not economic or efficient. Metronet's projected overrun for the first seven-and-a-half-year period was estimated at GBP 750 million, part of which could fall on shareholders. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
7. Under the PPP payment structure, Metronet's two companies were paid monthly instalments of public money worth a combined GBP 660 million in 2005-06 (per year, not per month), adjusted for performance. [confidence: medium] [source: 2]
8. In July 2007 the PPP Arbiter refused Metronet's request for GBP 551 million in an extraordinary review and awarded GBP 121 million. Metronet's lenders had frozen its loan facility after granting two earlier waivers, and its shareholders declined to put in more money. The cost overrun was then estimated at about GBP 2 billion. [confidence: high for 551/121; medium for GBP 2 billion] [source: 5, 6]
9. Metronet entered administration on 18 July 2007; the administrator was Alan Bloom of Ernst & Young, who had also handled Railtrack. TfL provided emergency funding from its reserves. The European Investment Bank was among Metronet's lenders. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
10. TfL guaranteed 95% of Metronet's borrowing, and the Secretary of State for Transport had assured Metronet's lenders that the DfT would not stand by if LUL or TfL could not honour that guarantee. When Metronet failed, the DfT made a GBP 1.7 billion payment to meet the guarantee. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
11. The NAO (5 June 2009, HC 512 2008-09) estimated the direct loss to the taxpayer at between GBP 170 million and GBP 410 million, equivalent to between 4% and 10% of the work delivered; Metronet nevertheless delivered GBP 4.2 billion of maintenance and upgrades. The NAO said Metronet failed "predominantly because its poor corporate governance and leadership meant that it could not manage its shareholder-dominated supply chain", and that the PPP contracts gave the DfT few formal levers to protect the taxpayer. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
12. The House of Commons Transport Committee (January 2008) criticised the PPP heavily (the Guardian reported its description of the contracts as "pretty much useless"); in its March 2008 response, the government said the failure was "predominantly a corporate failure, and that the structural weaknesses of Metronet led to its own downfall". [confidence: high for the government response; medium for the Committee phrase, which is reported] [source: 9]
13. Metronet's business transferred to TfL ownership in May 2008 (two TfL-owned nominee companies, LUL Nominee BCV and LUL Nominee SSL) and was later integrated into London Underground. [confidence: medium] [source: 8]
14. The House of Commons Committee of Public Accounts reported on the failure of Metronet on 22 February 2010; reporting of it cites the GBP 170 million to GBP 410 million loss range and shareholder losses of about GBP 350 million. [confidence: medium] [source: 8]
15. On 7 May 2010 Amey (Ferrovial) agreed to sell its 66.7% of Tube Lines to TfL for about GBP 206 million; Bechtel also sold its stake to TfL; completion was scheduled for June 2010; Amey kept a seven-and-a-half-year maintenance contract for the three lines. With this purchase, the PPP model on the Underground ended. [confidence: high] [source: 10]

## Financing and structure details

- Contract form: three 30-year infrastructure service contracts between LUL and each infraco; LUL kept train operation, the infracos maintained and upgraded assets for an infrastructure service charge adjusted for performance (availability, ambience, capability). [source: 1, 3]
- Periodic review: price, scope and funding reset every seven and a half years; the PPP Arbiter decided what an "economic and efficient" infraco would have spent, which determined how much of any overrun the infraco bore. Infracos could seek an extraordinary review mid-period. [source: 1, 2, 5]
- Risk-sharing on overruns: Metronet bore overruns found not economic or efficient; the press reported Tube Lines as liable for overspends up to GBP 200 million, which signals the infracos' capped downside. [source: 4, 5]
- Debt: at least 95% of infraco borrowing was effectively guaranteed by the public sector (NAO 2004); on Metronet's failure, TfL's 95% guarantee was met with a GBP 1.7 billion DfT payment (NAO 2009). Lenders included a bank group and the European Investment Bank. [source: 1, 5, 7]
- Equity: about GBP 350 million in total from five equal shareholders, which were also the main subcontractors. [source: 5]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Governance and the tied supply chain (NAO 2009; ORR 2006). Shareholders were also suppliers, so the infraco could not hold its contractors to price and performance; information on costs was poor; management structures were "deficient". The NAO named poor corporate governance and leadership as the predominant cause. [source: 2, 7]
- Risk transfer limited by the guarantee (NAO 2004 and 2009). With 95% of debt guaranteed, lenders had little incentive to police the infraco, and when it failed the public sector repaid them; private capital at risk was equity of about GBP 350 million against billions of spending. [source: 1, 7]
- Weak public levers (NAO 2009). The contracts gave the DfT, the party ultimately exposed, few formal levers; it relied on LUL, TfL, shareholders and lenders to fix problems, and when they did not, the taxpayer was exposed. [source: 7]
- Contract design versus corporate failure. The government (March 2008) and the NAO emphasised corporate failure; the Transport Committee and TfL figures such as Stephen Glaister argued the PPP structure itself had failed. Tube Lines, under the same contract model but with competitive procurement, stayed on budget until its own dispute over the second review period, which supports the governance diagnosis without clearing the structure. [source: 4, 7, 9]
- Price and complexity (NAO 2004). The deals cost more than public borrowing, bid prices rose during negotiation, and the condition of assets was poorly known, so the output specification and periodic-review mechanism carried more weight than they could bear. [source: 1]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 57 (The public-sector case for PPPs): Use NAO's numbers to teach value-for-money arithmetic: a GBP 450 million financing premium for a structure in which 95% of debt was publicly guaranteed means the public sector paid for risk transfer it did not get. Then use the 2009 outcome (GBP 1.7 billion repaid, GBP 170 to 410 million loss) to show contingent liabilities crystallising and why fiscal treatment and real exposure must be assessed together. Stress the lesson that a government needs contractual levers in proportion to its ultimate exposure.
- Chapter 80 (Rail, urban transit, airports and ports): Use Metronet versus Tube Lines as a controlled comparison: same contract family, different supply-chain governance, different outcomes. Teach how an availability-style rail maintenance PPP with periodic review and an independent Arbiter shifts disputes to "economic and efficient" cost, and how a tied supply chain defeats the cost discipline that the structure assumes. Close with the end of the PPP (TfL buys Tube Lines in 2010) to show the public sector re-internalising complex brownfield rail renewal.

## Do not state

- Do not state that Metronet's monthly payments were GBP 660 million a month; the Guardian figure is a combined annual amount for 2005-06.
- Do not state the precise Metronet debt quantum (figures of GBP 2 billion bank loans and GBP 600 million from the EIB appear in secondary sources only) or the exact maturity and pricing of tranches.
- Do not state that the total value of all PPP contracts was GBP 30 billion or that Metronet's were GBP 17 billion as precise contract values; these are press figures with unclear basis. Use NAO's present value of GBP 15.7 billion for the three contracts.
- Do not state exact dates for the Tube Lines contract (often given as 31 December 2002) or Metronet's (4 April 2003) without a primary document; use December 2002 and April 2003.
- Do not quote the Transport Committee report's wording beyond what the Guardian reported, or attribute to it findings not verified from the report itself.
- Do not state the total price TfL paid for Tube Lines (about GBP 310 million is often cited); only Amey's GBP 206 million for 66.7% is verified from the seller's release.
- Do not state that the PPP Arbiter or NAO found the PPP structure itself to be the cause; NAO's finding was predominantly corporate governance and leadership, with weak DfT levers.
- Do not state that shareholders lost exactly GBP 350 million or each lost GBP 70 million as an audited figure; it is the reported equity invested.
