# Indicative project finance debt terms by sector and region, 2023–2026: DSCRs, tenors, gearing, margins, fees, reserve sizes

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: ReNew Energy Global plc Form 20-F for the year ended 31 March 2026, filed 30 July 2026; latest market survey covered: Norton Rose Fulbright "Cost of Capital: 2026 Outlook", January 2026)

## Summary

Project finance terms are not fixed numbers but negotiated outcomes that move with sector, revenue contract quality, country risk, lender type and the credit cycle. The best public evidence for 2023–2026 comes from two kinds of source: practitioner surveys (above all the annual Norton Rose Fulbright "Cost of Capital" outlook, in which bank panellists state US renewables, storage and data-centre spreads and coverage ratios), and the finance-document summaries that listed sponsors must file with the US SEC (LNG, toll roads, data centres, Indian renewables, legacy Latin American and South African assets). Together these show a consistent pattern. Fully contracted US solar was sized at about 1.25–1.30x P50 DSCR, wind at 1.30–1.40x, contracted storage at 1.15–1.20x, while merchant exposure pushed requirements to about 1.75–2.0x; bank spreads for clean contracted US deals sat at about SOFR + 125–200 bp across 2023–2026, tightening slightly in 2025. US LNG export projects (Port Arthur 2023, Rio Grande 2023, CP2 2025) used seven-year-style bank mini-perms priced at SOFR + 2.00–2.75%, six-month debt service reserves, 1.10x historical DSCR covenants and 1.20x distribution tests, against amortisation sculpted over a much longer notional profile. US managed-lane toll roads used tax-exempt Private Activity Bonds and federal TIFIA loans with final maturities into the 2050s. AI data-centre financings in 2025 priced far wider (CoreWeave SOFR + 4%; Applied Digital 9.25% notes at 97) than contracted renewables. Indian renewables borrowed rupee term loans of up to 20 years from state lenders with two-quarter DSRAs. The verified record is thin for Middle East, Latin American, African, mining and PPP pricing in this period; for those, the sheet gives principles and flags what must not be stated as fact.

## Verified facts

### A. North America: renewables, storage, thermal (practitioner survey evidence)

1. The Norton Rose Fulbright (NRF) "Cost of Capital" outlook is an annual panel of project finance bankers and advisers moderated by Keith Martin (NRF). The 2024 edition (published 19 February 2024), 2025 edition (January 2025) and 2026 edition (webinar 21 January 2026) included Beth (Elizabeth) Waters (MUFG) and Ralph Cho (Apterra Infrastructure Capital) on bank debt. Figures below are panellists' stated market views for the US market, not regulatory rules. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
2. DSCR norms, US contracted renewables, P50 basis (Waters, MUFG): early 2024: wind 1.30–1.40x P50; solar 1.25x P50 and 1.0x P99; contracted battery storage 1.15–1.20x P50. Early 2025: solar 1.25–1.30x P50; wind 1.35–1.40x P50 (operating wind portfolios 1.30x minimum); storage 1.15–1.20x. Early 2026: solar 1.25–1.30x; wind 1.35–1.40x; storage 1.15–1.20x. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
3. DSCR norms, US merchant exposure (Waters): early 2024: merchant wind 1.80–2.0x P50; merchant solar 1.75x P50 and 1.40x P99; merchant storage at least 2.0x P50. Early 2025: solar 1.75x P50 / 1.40x one-year P99; wind 1.80x P50 / 1.40–1.50x one-year P99; storage 2.0x minimum. Early 2026: solar 1.75x, wind 1.80x, storage 2.0x (P50). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
4. In 2024 Cho described a "tight" coverage ratio in the then-current market as 1.2–1.35x P50, and the panel agreed lenders do not want to see coverage below 1.0x on P99 numbers. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
5. Spreads, US construction debt for contracted renewables: early 2024, about SOFR + 150–175 bp (Waters) or 162.5–175 bp (Cho, up from 150 bp previously). Early 2025, about SOFR + 150 bp for fully contracted projects (Waters) and 150–187.5 bp for plain-vanilla renewables (Cho). Early 2026, about 150 bp for clean deals, with some developers achieving 125–137.5 bp (Waters); Cho said spreads had tightened about 12.5 bp at both ends year on year. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
6. Spreads, US term (post-construction) debt: early 2024, fully contracted term debt about SOFR + 178–200 bp with 12.5 bp step-ups (Waters, as reported). Early 2026, "middle of the fairway" term loans SOFR + 162.5–187.5 bp; 10–20 percent merchant revenue adds about 25 bp and 30–40 percent merchant adds a further 25 bp (Waters). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
7. Merchant and other premia in the US: early 2024, merchant risk added 75–125 bp (Cho) or put pricing at about SOFR + 170–250 bp depending on merchant share (Waters); merchant gas deals priced at SOFR + 350–500 bp, with private credit needing at least 400 bp (Cho). Early 2025: merchant batteries SOFR + 275–350 bp, community solar 237.5–287.5 bp, and a battery premium of about 25 bp over wind and solar in operations. Early 2026: Cho suggested 25–30 percent as the practical maximum merchant share before financing becomes difficult. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
8. Tax credit bridge loans (US): committed (tax-equity-covered) bridges at a 98 percent advance rate priced about SOFR + 150–162.5 bp (2024, 2025) and about 150 bp (2026); uncovered tax-credit-sale bridges at a 75 percent advance rate against 90 percent of credit face value (net 67.5 percent) priced at SOFR + 225–300 bp (2024) and about 225 bp (2026). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
9. Development (pre-NTP) facilities: SOFR + 375–600 bp (2024), 350–650 bp (2025), and 350–450 bp mainstream with riskier facilities at 600–800 bp (2026) (Cho). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
10. Fees (US, early 2024, Cho): total upfront economics of about 200–300 bp, comprising a structuring fee of 25–100 bp, upfront fees to participating banks of 100–200 bp and an underwriting fee of 25–50 bp; Waters said the upfront fee typically matches the starting margin. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
11. Leverage (US, early 2024, Waters): construction debt maximum of roughly 90 percent of total project cost (in a US structure in which tax equity or tax-credit proceeds repay much of the construction loan at completion). [confidence: high] [source: 3]
12. Lender depth and volume (US, Cho): 2023 North American bank project finance volume about USD 115 billion over 214 deals (USD 116 billion over 226 deals in 2022); 70–80 active lenders, down from about 100 historically, with 30–40 core lenders (2024 edition). 2025 North American project finance volume just under USD 260 billion across about 500 deals, up 41 percent; global project finance lending over USD 500 billion (2026 edition). [confidence: medium; panellist estimates from different data sets] [source: 1, 3]
13. Base rates: the 20-year swap rate used for US project finance was 4.10 percent at end-2025 and about 4.25 percent in January 2026 (Waters). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. An illustrative Canadian rated contracted wind bond: Comber Wind Financial Corporation's senior secured bonds (BBB, Morningstar DBRS, confirmed 20 June 2023) mature 15 November 2030, about 12 months before the 20-year Ontario feed-in tariff contracts expire (November 2031); DBRS's rating-case minimum DSCR projection was 1.38x and the 12-month DSCR to 31 December 2022 was 1.56x. [confidence: high] [source: 20]

### B. LNG export (US Gulf Coast, from SEC filings)

15. Port Arthur LNG Phase 1 (Sempra, Texas), financial close 20 March 2023: term loan facility approximately USD 6.8 billion plus a working capital facility approximately USD 200 million; estimated capital expenditure approximately USD 13.0 billion (implying term debt of roughly 52 percent of capex); upfront equity of USD 4.7 billion required before first term-loan advance. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
16. Port Arthur LNG pricing and fees: margin over Term SOFR 2.00 percent before project completion and 2.25 percent after; commitment fee 30 percent of the applicable margin on undrawn commitments; upfront and transaction fees in aggregate approximately USD 200 million (about 2.9 percent of the term loan). [confidence: high; percentage computed] [source: 5]
17. Port Arthur LNG tenor and amortisation: loans mature 20 March 2030 (seven years, a mini-perm); quarterly repayment begins at the earlier of about three months after completion and 20 April 2029, based on a 20-year sculpted amortisation profile, so most principal must be refinanced at maturity. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
18. Port Arthur LNG covenants: at least 60 percent of projected term loans must be hedged or fixed; distributions require completion (or confirmed sufficiency of funds), a debt service reserve account (DSRA) funded to six months of debt service, and historical and projected 12-month DSCR of at least 1.20x; minimum historical DSCR covenant 1.10x; additional supplemental senior debt requires a projected DSCR of not less than 1.40x. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
19. Rio Grande LNG Phase 1 (NextDecade, Texas), FID 12 July 2023: construction/term loans up to USD 10.3 billion (commercial bank facility) plus a USD 800 million construction/term facility under a second credit agreement and USD 700 million of 6.67 percent senior secured notes due 7 July 2033 (private placement at par), plus a USD 500 million revolving loan and letter of credit facility. Bank loans bear interest at SOFR + 2.25 percent (base rate + 1.25 percent), with commitment fees on undrawn amounts. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
20. Rio Grande LNG: the main credit agreement matures seven years from closing; term loan proceeds may fund a DSRA up to six months of scheduled debt service; minimum historical DSCR covenant 1.10x from the initial principal payment date; EPC lump-sum price approximately USD 11.96 billion; equity commitments of approximately USD 5.9 billion (TotalEnergies and financial investors) plus approximately USD 283 million from NextDecade. On these figures term debt (about USD 11.8 billion excluding the revolver) was roughly two-thirds of committed term debt plus equity. [confidence: high for filing figures; medium for the computed ratio] [source: 4]
21. CP2 LNG Phase 1 (Venture Global, Louisiana), 28 July 2025: USD 11.25 billion construction/term loan and USD 850 million working capital facility (USD 12.1 billion total) at SOFR + 2.25–2.75 percent (base rate + 1.25–1.75 percent); construction drawings available until the earlier of 23 January 2030 or project completion; plus a separate USD 2.809 billion equity bridge facility and a USD 191 million three-year interest reserve facility at the holding company. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

### C. Offshore wind

22. Baltic Power (Poland, 1.14 GW; PKN Orlen 51 percent, Northland Power 49 percent): financial close announced 22 September 2023 with approximately CAD 5.2 billion of non-recourse green financing from 25 financial institutions including EIB, EBRD, Euler Hermes, EIFO (Denmark) and Export Development Canada. 23. Empire Wind 1 (Equinor, New York): financial close at the end of December 2024 on a project financing package of over USD 3 billion, against expected total capital investment of approximately USD 5 billion including the effect of expected investment tax credits. [confidence: medium; from Equinor release as reported] [source: 11]

### F. Asia-Pacific (India)

### J. Drivers that move the numbers (as evidenced above)

39. Revenue certainty: the same US banks moved from 1.25–1.40x (contracted) to 1.75–2.0x (merchant), and added about 25 bp per merchant band. [source: 1, 2, 3]
40. Resource and technology risk: wind is sized above solar (more volatile output); storage is sized lower when contracted (tolling or capacity revenues) but highest when merchant. [source: 1, 2, 3]
41. Base-rate cycle and hedging: all-in cost is margin plus swap; lenders require substantial hedging (Port Arthur at least 60 percent; Baltic Power hedged for the full amortisation period). [source: 1, 5, 8]
42. Market depth and refinancing risk: large US LNG debt uses seven-year mini-perms with long notional amortisation, creating refinancing risk sponsors accept for lower pricing. [source: 4, 5]
43. Sovereign and public lenders extend tenor (TIFIA and PABs into the 2050s; PFC 20-year rupee loans; DOE/FFB loans) and DFIs/ECAs anchor emerging-market and first-of-a-kind deals (Baltic Power, Samarkand). [source: 7, 13, 15, 18, 21]
44. Counterparty credit: data-centre spreads depend on the tenant (SOFR + about 250 bp for an Oracle-type tenant versus 325–425 bp for CoreWeave-type tenants). [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Merchant price exposure breaks thinly covered debt: Atlantica reported Chile PV 1 and 2 in default at end-2023 "due to low electricity prices", with a DSRA that could not be kept at its minimum; this is the case for the 1.75–2.0x merchant DSCRs quoted by MUFG's Waters. [source: 14, 1, 2]
- Mini-perm economics: Port Arthur's filing shows the trade-off explicitly: a seven-year bank maturity against a 20-year sculpted amortisation profile, accepting refinancing risk in exchange for bank pricing and liquidity. [source: 5]
- Refinancing to extend tenor: Ferrovial's managed lanes repeatedly refinanced TIFIA and short bonds into longer instruments (I-77 2024; LBJ 2021), which Ferrovial describes as optimising financial structure. [source: 13]
- Confidentiality of the key numbers: the DOE Thacker Pass agreement redacts its DSCR thresholds, a reminder that public sources often omit the very parameters a textbook would want. [source: 18]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 35 (CFADS and cover ratios): Use the MUFG ladder (storage 1.15–1.20x, solar 1.25–1.30x, wind 1.35–1.40x, merchant 1.75–2.0x) to show that the "right" DSCR is a function of cash-flow volatility, and pair P50 with P99 tests. Contrast sizing ratios with lock-up (1.20x) and default (1.10x) covenants using Port Arthur.
- Ch 36 (Sizing and sculpting): Port Arthur is a clean worked example of a mini-perm with a 20-year sculpted notional profile; Comber Wind shows a one-year contract tail. Have readers compute how much debt a 0.10x change in target DSCR removes.
- Ch 37 (Reserves, sweeps, covenants, hedging): Six-month DSRAs (US LNG) versus two-quarter DSRAs (India) are the same norm in different words; Port Arthur's 60 percent hedging minimum and Baltic Power's full-tenor hedging illustrate hedge profiles; Thacker Pass shows cash sweeps keyed to historical DSCR.
- Ch 38 (Pricing): Build an all-in cost from margin plus swap (4.10–4.25 percent 20-year swap, early 2026) plus fees (200–300 bp upfront; commitment fee 30 percent of margin). Compare bank margins with bond coupons (Rio Grande 6.67 percent; NTE PABs 5.50 percent; Applied Digital 9.25 percent at 97: assuming a 4.5–5-year life and semi-annual coupons, yield to maturity is roughly 10.0–10.1 percent, about 0.8 percentage points above the coupon).
- Ch 69 (Thermal power): Use the merchant gas range (SOFR + 350–500 bp, 2024) against contracted renewables to show the price of merchant thermal risk; note that verified contracted-IPP thermal terms for 2023–2026 are not in this sheet.
- Ch 70 (Onshore wind and solar): The US DSCR and spread ladder is the anchor; add the merchant premium bands (+25 bp per merchant tranche) and the Chile PV default as the downside case.
- Ch 71 (Offshore wind): Baltic Power shows DFI and ECA anchoring of a first-of-its-kind market with long tenor and full-tenor hedging; Empire Wind shows debt of about 60 percent of capex in a US ITC-supported structure.
- Ch 72 (Hydro and geothermal): No verified 2023–2026 norms in this sheet; use the principle that very long-lived assets support longer tenors when the offtake permits, and source deal-specific data separately.
- Ch 73 (Storage): Storage sits at both ends of the DSCR ladder (1.15–1.20x contracted, 2.0x merchant), which makes it the best teaching example of revenue-contract quality driving sizing.
- Ch 74 (Nuclear): Only the Fraunhofer modelling assumption (60 percent debt at 8.0 percent) is in this sheet; do not present it as a market term.
- Ch 75 (Upstream and midstream): Use LNG's covenant package as the bridge to midstream; RBL terms are not covered here.
- Ch 76 (LNG): Three US filings give a full term-sheet skeleton: margins (2.00–2.75 percent), seven-year bank tenor, six-month DSRA, 1.10x/1.20x/1.40x ratio tiers, hedging minimums, equity bridges (CP2). Ask readers why lenders accept 50–65 percent gearing in LNG versus 80 percent-plus in contracted renewables.
- Ch 77 (Industrial): Link to the Sadara sheet; no 2023–2026 norms here.
- Ch 78 (Mining): Teach that mining terms are bespoke and often confidential (Thacker Pass redactions), that completion support is standard (QB2 distribution restrictions until completion), and that political-risk mitigation via DFIs is a stated rationale (Reko Diq).
- Ch 79 (Toll roads): US managed lanes show the public-subsidised long tenor model (TIFIA, PABs, maturities 2050s) and the use of refinancings to extend average life.
- Ch 80 (Rail, airports, ports): No verified norms here; reuse toll-road principles cautiously.
- Ch 81 (Social infrastructure PPP): No verified 2023–2026 PPP pricing in this sheet; teach availability-payment DSCRs as a principle and source current figures separately.
- Ch 82 (Digital): Data centres price on tenant credit (SOFR + 225–425 bp) and DSCRs as low as 1.05–1.15x on hyperscaler leases; contrast CoreWeave's SOFR + 4 percent and Applied Digital's 9.25 percent notes with renewables.
- Ch 83 (Hydrogen, CCS, fuels): No verified norms; use the merchant-risk lessons (Cho's 25–30 percent merchant ceiling) to explain why such projects need contracted offtake or public support.

## Do not state

- Any single "market standard" DSCR, tenor or gearing for Europe, the Middle East, Latin America, Africa or Asia-Pacific for 2023–2026 (for example "Saudi solar tenors of 25–27 years", "European CfD wind at 1.15–1.20x", "Australian mini-perms of 5–7 years", "REIPPPP gearing of 70–80 percent", "PPP gearing 85–90 percent"): plausible and widely repeated, but not verified from primary or reliable sources in this research.
- Baltic Power's exact debt figure and tenor: sources differ (Northland: CAD 5.2 billion; trade press: about EUR 4.2 billion construction-plus-20-year facility; a law-firm headline: EUR 4.4 billion). State "approximately EUR 4.2–4.4 billion" or the Northland CAD figure, with the attribution.

- The headline "USD 2.26 billion" DOE loan for Thacker Pass: the filed agreement shows USD 1.97 billion principal plus capitalised interest; the larger figure (if used) must be described as including capitalised interest and verified first.
