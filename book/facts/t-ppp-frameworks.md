# PPP frameworks: World Bank PPP guidance, Eurostat/EPEC statistical treatment, IMF–World Bank PFRAM, and national PPP guidance (UK and selected others)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: UK Department of Health and Social Care "10 Year Capital Plan for Health and Social Care", published 8 July 2026, describing the new PPP model for Neighbourhood Health Centres)

## Summary

Most governments draw on the same international toolkit for PPP policy. The World Bank-led *PPP Reference Guide* (Version 3, 2017) defines a PPP as "a long-term contract between a private party and a government entity, for providing a public asset or service, in which the private party bears significant risk and management responsibility, and remuneration is linked to performance". It is a joint product of the World Bank Group, ADB, EBRD, GI Hub, IADB, IsDB, OECD, UNECE and UNESCAP. The World Bank's *Guidance on PPP Contractual Provisions* (2019 edition) covers standard contract clauses such as force majeure, change in law and termination.

On the fiscal side, the IMF and World Bank launched the PPP Fiscal Risk Assessment Model (PFRAM) in April 2016 (a later version 2.0 dates from 2019). It estimates the fiscal costs and contingent liabilities of PPP projects.

In the EU, whether a PPP is on or off the government balance sheet is decided under ESA 2010 and Eurostat's Manual on Government Deficit and Debt (MGDD). Eurostat and the EIB's European PPP Expertise Centre (EPEC) explain these rules in *A Guide to the Statistical Treatment of PPPs* (September 2016). The test is which party bears most of the risks and rewards. Several bright-line rules apply: government financing or support of 50% or more of construction capex puts the asset on balance sheet automatically, as does a government share of refinancing gains above one third. Accounting standards apply a different test (control, under IPSAS 32), so the same PPP can be off the statistical balance sheet but on the government's accounts.

National frameworks show the cycle of enthusiasm and retreat. The UK pioneered PFI. The National Audit Office counted over 700 operational PFI/PF2 deals with a capital value of around GBP 60 billion and future charges of GBP 199 billion running into the 2040s (January 2018). The Chancellor announced on 29 October 2018 that PFI and PF2 would not be used for future projects. In November 2025 the UK government announced a new PPP model, alongside public investment, to deliver Neighbourhood Health Centres; it is being developed by NISTA with a simpler payment mechanism and more flexibility for service change.

## Verified facts

**A. World Bank and multilateral PPP guidance (international; as of 2026-10-03)**

1. *Public-Private Partnerships Reference Guide, Version 3* was published in 2017. It is a joint product of the World Bank Group, ADB, EBRD, GI Hub, IADB, IsDB, OECD, UNECE and UNESCAP. Version 2.0 was published in 2014. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. Reference Guide definition: "A long-term contract between a private party and a government entity, for providing a public asset or service, in which the private party bears significant risk and management responsibility and remuneration is linked to performance." The Guide states there is no single internationally accepted definition, and that risk transfer is not a goal in itself but instrumental. [confidence: high, exact definition from the Guide] [source: 1]
3. The PPP Knowledge Lab was launched in 2015 by the AfDB, ADB, EBRD, IADB, IsDB and the World Bank Group, with PPIAF support. The EIB, GPOBA, OECD, UNESCAP and UNECE joined later. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The World Bank's *Guidance on PPP Contractual Provisions* has a 2019 edition (published with PPIAF). A 2023 World Bank compendium recommends it as a reference for drafting provisions to handle pandemic-type events. [confidence: high for the existence and date of the 2019 edition; its chapter list was not read] [source: 3]
5. Other World Bank tools (titles and dates verified in the World Bank Documents repository): *A Framework for Disclosure in Public-Private Partnerships* (2016); *Benchmarking Infrastructure Development 2020*, which assesses regulatory quality to prepare, procure and manage PPPs and traditional public investment (7 October 2020); and *Fiscal Risks from Early Termination of Public-Private Partnerships in Infrastructure* (March 2022). [confidence: high] [source: 4]
6. Examples of national PPP rules cited in the Reference Guide (as at its 2017 drafting; check current law):
   - Australia's National PPP Guidelines suggest PPPs will likely only suit projects over AUD 50 million.
   - Singapore pursues PPPs only for projects over 50 million (Singapore dollars implied).
   - Brazil's federal PPP Law 11079 (2004) set a BRL 20 million minimum, covered sponsored and administrative concessions only, and required a minimum five-year term.
   - Chile's Concessions Law caps concession terms at 50 years.
   - Colombia's Law 1508 (2012) requires contracts under 30 years unless approved by the national economic and social policy council, and conditions payments on availability.
   - Mexico's PPP Law (2012) caps terms at 40 years including extensions.
   [confidence: high that the Guide states these; medium that each is still current] [source: 1]

**B. IMF–World Bank PFRAM (international; as of 2026-10-03)**

7. The PPP Fiscal Risk Assessment Model (PFRAM) is an analytical tool developed by the IMF and the World Bank Group to assess the fiscal costs and risks of PPP projects. It was launched in April 2016 and has been used in IMF and World Bank technical assistance and by country PPP units, ministries of finance and public corporations. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
8. PFRAM also estimates the potential fiscal impact of PPP contingent liabilities, specifically debt guarantees and minimum revenue guarantees, under a worst-case scenario. The aim is that existing PPPs' fiscal impacts are understood and new PPPs are awarded only if they do not undermine long-term fiscal sustainability. [confidence: high, paraphrase] [source: 5]
9. A PFRAM Guideline / User Manual dated 2019 is cited as "PFRAM ... V.2". [confidence: high that a 2019 version 2 exists; its contents were not read] [source: 5]

**C. Statistical treatment in the EU: Eurostat/EPEC Guide (EU member states; as of 2026-10-03)**

10. *A Guide to the Statistical Treatment of PPPs* was published in September 2016 by Eurostat and the European PPP Expertise Centre (EPEC, part of the EIB's Advisory Services, created in 2008). It reflects ESA 2010 and the MGDD 2016. If the Guide conflicts with the MGDD, the MGDD prevails. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
11. Eurostat first published PPP rules in February 2004 (Eurostat decision of 11 February 2004). ESA 2010 has been the reference framework since September 2014. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
12. Key principle: the PPP asset is recorded on the balance sheet of its economic owner, the party that bears most of the risks and has the right to most of the rewards. If the risk–reward assessment is inconclusive, control of the asset decides: control of its specification, of the services delivered from it, and of the asset at contract expiry. The assessment is made at financial close under the rules then in force. Later contract changes are assessed under the rules in force at the time of the change. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
13. ESA 2010 is "binary". A PPP asset is wholly government or wholly non-government. When it is on balance sheet, the full asset value and related liabilities are recorded. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
14. Eurostat's PPP definition tests:
    - For projects that refurbish an existing asset, the partner's capital expenditure must be at least 50% of the asset's value after the works.
    - Contracts under 10 years total duration are likely to be questioned.
    - The operational phase should normally cover at least one major maintenance cycle.
    - The partner must at minimum maintain the asset.
    - If government revenues from users are forecast (in NPV at financial close) to exceed 50% of government payments to the partner, the project is on balance sheet.
    [confidence: high] [source: 6]
15. Government financing (all forms, including loans, equity, guarantees and tax exemptions, from any government entity):
    - 50% or more of construction capex: automatically on balance sheet.
    - More than one third but under 50%: issue of very high importance.
    - More than 10% up to one third: high importance.
    - 10% or less: moderate importance.
    EU funds under intergovernmental agreements are excluded. EIB financing counts as private. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
16. Refinancing gains: the contract may give the authority a share of gains from refinancing in one of two ways. Either the share depends on gains arising from the authority's specific actions, or a fixed share of no more than one third applies to any gain. A share of more than one third, or any hybrid or alternative approach, automatically puts the PPP on balance sheet. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
17. Eurostat's view is that any minimum use or minimum revenue guarantee influences the statistical treatment (Theme 4.10.3 and Theme 14.5 of the Guide). [confidence: high that such guarantees are treated as relevant; the importance rating is in the Guide's annex] [source: 6]
18. The Guide warns that an excessive focus on off-balance-sheet recording can come at the expense of sound project preparation and value for money. It says PPPs can create an "affordability illusion" by deferring and spreading public payments, which off-balance-sheet treatment tends to worsen. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

**D. Accounting versus statistics (international; as of 2026-10-03)**

19. IPSAS 32, *Service Concession Arrangements: Grantor* (2011), uses a control approach. PPP assets are included in government accounts if the public partner controls them: that is, if it can determine how the assets are used, who can use them, and how much is paid for their use or availability, regardless of legal ownership. The approach is similar to IFRIC 12 (Service Concession Arrangements). Under it, government debt and deficit indicators rise as the PPP asset is constructed, much as with a publicly financed project. [confidence: high, paraphrase of the World Bank's description; IPSAS 32 itself not read] [source: 7]
20. The World Bank Reference Guide notes that Eurostat uses a risks-and-rewards criterion while IPSAS uses control, so ESA and IPSAS outcomes can differ. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 7]
21. UK example: the NAO reported in 2018 that most PFI debt was scored off balance sheet under ESA (which determines government debt), but most PFI debt was on balance sheet under IFRS in departmental accounts and the Whole of Government Accounts. [confidence: high] [source: 8]

**E. United Kingdom (as of 2026-10-03)**

22. NAO, *PFI and PF2* (HC 718, 18 January 2018):
    - There were over 700 operational PFI and PF2 deals, with a capital value of around GBP 60 billion.
    - Annual charges were GBP 10.3 billion in 2016-17.
    - Future charges until the 2040s totalled GBP 199 billion.
    [confidence: high] [source: 8, 9]
23. NAO findings on cost:
    - The 2010 National Infrastructure Plan estimated an indicative cost of capital for PFI of 2% to 3.75% above gilts.
    - IPA data on deals since 2013 showed forecast investor returns of 2% to 4% above government borrowing.
    - Some 2013 deals projected investor returns over 8%.
    - The cumulative cash cost of a group of PF2 schools was around 40% higher than financing by government borrowing.
    [confidence: high] [source: 8]
24. PF2 was planned with 20%–25% equity, but this was not implemented. As at September 2017 there were six PF2 projects (five education, one health), all financed with around 10% equity and 90% debt, the same as PFI. No pension funds had invested in PF2 debt. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
25. In the Budget speech of 29 October 2018, Chancellor Philip Hammond said he had never signed off a PFI contract and never would. He announced that the government would abolish the use of PFI and PF2 for future projects, and that a centre of excellence would actively manage existing contracts, starting in the health sector. [confidence: high, paraphrase of the speech] [source: 10]
26. NAO published *Lessons learned: private finance for infrastructure* on 25 March 2025. It draws on over 140 prior NAO publications and sets out 12 considerations under three themes: conditions for investor and public confidence, sound policy and project-level decisions, and effective commercial strategies. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
27. On 24 November 2025, ahead of the Autumn Budget 2025, the government announced that new Neighbourhood Health Centres would be delivered through a combination of public-private partnerships and public investment. Of 250 planned centres, more than 100 would open by 2030. It said the new PPP model would learn lessons from past and current models so taxpayers get value for money. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
28. The DHSC *10 Year Capital Plan for Health and Social Care* (published 8 July 2026) says the new PPP model is being developed by NISTA, supported by DHSC, and draws on lessons including the NAO's 2025 report. It names three design features: a simpler and more transparent payment mechanism, greater flexibility to accommodate service change over time, and stronger oversight of asset condition and maintenance, including digital tools. It also says public capital remains the foundation of health infrastructure investment. [confidence: high] [source: 13]

## Financing and structure details (parties, tranches, amounts, tenors, guarantees, where verified)

- **Typical UK PFI/PF2 financing.** Around 90% debt and 10% equity (NAO 2018). Forecast investor returns were 2%–4% over gilts for post-2013 deals, and above 8% for some 2013 deals (facts 23–24).
- **Design levers that move statistical treatment (Eurostat/EPEC).** Share of government financing (50% / one-third / 10% thresholds), refinancing gain sharing (one-third cap), minimum revenue or use guarantees, termination compensation that would pay out senior debt regardless of performance, and allocation of the asset at expiry (facts 12–17).

## What went wrong or right, and why (well-established analysis only, attributed to named sources)

- **NAO (2018).** Private finance has a visibly higher cost of finance. The planned PF2 equity reforms were not delivered. Most PFI debt was off the ESA balance sheet but on the IFRS accounts. The NAO said it did not seek to form a view on the model or individual projects. [source: 8, 9]
- **Eurostat/EPEC (2016).** Chasing off-balance-sheet treatment can drive PPPs where they are not appropriate. PPPs can create an "affordability illusion". [source: 6]
- **World Bank Reference Guide (2017).** Risk transfer is a means, not an end. Failures often trace to deviations from the defining characteristics of a PPP. [source: 1]
- **UK government (2025–2026).** The new Neighbourhood Health Centre PPP model is framed as learning from past models, with simpler payment mechanisms, flexibility for service change and stronger asset-condition oversight. These address weaknesses commonly attributed to PFI. [source: 12, 13]

## Teaching angles by chapter (for each listed chapter: the transferable lesson and the angle to take, 2–4 sentences)

- **Chapter 57 (The public-sector case for PPPs).** Separate three questions that are often blurred: value for money, affordability, and fiscal/statistical treatment. Use the Eurostat/EPEC bright lines (50%/one-third thresholds, refinancing gain cap) and the ESA-versus-IPSAS gap (UK PFI: off ESA, on IFRS) to show how accounting rules can drive procurement choices. Then use the NAO numbers (GBP 199 billion of future charges; 2%–3.75% cost-of-capital premium) and PFRAM's contingent-liability lens to test whether a PPP is a policy choice or a fiscal illusion. Close with the UK arc from PFI to abolition (2018) to a new PPP model (2025–2026) for Case T's procurement decision.
- **Chapter 58 (Procuring and designing PPPs).** Treat the Eurostat/EPEC Guide's 16 contract "themes" (site, construction, payment mechanism, change, termination compensation, refinancing, government influence and so on) as a checklist. Each design choice has commercial and statistical consequences. Show students how refinancing gain sharing, minimum revenue guarantees and termination compensation formulas are drafted with the statistical rules in mind. Point readers to the World Bank's 2019 *Guidance on PPP Contractual Provisions* for standard clause language, and use the new UK NHC model's features as a modern "lessons learned" design brief.

## Do not state (claims commonly repeated but unverified or wrong, and uncertain details)

- Do not say there is a PPP Reference Guide "Version 4" or a later edition of the 2019 *Guidance on PPP Contractual Provisions*. None was verified; ppp.worldbank.org could not be accessed for this sheet.
- Do not describe PFRAM's internal balance-sheet tests, the content of its version 2, or any later version without reading the IMF/World Bank manual.
- Do not give the current MGDD edition. The Eurostat/EPEC Guide is based on MGDD 2016, and later MGDD editions exist but were not checked. Tell readers to check the latest MGDD chapter on PPPs.
- Do not describe the 2004 Eurostat rule as "construction risk plus either availability or demand risk" from this sheet's sources. The rule is widely cited, but its wording was not read here.
- Do not give PF2's launch date (commonly given as December 2012) as verified from this sheet's sources.
- Do not give the number of PFI contracts still operating in 2026, the UK's current total of outstanding unitary charges, or PFI expiry and handback statistics. These were not verified here (HM Treasury publishes annual PFI/PF2 summary data on gov.uk).

- Do not treat the national thresholds in fact 6 as current law without checking (for example, Brazil's minimum project value is reported to have been lowered after 2004).
