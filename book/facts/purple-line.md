# Maryland Purple Line: construction dispute and contractor exit

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: MTA Purple Line newsletter, July 2026, reporting the project more than 90% complete with trial running planned for 2027)

## Summary

The Purple Line is a 16.2-mile, 21-station light rail line in the Maryland suburbs of Washington, D.C., running from Bethesda to New Carrollton. In 2016 the Maryland Department of Transportation and the Maryland Transit Administration (MDOT MTA) awarded it as a 36-year design-build-finance-operate-maintain availability-payment P3 to Purple Line Transit Partners (PLTP). PLTP's members were Meridiam, Fluor and Star America. The P3 agreement was worth about $5.6 billion over its term. PLTP passed its design and construction obligations back-to-back to Purple Line Transit Constructors (PLTC), a Fluor-led joint venture. A federal court vacated the project's environmental approval in August 2016, which delayed the start by about a year, and the project ran into further delays and disputed claims. Both the P3 agreement and the design-build contract let the private party terminate if qualifying delays reached 365 days. PLTC gave notice in May 2020, and PLTP terminated the P3 agreement in June 2020. MDOT MTA lost its bid in the Baltimore City Circuit Court to block the exit, took over the key contracts in September 2020, and settled in December 2020. Under the settlement the state paid $250 million, Fluor left entirely, and PLTP stayed on as concessionaire and procured a new contractor. In January 2022 the Board of Public Works approved Maryland Transit Solutions (Dragados USA and OHLA USA) and a restructured P3 worth $9.3 billion. Further modifications in 2023 and 2024 took it to about $9.8 billion, with revenue service expected in winter 2027, more than five years after the original March 2022 date.

## Verified facts

1. The Purple Line is a new 16.2-mile light rail line linking Bethesda and Silver Spring (Montgomery County) with College Park/University of Maryland and New Carrollton (Prince George's County). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. In spring 2016 MDOT MTA selected PLTP to design, build, finance, operate and maintain the line. The concession was 36 years: a 6-year construction period followed by 30 years of operations and maintenance. During construction PLTP was paid through construction progress and milestone payments. On the start of revenue operations it would receive a revenue service availability payment, followed by semi-annual availability payments (APs) during operations. Revenue service was expected in March 2022. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
3. The original P3 agreement (2016–2052) totaled $5,590.0 million. The components were design-build $1,971.9 million, operations and maintenance $1,744.3 million, insurance $272.6 million, capital renewal $289.1 million and financing $1,312.0 million. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. The Board of Public Works approved the P3 agreement on 6 April 2016. [confidence: medium; reported by The Washington Post and others, not checked against BPW minutes] [source: 8]
5. The planning, design, construction and equipping phase had a projected cost of $2.4 billion. Funding sources were $900 million of FTA New Starts funds, $36 million of FTA Section 5307 funds, about $440–450 million of state and local funds, and about $1.0 billion of PLTP financing (private equity plus borrowings, including a federal TIFIA loan). [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4]
6. In August 2016 the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia vacated the project's record of decision. The D.C. Circuit reinstated it in August 2017. The FTA signed a Full Funding Grant Agreement for $900 million on 22 August 2017, based on a $2.4 billion FFGA project cost. DLS records the construction start as 28 August 2017. MDOT MTA granted part of the time extension PLTC sought for the vacatur but denied other time and compensation claims. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4]
7. PLTP financed its share with $313 million of private activity bonds (PABs), a TIFIA loan of $859 million (per DLS, undrawn by early 2022) and about $140 million of equity. [confidence: high for the $313 million PABs and $140 million equity; medium for the TIFIA amount, which DLS gives as $859 million while other public sources cite about $875 million] [source: 3]
8. Fluor held a 15% membership interest in PLTP and a 50% interest in PLTC, the design-build contractor. PLTC performed PLTP's design and construction obligations under an Amended and Restated Design-Build Contract on a back-to-back basis. Lane Construction and Traylor Bros. were Fluor's partners in the construction joint venture. [confidence: high for the Fluor interests; medium for the naming of Lane and Traylor, from trade press] [source: 5, 6]
9. The P3 agreement gave PLTP an unconditional right to terminate if certain events delayed design and construction by 365 days or more. The design-build contract gave PLTC a matching right. Fluor stated that the project had been delayed by more than two and a half years by events outside PLTP's or PLTC's control. [confidence: high as to Fluor's description of the contract; the characterization of fault is Fluor's] [source: 5]
10. PLTC gave PLTP notice of its intent to terminate the design-build contract on 1 May 2020. PLTP exercised its right to terminate the P3 agreement on 23 June 2020. MDOT MTA disputed the termination and sued PLTP in Maryland state court for breach. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 5]
11. Disputed claims at the time of the exit were reported at about $755–800 million. [confidence: medium; press figures] [source: 6, 7]
12. On 10 September 2020, Judge Jeffrey M. Geller of the Baltimore City Circuit Court ruled that the contract gave a clear right to terminate once delays exceeded a year, which let the consortium leave. MDOT MTA took key contracts and subcontracts under an assignment and assumption agreement dated 28 September 2020. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 6]
13. On 16 December 2020 the Board of Public Works approved a settlement agreement between MDOT MTA and PLTP. Its terms:
    - MDOT MTA paid a $250 million settlement payment in two installments: $100 million on 28 December 2020, and $150 million on the earlier of new-financing close or 12 months.
    - Fluor and its affiliates left the project completely.
    - PLTP stayed as concessionaire and was to select a replacement design-build contractor within nine months.
    - MDOT MTA managed the works during the interim, with costs shared 50/50 and PLTP's share capped at $50 million.
    - The project would get new financing, with the TIFIA loan restructured.
    - The P3 agreement would terminate if a replacement contractor or new financing was not secured within the stated period.

    [confidence: high] [source: 2]
14. Fluor's account of the settlement: Fluor transferred its 15% PLTP interest and sold its 50% interest in the operations and maintenance entity to the remaining partners. PLTC received an initial $116 million from PLTP in December 2020 and was contractually owed a further $150 million by December 2021. The lawsuit was dismissed with prejudice. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
15. MDOT MTA also provided funds to retire the $313 million of PABs. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
16. PLTP began procuring a replacement contractor in January 2021, received two proposals in October 2021 and, with MTA's concurrence, selected Maryland Transit Solutions (MTS), a joint venture of Dragados USA, Inc. and OHLA USA, Inc. The Board of Public Works approved the new design-build contract and the P3 amendments on 26 January 2022. Its approval was required by the settlement even though the state is not a party to the design-build contract. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
17. The revised P3 agreement (2016–2056) totaled $9,284.0 million, up $3,694.0 million. The main changes:
    - design-build rose to $3,435.8 million (+$1,464.0 million);
    - financing rose to $2,765.8 million (+$1,453.7 million);
    - the $250 million settlement payment was added;
    - $218.7 million of work done by MTA since September 2020 was added;
    - operations and maintenance rose to $1,977.2 million.

    The term grew to just over 40 years to keep 30 years of operations after a revenue start then projected for fall 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
18. MDOT cited six drivers of the increase: the delay from the later-overturned federal court action, COVID-19, living-wage laws, a harder insurance market, five years of inflation, and bringing in a new contractor mid-construction. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
19. The planned refinancing at the 2022 close comprised a TIFIA loan restructured or replaced, increasing to about $1.7 billion, new PABs of about $700 million, and PLTP equity doubled from $140 million to $280 million as required by the settlement. APs were expected to average $250 million a year over 30 years, with a $100 million milestone payment at revenue service. The state took interest-rate risk until financial close and carried a $192 million rate contingency. [confidence: high as to the plan; final closing amounts not verified] [source: 3]
20. In the 2022 amendment the state took back several risks:
    - unknown defects in work completed to date;
    - certain owner-performed design;
    - utility adjustments;
    - MTA-provided approvals;
    - expanded pandemic relief;
    - state-procured materials.

    [confidence: high] [source: 3]
21. On 19 July 2023 the Board of Public Works approved a modification for utility-relocation delay that added a net $148.3 million and moved revenue service availability from fall 2026 to spring 2027 (207 days). In March 2024 it approved a further net $415.0 million as a final settlement of open PLTP claims. That took the agreement to about $9.8 billion and moved revenue service to winter 2027, with payments contingent on milestones. [confidence: high] [source: 9, 10]
22. The project was 74.2% complete at 30 November 2024. MTA's July 2026 newsletter reported it as more than 90% complete, with live-wire light rail vehicle testing reaching Bethesda and trial running planned for 2027. [confidence: high] [source: 10, 11]

## Financing and structure details

- **Parties:**
  - Owner: MDOT MTA.
  - Concessionaire: PLTP, originally Meridiam, Fluor (15%) and Star America; Meridiam and Star America after December 2020.
  - Original design-builder: PLTC (Fluor 50%, with Lane and Traylor).
  - Replacement design-builder: MTS (Dragados USA and OHLA USA).
  - Light rail vehicles: CAF.
  - Operator: Purple Line Transit Operators.
- **Payment mechanism:** state-funded progress and milestone payments during construction, followed by availability payments for 30 years. DLS noted that the upfront construction progress payments had been fully paid before the 2022 restructuring, so PLTP financed all remaining construction [3].
- **Original capital stack (2016):** $900m FFGA; $36m Section 5307; about $440–450m state and local funds; about $1.0bn private ($313m PABs, TIFIA, about $140m equity) [2, 3, 4].
- **Restructured stack (planned 2022):** TIFIA about $1.7bn; new PABs about $700m; equity $280m. Original PABs retired with state funds [3].
- **Cost growth of the P3 agreement:** $5,590m (2016) → $9,284m (Jan 2022) → about $9.8bn (Mar 2024) [3, 10].

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **A hard, early, unconditional exit right.** Both contracts let the private parties walk away after 365 days of qualifying delay. Fluor called the right "unconditional" [5], and the court read it as "clear, direct and absolute" [6]. Once an external delay (the 2016 vacatur) used up much of that cushion, PLTC gained a credible exit option and with it leverage over disputed claims.
- **Back-to-back pass-through and contractor leverage.** PLTP's obligations flowed down to PLTC, and PLTC's termination notice triggered PLTP's own termination of the P3 agreement. The equity holders (Meridiam and Star America) stayed in and rebuilt the project. The construction party left.
- **Public sector re-absorbed risk to finish.** According to DLS, the 2022 amendment shifted latent defects, utilities and other risks back to the state, and financing costs roughly doubled [3]. This illustrates what replacing a contractor mid-stream costs.
- **Restructuring rather than retendering the whole P3.** Keeping the concessionaire meant the project avoided procuring a new P3 from scratch. Instead the concessionaire retendered only the design-build contract under state oversight. Yet the new contractor again attracted only two proposals [3].
- No authoritative published analysis was located that apportions fault for the delays. Present MDOT's stated cost drivers [3] and Fluor's position [5] as the parties' own views.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 58 (Procuring and designing PPPs):** Use the 365-day extended-delay termination right as a drafting lesson. Ask what triggers it, whether owner-caused and third-party (court) delays count, what compensation follows, and whether a cure or negotiation period sits in front of it. Pair this with the settlement's design: a time-limited replacement-contractor procurement, interim cost sharing capped at $50 million, a refinancing condition and a fallback termination. That gives a template for restructuring rather than terminating a troubled P3.
- **Ch 61 (Construction to completion):** This is a contractor exit without insolvency. Fluor was solvent, and it left by exercising a contract right. Trace what happened next: assignment of subcontracts to the owner, the owner managing the works during the gap, latent-defect risk on part-completed work (the new contractor would not take it), and roughly 18 months before full-scale work restarted. Contrast with Carillion, where the contractor failed outright.
- **Ch 80 (Rail, urban transit, airports and ports):** Urban light rail in a dense corridor carries heavy utility-relocation, third-party and permitting risk. The 2023 modification was driven by utility relocation [9]. Show how the availability-payment model kept ridership risk with the state while construction interfaces still drove most of the cost growth from $5.6 billion to about $9.8 billion.

## Do not state

- Do not state a single "total project cost" without saying what it covers. The $5.6 billion and $9.3–9.8 billion figures are P3 contract values over the term, including O&M and financing. Construction-phase cost is about $2.0 billion originally and about $3.4 billion after the 2022 restructuring.
- Do not use Wikipedia-reported figures of "$425 million" and "$9.53 billion" for the March 2024 change. DLS gives a net $415.0 million and a revised total of about $9.8 billion.
- Do not give actual 2022 financial-close amounts (final TIFIA size, PAB size, rates) as fact. Only the planned amounts in DLS are verified here.
- Do not say Maryland "paid Fluor $250 million". The $250 million settlement payment went from MDOT MTA to PLTP. Fluor reports PLTC receiving $116 million plus $150 million owed from PLTP.
- Do not state that the 2016 PABs were exactly $313 million issued by a named conduit, or name the issuer, without a primary source. DLS confirms only "$313 million in PABs."
- Do not give a firm opening date. Official sources say winter 2027 and trial running in 2027.
- Do not attribute the delay mainly to either side as fact. Neither the court nor any audit reviewed here has apportioned fault.
- Do not state the comptroller's March 2025 finding on permitting and legal costs (Wikipedia: about $800 million against $6 million budgeted). Not verified against the report.
