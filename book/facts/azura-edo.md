# Azura-Edo IPP (Nigeria): World Bank partial risk guarantee, payment security

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Azura's October 2024 notice that it would draw on the NBET letter of credit unless arrears were paid by 25 October 2024)

## Summary

Azura-Edo is a gas-fired open-cycle power plant of about 450-461 MW (sources differ on the figure) on the north-eastern outskirts of Benin City, Edo State, Nigeria. It sells all of its output to the state-owned Nigerian Bulk Electricity Trading Plc (NBET) under a 20-year power purchase agreement (PPA) signed on 22 April 2013. Clifford Chance, counsel on the deal, described it as Nigeria's first fully privately financed independent power project (IPP). It was also the first Nigerian generation project to receive both a World Bank (IBRD) partial risk guarantee (PRG) and Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA) cover. The credit support had four layers:

- a revolving letter of credit (LC) issued by JP Morgan on behalf of NBET, backed by an IBRD liquidity PRG capped at USD 120 million;
- an IBRD debt-mobilisation PRG on USD 117 million of commercial loans;
- MIGA political risk insurance for commercial lenders, hedge providers and equity;
- a put and call option agreement (PCOA), under which the Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN) must buy the plant or shares for at least the outstanding debt if the PPA is terminated early.

Financial close came on 28 December 2015, more than a year after the loan agreements were signed in November 2014. The contractor mobilised on 4 January 2016. Construction finished on 1 May 2018, eight months ahead of the 36-month schedule. Commercial operation began on 24 May 2018.

The plant has run well, but naira receivables, scarce dollars and NBET arrears have repeatedly strained it. In February 2023, payments to lenders were reported overdue and lenders reportedly claimed under MIGA. In October 2024 Azura gave notice that it would draw on the NBET LC.

## Verified facts

1. The project company is Azura Power West Africa Limited (APWAL), incorporated in Nigeria in 2010. It is owned 97.5% by Azura-Edo Limited, a Mauritius holding vehicle, and 2.5% by the Edo State Government. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. The sponsors were Amaya Capital (lead sponsor), American Capital Energy & Infrastructure, African Infrastructure Investment Fund 2 (AIIF2, managed by a Macquarie/Old Mutual joint venture), Aldwych International, and Asset & Resource Management Company (ARM). The Edo State Government also held equity. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 4]
3. The plant uses three Siemens heavy-duty E-class open-cycle gas turbines. It connects to the Benin North substation by a 330 kV line of about 1 km, and to the Escravos-Lagos Pipeline System by a gas spur of about 1 km. The site was designed for later conversion to combined cycle. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 8]
4. Capacity figures differ:
   - World Bank and OPIC: 459 MW;
   - Clifford Chance and the 2014 press release: 450 MW;
   - Africa50 and Azura: 461 MW.

   [confidence: high that the figures differ; medium on which is net or gross]. [source: 1, 3, 8, 11]
5. The PPA with NBET was signed on 22 April 2013 and amended on 1 December 2014 and 25 November 2015. It runs 20 years from commercial operation and ends on 23 May 2032. The tariff has a capacity charge and an energy charge. Clifford Chance says the capacity payment is due while the plant is available or deemed available, and it covers scheduled debt service among other things. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
6. Gas risk is allocated in two ways under the PPA:
   - a failure by the gas supplier is not an availability event, so capacity payments stop, but a prolonged failure allows termination and lets the project exercise its put under the PCOA;
   - a failure caused by the public gas transportation system is treated as a government risk, and capacity payments continue.

   [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. The PCOA among APWAL, Azura-Edo Limited, the FGN and NBET was executed on 22 October 2014. On early PPA termination in nearly all circumstances, the project can "put" the plant or its shares to the government. The government must then pay a purchase price that at minimum covers outstanding debt. Clifford Chance says the FGN chose this structure over a plain guarantee of the termination sum because it always receives an asset in return for its payment. The firm describes the PCOA as becoming the template for later Nigerian IPPs. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
8. The project contracts are as follows: [confidence: high] [source: 3]
   - gas sales and purchase agreement (GSPA) with Seplat and NPDC, signed on 14 April 2014 for 15 years with a five-year renewal option, on take-or-pay terms and secured by a USD 55 million LC;
   - gas transportation agreement with the Nigerian Gas Company, signed on 22 July 2014;
   - fully wrapped turnkey EPC contract with Siemens AG, Siemens Nigeria and Julius Berger, signed on 29 April 2014;
   - O&M agreement with PIC Group (a Marubeni subsidiary), signed on 5 May 2014;
   - Siemens long-term maintenance contract, signed on 24 July 2014;
   - grid connection agreement with the Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN), signed on 24 June 2014.
9. Unusually, the GSPA LC was provided by a senior lender, Standard Chartered, as part of the financing rather than collateralised by equity. This required a bespoke intercreditor and accounts structure. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. The World Bank's Project Appraisal Document (PAD, Report 82664-NG, 14 April 2014) proposed IBRD PRGs for Azura of up to USD 245 million: an NBET credit-enhancement (LC) guarantee of up to USD 120 million and a commercial debt mobilisation guarantee of up to USD 125 million. These formed part of a Power Sector Guarantees Project series of up to USD 395 million that also covered Qua Iboe. The final maturity was up to 20 years from effectiveness of each PRG. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

    - if NBET does not repay, the LC bank claims on IBRD;
    - IBRD then recovers from the FGN under the Indemnity Agreement.

## Financing and structure details

- **Cost and gearing.** Appraisal cost was USD 813 million at 72.5:27.5 debt to equity (World Bank). Later public figures are "approximately USD 900 million" (MIGA) and USD 860 million (OPIC summary).
- **Senior offshore (USD 501.5 million).** This was split into an IBRD-covered commercial tranche (USD 117 million guaranteed), MIGA-covered commercial tranches, and DFI direct loans. The DFI loans included IFC (A loan of up to USD 125 million proposed) and OPIC (up to USD 35 million senior, plus USD 15 million junior, 15-year tenors, as proposed). The USD 55 million GSPA LC facility from Standard Chartered sits inside this amount.
- **Senior onshore.** NGN 24 billion from CBN/BOI-PAIF through FCMB at a 7% fixed rate. BOI's step-in right is made subject to the intercreditor agreement.
- **Mezzanine.** USD 65 million from IFC, Proparco, EAIF and OPIC, with entrenched DFI policy rights agreed with the senior lenders.
- **Equity.** Approximately USD 190 million, largely as shareholder loans.
- **Revenue security stack.** The layers run as follows:
  1. NBET pays under the PPA.
  2. The JP Morgan LC is available for undisputed unpaid amounts.
  3. The IBRD liquidity PRG (USD 120 million) backs the LC bank, and the FGN must reimburse IBRD under the Indemnity Agreement.
  4. On termination, the PCOA purchase price is at least the outstanding debt.
  5. The IBRD debt-mobilisation PRG (USD 117 million) and MIGA breach-of-contract cover protect lenders if the FGN fails to pay under the PCOA.
  6. MIGA transfer-and-convertibility (T&C), expropriation and war cover protect lenders and equity.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right: construction and operations.** Building finished eight months early, and availability was reported above 97% (company retrospective, 2019).
- **Right: risk allocation templates.** Clifford Chance (2016) credits the PCOA design, under which government pays and receives the asset, and the split of gas-supply from gas-transport risk with making the deal bankable. It describes these as templates for later Nigerian IPPs. The World Bank PAD (2014) set out the principle of complementary, non-duplicative IBRD and MIGA cover so that each risk is covered once.
- **Problem: payment and currency.** Contracts cannot create dollars. The African Catalyst case study (2023), drawing on press reports, says repayment delays came from difficulty sourcing foreign exchange rather than from cash flow or profitability. BusinessDay (2020, 2023) attributes the near-defaults to CBN dollar rationing. The take-or-pay PPA has also drawn political criticism because capacity is paid when TCN cannot evacuate power, with scrutiny reported by the Guardian (2022) and the House of Representatives.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 34 (blended, concessional, local-currency finance).** This is the canonical worked example of a World Bank PRG used two ways at once: a liquidity guarantee behind an offtaker LC, and a debt-mobilisation guarantee behind a commercial tranche. Show how USD 237 million of IBRD cover, MIGA cover and a concessional naira tranche from a state intervention fund (PAIF at 7%) together mobilised an USD 800-900 million deal. Contrast the 5.25% margin on covered loans with the 12.25% DFI mezzanine margin.
- **Ch 59 (country, currency and payment risk).** Use Azura to separate three risks that are often blurred. Offtaker payment risk is covered by the LC and PRG. Termination risk is covered by the PCOA and the IBRD/MIGA cover. Convertibility and transfer risk is the hardest of the three in practice. Walk through the LC drawing, the reinstatement and the IBRD backstop. Then ask why a fully guaranteed structure still came close to default in 2020 and 2023: naira revenues and dollar debt, and CBN FX allocation.

## Do not state

- That Azura's World Bank guarantees totalled "USD 327 million". GTR's headline says so, but its own components sum to USD 237 million.
- That total debt was USD 687 million plus a further USD 120 million PAIF tranche, or that total exposure was USD 997 million. Clifford Chance treats the NGN 24 billion PAIF tranche as part of the approximately USD 687 million; the African Catalyst table appears to double count it.
- That COD was December 2017. The MIGA case study says so, but the company's audited accounts give 24 May 2018.
- That MIGA paid a claim, or that the IBRD guarantee or the LC was ever actually called, or the outcome of the October 2024 demand. None of this is verified.
- Exact PPA tariff levels, the precise MIGA split between debt and equity, the LC size in months, or any statement that the "sovereign guarantee" was itself a loan (TheCable's wording is loose).
- Africa50's stake size or entry date. Africa50 confirms an equity investment but no details were verified.
