# PURPA 1978, qualifying facilities and the rise of US independent power producers in the 1980s

As of: 2026-10-03 (history covered to the Energy Policy Act of 1992 and EIA data for 1995; the current statutory text is cited as in force, including the 2005 amendment, section 210(m))

## Summary

The Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978 (PURPA, Pub. L. 95-617, enacted 9 November 1978) was part of the National Energy Act passed after the 1970s oil shocks. Section 210 required electric utilities to interconnect with "qualifying facilities" (QFs) and to buy their power. QFs were either cogenerators, which produce electricity and useful heat in sequence from one fuel, or small power producers using renewable, waste or geothermal energy. Purchase rates were based on the utility's "avoided cost" and capped at the utility's incremental cost of alternative energy. Congress also exempted most QFs from:
- rate and accounting regulation by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) under the Federal Power Act;
- regulation by the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) under the Public Utility Holding Company Act (PUHCA);
- state utility rate, financial and organisational regulation.

That combination gave a non-utility developer a guaranteed buyer and a price set by the utility's costs rather than its own. In practice this was a long-term offtake contract with a creditworthy utility, which is the anchor that limited-recourse power project finance needs. The Energy Information Administration (EIA) records the result:
- non-utility capacity was flat at about 19 GW through the 1970s, then grew strongly in the late 1980s;
- non-utility generation rose from 98 billion kWh in 1986 to 275 billion kWh in 1991;
- in 1990 and 1991 non-utilities added more net capacity than utilities did;
- by 1991 non-utilities owned about 6% of US generating capacity and produced about 9% of generation.

The IFC's 1999 history describes PURPA as "the foundation on which non-recourse lending could take place in the power industry". It lists Coso geothermal and Indiantown cogeneration as landmark US power project financings of the early 1990s. The Supreme Court upheld PURPA (FERC v. Mississippi, 1982) and FERC's implementing rules (American Paper Institute v. AEP, 1983). Administratively set avoided costs sometimes forced utilities to buy unneeded, expensive power. States therefore moved to competitive bidding, starting with Central Maine Power in 1984. The Energy Policy Act of 1992 then created "exempt wholesale generators", which did not need QF status.

## Verified facts

1. **Enactment.** PURPA is Pub. L. 95-617, enacted 9 November 1978 (92 Stat. 3117). Section 210 is codified at 16 U.S.C. §824a-3. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. **Price cap in the statute.** Rates for utility purchases from QFs must be "just and reasonable to the electric consumers of the electric utility and in the public interest" and must not discriminate against QFs. No rule may provide for "a rate which exceeds the incremental cost to the electric utility of alternative electric energy". [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. **Core mechanics, as summarised by EIA.**
   - PURPA required utilities to interconnect with and purchase power from any facility meeting QF criteria, and to pay "the utility's own incremental or avoided cost of production".
   - EIA says this "created, by fiat, a market in which QFs could, unilaterally, sell electricity to utilities".
   - QF prices were not cost-of-service based; they reflected the purchasing utility's avoided cost.

   [confidence: high] [source: 2]
4. **Regulatory exemptions.** Congress exempted most QFs from FERC rate and accounting regulation under the Federal Power Act, from SEC regulation under PUHCA, and from state rate, financial and organisational regulation of utilities. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
5. **QF types and ownership limit.**
   - QFs are either cogenerators (sequential production of electricity and another useful form of energy, such as heat or steam, from the same fuel) or small power producers (renewable, waste or geothermal sources).
   - For a small power producer, renewable sources must provide at least 75% of total energy input.
   - Both types of QF were limited to no more than 50% equity ownership by an electric utility (EIA, 1993).

   [confidence: high] [source: 2]
6. **Size limit.** Under the Federal Power Act definition, a "small power production facility" must have capacity, together with other facilities at the same site, of not more than 80 megawatts. Eligible solar, wind, waste or geothermal facilities are treated separately. EIA notes that 1990 amendments removed the original size criterion for all sources except hydroelectric. [confidence: high] [sources: 3, 2]
7. **Pre-PURPA baseline.** Throughout the 1970s non-utility capacity was approximately 19 GW, mostly industrial cogeneration for own use, and changed very little between 1970 and 1979. Utility capacity grew from 342 GW (1970) to 598 GW (1979). The utility share of US generation peaked at 97% in 1979. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
8. **Growth after PURPA:**
   - non-utility generation rose from 98 billion kWh (1986) to 275 billion kWh (1991), about 19% a year;
   - non-utility sales to utilities rose from 28 billion kWh (1985) to 137 billion kWh (1991), about 30% a year;
   - in 1990 and 1991 non-utilities provided more than half of net capacity additions;
   - by 1991 the utility share of generation had fallen to 91%;
   - non-utilities owned about 6% of capacity and produced about 9% of generation.

   [confidence: high] [source: 2]
9. **Composition in 1991.** Total non-utility capacity was 48.2 GW:
   - cogenerator QFs, 59%;
   - small power producer QFs, 15%;
   - other QFs, 1%;
   - non-QF cogenerators, 14%;
   - other non-utilities (including IPPs that were not QFs), 11%.

   Non-utilities accounted for about one-fifth of all additions to generating capacity in the 1980s. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
10. **Size by 1995.** Total non-utility nameplate capacity reached 70.3 GW. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
11. **Supreme Court.** EIA's table of major cases lists:
    - FERC v. Mississippi, 456 U.S. 742 (1982), which upheld the constitutionality of PURPA;
    - American Paper Institute v. American Electric Power Service Corp., 461 U.S. 402 (1983), which upheld the constitutionality of FERC's cogeneration rules under PURPA.

    [confidence: high] [source: 4]
12. **The avoided-cost problem and bidding.** EIA reports that in some states avoided-cost formulas forced utilities to pay for QF capacity they did not need. In 1984 Central Maine Power and the Maine PSC became the first to use competitive bidding, after the PSC had based QF avoided-cost rates on the cost of a nuclear plant, which drew offers of more power than the utility needed. EIA also gives utilities' estimates of savings from bidding:
    - Boston Edison: an 18% saving in one solicitation;
    - Virginia Power: 5–10% below its own cost;
    - Central Maine Power: 5–12% below avoided cost in its third solicitation.

    [confidence: high] [source: 2]
13. **Tax credits were secondary.** The Energy Tax Act of 1978 added a 10% energy tax credit on top of the existing 10% investment tax credit, for 20% in total. In 1980 some rates rose to 15%. EIA judged the Act "probably was not a major force" in non-utility growth compared with PURPA, though it helped small renewable producers, especially in California. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
14. **EPAct 1992.** The Energy Policy Act of 1992 created "exempt wholesale generators" (EWGs), exempt from PUHCA and not required to meet QF fuel or cogeneration tests. It also gave FERC authority to order transmission access. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
15. **IFC on PURPA's significance.** IFC's 1999 history says PURPA required utilities to buy QF output "under long-term contract at the utility's marginal cost of generating electricity". It says PURPA "thus provided the foundation on which non-recourse lending could take place in the power industry (1978)". The IFC milestones also list:
    - the Coso geothermal project (USD 560 million) as the first project financing arranged in the Rule 144A market and the first project-related financing to achieve investment-grade status (1990–93);
    - Indiantown Cogeneration (USD 505 million) as a publicly registered capital-markets project financing, the first to receive an investment-grade rating during construction (1994–95).

    [confidence: medium for the Coso/Indiantown details, read from a densely laid-out table; high for the PURPA characterisation] [source: 5]
16. **2005 amendment (current law).** Section 210(m), added by the Energy Policy Act of 2005, provides that after 8 August 2005 a utility need not enter a new QF purchase obligation if FERC finds the QF has nondiscriminatory access to competitive wholesale markets, as defined in the statute. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

## Financing and structure details

- **Revenue anchor.** A QF had a statutory right to sell to the utility at avoided cost, plus exemption from utility-style regulation. In practice this made long-term power purchase contracts with investment-grade utilities available, which supported non-recourse debt. The statute requires the purchase obligation and rate principles, not a particular contract length. The "long-term contract" feature is IFC's description of how the market worked. [sources: 1, 2, 5]
- **Ownership.** The 50% cap on utility equity ownership of QFs pushed development toward independent sponsors and industrial hosts. [source: 2]
- **Capital markets.** By the early 1990s, US independent power projects were tapping the Rule 144A and registered bond markets with investment-grade ratings (Coso, Indiantown). [source: 5]
- **Cross-reference.** See hub-power.md (Ch 3 angle) for how the PURPA-style IPP model was exported to emerging markets in the 1990s.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right (EIA, 1993).** EIA concluded that the doubling of the non-utility share of generation between 1985 and 1991 "would not have occurred had PURPA not exempted QFs from PUHCA and guaranteed them a market". [source: 2]
- **Wrong (EIA, 1993).** Administratively set avoided costs could be too high, or blind to whether the utility needed capacity at all, which locked in expensive contracts. States responded with competitive bidding. [source: 2]
- **Context (EIA, 1993, drawing on the Office of Technology Assessment).** Generating costs at large central plants rose dramatically after 1973, which made independent supply attractive to regulators. EIA cites fuel prices, interest rates that "more than tripled", environmental and safety requirements, and construction delays. [source: 2]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 3 (How project finance evolved).** PURPA is the moment when regulation created a contract that lenders could finance. The transferable lesson is that project finance follows bankable revenue: when law gives a stand-alone generator a creditworthy buyer and a predictable price, non-recourse debt appears quickly. The flip side is that a price set by formula rather than by competition can leave consumers holding expensive long-term contracts. That is why procurement moved to competitive bidding and then to merchant and market-based models (EPAct 1992, PURPA 210(m) in 2005). Use the EIA growth numbers to show the scale of the shift.

## Do not state

- Do not state that PURPA itself mandated a specific contract term (for example, "20-year contracts"). The statute sets the purchase obligation and the avoided-cost cap; contract lengths were set under FERC and state implementation.
- Do not state specific California Standard Offer (for example "SO4") contract terms or prices. They were not verified for this sheet.
- Do not give the date or content of FERC's original 1980 implementing order, or of later FERC orders (including the 2020 PURPA rule revisions), as verified. Neither was checked here; the current rules are in 18 CFR Part 292 and on FERC's PURPA pages.
- Do not quote a total count of QFs or of PURPA projects financed, or total project finance volumes for US power in the 1980s. These were not verified.
- Do not name "the first PURPA project financing". It was not established.
