# Cross City Tunnel and Lane Cove Tunnel: traffic forecast failure

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: 26 June 2014, Transurban's purchase of the Cross City Tunnel concession from receivers; Lane Cove Tunnel coverage ends with the 9 August 2010 completion of its sale to Transurban)

## Summary

Sydney's Cross City Tunnel (CCT) and Lane Cove Tunnel (LCT) were user-pay toll-road PPPs procured by the New South Wales Roads and Traffic Authority (RTA) in 2002 and 2003. In both, the private concessionaire took full traffic (patronage) risk, the RTA made no representation about traffic, and the bidder paid the RTA an up-front fee. Both opened ahead of schedule and both were financial failures within three years of opening because traffic came in far below the winning bidder's forecast. The CCT (2.1 km, about AUD 680 million capital cost, opened 28 August 2005) carried fewer than 25,000 vehicles a day at full toll against a bid forecast of about 70,000 at opening and 87,088 by June 2006; receivers were appointed on 27 December 2006, and the asset was sold in 2007 to an ABN AMRO and Leighton consortium, failed again in 2013 and was bought by Transurban in 2014 for about AUD 475 million. The LCT (3.6 km, contract value about AUD 1.1 billion, opened 25 March 2007) went into receivership on 19 January 2010 and was sold to Transurban for about AUD 630 million. The NSW Auditor-General (2006) found that the winning CCT bid's traffic projections were higher than every other projection and should have been scrutinised, and the RTA's own 2010 post-implementation review conceded that the models used were not built for toll forecasting, ignored ramp-up, and over-predicted patronage on both tunnels. The public sector bore no direct financial loss on traffic, but it bore political damage, reversed road changes and, on the LCT, paid AUD 25 million in compensation for its own changes to surface works. The pair is the standard Australian illustration of optimism bias in competitive toll-road bids and of the gap between legal risk transfer and real-world consequences.

## Verified facts

### Cross City Tunnel

1. The RTA invited registrations of interest in September 2000; eight consortia responded and in February 2001 the RTA shortlisted three: CrossCity Motorway (sponsored by Bilfinger Berger AG, Baulderstone Hornibrook and Deutsche Bank AG), E-TUBE (Leighton Contractors and Macquarie Bank), and Sydney City Tunnel Company (Transfield Holdings and Multiplex). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. CrossCity Motorway Pty Ltd (CCM) was announced as preferred proponent in February 2002. The Auditor-General describes its three major shareholders as Bilfinger Berger BOT, Cheung Kong Infrastructure and clients of Deutsche Asset Management. The RTA chose CCM's non-conforming "Long 80" bid: a longer (2.1 km instead of 1.8 km), deeper tunnel allowing 80 km/h. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. The Treasury bidding model let bidders compete on the up-front payment, the concession period or the toll, but proposals were in practice based on a maximum base toll of AUD 2.50 (1999 prices) and a 30-year concession, so the up-front payment became the decisive variable. CCM offered AUD 100.1 million (a AUD 54 million Development Fee plus a AUD 46.1 million "Business Consideration Fee"); the amount actually paid in December 2002 was just under AUD 97 million, mainly because interest rates moved. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The Project Deed was executed in December 2002 (contract term 19 December 2002 to 18 December 2035). Its toll escalation formula was changed to compensate CCM for about AUD 75 million of extra work; a December 2004 First Amendment Deed raised the base toll by 15 cents (to AUD 2.65 main tunnel, 1999 prices) in exchange for a further AUD 35 million of work. The Auditor-General judged that by 2018 the toll would be about 35% higher than it would otherwise have been because of the escalation change and the 15-cent increase. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 4]
5. Capital cost: about AUD 680 million for development, design, construction, fit-out and commissioning (Auditor-General; NSW Treasury's awarded-projects register); the RTA's 2008 contract summary says "more than $700 million". The Auditor-General put the total cost funded by CCM, including financing costs, at just over AUD 1.0 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 4]
6. Under the Project Deed, the private parties accepted "the risks that traffic volumes or project revenues might be less than expected"; the deed records that the RTA made no representations about traffic levels, and the bidders signed a Deed of Disclaimer (18 December 2002) confirming they had not relied on RTA information. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. On RTA termination for an unremedied event of default, the RTA "will not be liable to pay any compensation or other money" to the private trustee, apart from damages for its own breach; the lenders' Security Trustee has step-in and cure rights before termination. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
8. Traffic forecasts. CCM's Long 80 bid projected 87,088 vehicles a day by June 2006 and 99,967 by June 2016; the RTA's revised public sector comparator projections were 82,347 and 91,628. CCM assumed 80% of the 2006 figure on opening, which the Auditor-General calculates as about 70,000 vehicles a day. For the original conforming bids, CCM's 2006 main-tunnel projection was 6% above the RTA benchmark and 10% and 54% above the two rival bidders; for the exit tunnel at Sir John Young Crescent, 40% above the benchmark. Two of the three private bidders considered the project not viable without significant government funding. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. The tunnel opened on 28 August 2005, about two months ahead of schedule. Opening tolls were AUD 3.56 (main tunnel) and AUD 1.68 (exit). Traffic in the first weeks was fewer than 20,000 vehicles a day; at full toll it averaged fewer than 25,000 a day; during the half-price period from March 2006 it averaged about 34,000. The Auditor-General said this was less than a third of CCM's forecast and of the RTA's own prediction, and that the shortfall was not an ordinary ramp-up but "a pattern of resistance" (paraphrase: the reluctance extended well beyond unfamiliarity, since even toll-free and half-price periods did not lift use near forecast). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 5]
10. In October 2005 CCM announced a toll-free period; in March 2006 it halved tolls for an indefinite period, and the government reversed some bus-lane road changes. Surface road changes (lane closures and restrictions on William Street and nearby) caused strong public resentment; the Auditor-General found the road changes were not designed as a "tunnel funnel" but could not say they were robustly assessed, partly because the patronage scenario was not robustly assessed. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. Inquiries: the Premier commissioned a review by David Richmond (October 2005); a Joint Select Committee on the Cross City Tunnel chaired by Rev. Fred Nile began in December 2005 and reported in February 2006 (CCT), May 2006 (CCT and PPPs) and August 2006 (Lane Cove Tunnel); the Auditor-General's performance audit was published in May 2006. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
12. On 27 December 2006 receivers and managers were appointed to the CrossCity Motorway parties. Press and secondary sources consistently identify KordaMentha as receivers, appointed by a syndicate of 16 banks, with debts reported at over AUD 500 million (figures of AUD 560 million and AUD 580 million appear). [confidence: high for date; medium for receiver name, syndicate size and debt figure] [source: 3, 5, 7]
13. After a competitive sale, ownership passed to a consortium of ABN AMRO and Leighton Contractors under contracts executed on 19 June 2007 and completed on 27 September 2007; the RTA consented to the sale, a refinancing and a change of O&M contractor the same day. Post-sale equity: ABN AMRO Diversified Infrastructure Trust 46%, ABN AMRO Global Infrastructure Fund 48%, Leighton 6%. The price was reported as about AUD 700 million. [confidence: high for dates and shares; medium for price] [source: 3, 7]
14. The original CCM parties had let the required professional indemnity insurance lapse on 30 June 2006 without renewal; the RTA learned of it on 23 September 2007, and alternative arrangements were put in place on 27 September 2007. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
15. By May 2009 traffic was approximately 35,400 vehicles a day (RTA). [confidence: high] [source: 5]
16. The CCT group failed a second time. On 30 December 2013 Transurban bought 100% of the senior debt from Royal Bank of Scotland while the group was in receivership, gaining the right to remove and appoint the receivers; on 26 June 2014 it bought the concession asset from the receivers for about AUD 475 million plus AUD 27 million of stamp duty and costs, and in June 2014 raised AUD 277 million of non-recourse debt on the asset. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
17. The second failure began in September 2013, when the ABN AMRO and Leighton-era owners entered administration; secondary sources attribute this to an inability to refinance, linked to a NSW claim for about AUD 64 million of stamp duty on the 2007 sale. [confidence: medium] [source: 7, 9]

### Lane Cove Tunnel

18. The RTA invited registrations of interest on 20 March 2002; four consortia registered by 24 April 2002: Lane Cove Tunnel Consortium (Thiess, Transfield Holdings, ABN AMRO), Lane Cove Motorway (Leighton, Deutsche Bank), Lane Cove Expressway (Baulderstone Hornibrook, Bilfinger Berger, Transurban) and TunnelLink (Abigroup, Ferrovial, Macquarie Bank). The Lane Cove Tunnel Consortium was named preferred proponent on 1 October 2003. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
19. The principal contracts were executed on 4 December 2003 and became fully effective on 9 December 2003, when the private parties also made an up-front payment to the RTA. The public sector comparator's risk-adjusted net present cost was AUD 193.2 million (10 September 2003 rates); the RTA expected private delivery to give a net financial benefit. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
20. Contract value about AUD 1.1 billion (estimated capital cost at award, NSW Treasury); the RTA's 2010 review gave the capital cost as "over $831.5 million". Design and construction was by a Thiess and John Holland joint venture. [confidence: high for figures; medium for D&C JV] [source: 4, 5, 11]
21. Maximum tolls were set at 1999 prices and indexed to CPI; at opening the cap for cars was AUD 2.55 (tunnel) and AUD 1.27 (Falcon Street ramps). The concession ran to 9 January 2037 (later extended to 30 June 2048 under Transurban). [confidence: high] [source: 4, 10]
22. The consortium renamed itself Connector Motorways in April 2006. In March 2007 the RTA paid Connector Motorways AUD 25 million in compensation for additional costs and lost revenue arising from the RTA's deferral of surface roadworks ("transition" changes). [confidence: high] [source: 10]
23. The tunnel opened on 25 March 2007, two months ahead of schedule (Stage 1 completed 20 March 2007). [confidence: high] [source: 5, 10]
24. Traffic and revenues were lower than anticipated; on 19 January 2010 the Connector Motorways entities "were forced into receivership" (RTA wording). Traffic in May 2009 averaged approximately 64,100 vehicles a day (RTA). Press reports said the original forecast was about 100,000 vehicles a day and that lenders had refused a further extension on about AUD 1.14 billion of debt. [confidence: high for date and May 2009 figure; medium for forecast and debt figures] [source: 5, 10, 12]
25. On 7 May 2010 Transurban agreed to buy the project assets; the sale completed on 9 August 2010 with novation of the contracts. The price was reported as AUD 630 million. Transurban funded the purchase and two growth projects with an AUD 542.3 million equity raising. [confidence: high for dates; medium for price] [source: 10, 12, 13]
26. In 2009 AMP Capital Investors, an equity investor in the LCT, sued the traffic forecasters Parsons Brinckerhoff and Booz Allen Hamilton in the NSW Supreme Court, alleging misleading or deceptive conduct and lack of reasonable care; AMP had invested about AUD 80 million and sought about AUD 160 million. The press reported AMP's claim that Parsons Brinckerhoff's 2002 base case forecast 187,700 vehicles a day by 2011, with a Booz Allen downside case of 149,900; actual traffic in 2012 was just over 70,000. The claim was reported as settled in 2014 on confidential terms. [confidence: medium] [source: 14]

### Both projects

27. The RTA's March 2010 post-implementation review found that the traffic models used for the CCT and LCT (NETANAL, the same method that worked reasonably for the M7) "over predicted the actual patronage", probably because of the difficulty of predicting total daily travel, inaccurate land-use inputs and toll-behaviour assumptions; the EIS traffic work used land-use predictions over a decade old by opening and did not consider ramp-up. It also found the private bids "utilised optimistic traffic forecasts", sustained by strong competition to invest and readily available finance. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

## Financing and structure details

- Both were build-own-operate-transfer style user-pay concessions under the NSW Working with Government guidelines: the private trustee and company financed, designed, built, operated and maintained the tunnel, collected tolls up to a capped, indexed maximum, and handed the asset to the public sector at concession end (CCT 18 December 2035; LCT originally 9 January 2037). [source: 3, 10]
- Each bid included an up-front payment to the RTA (CCT just under AUD 97 million). The Auditor-General noted the close link between a bidder's capacity to offer an up-front payment and its patronage projections, and recommended limiting up-front payments to development cost recovery and making the toll the point of competition. [source: 1]
- The RTA disclosed contract structure but not the private parties' financing terms, models or margins. CCT debt was syndicated among banks (reported as 16 banks, more than AUD 500 million); a refinancing accompanied the 2007 sale, and Royal Bank of Scotland held all the senior debt by late 2013. LCT debt was reported at about AUD 1.14 billion at receivership. No public guarantee of debt existed on either project; the RTA paid no compensation on default termination. [source: 3, 7, 8, 12]
- Lenders held step-in rights through a Security Trustee under RTA consent deeds; in both failures the lenders enforced through receivership and a sale of the concession as a going concern rather than termination, so the assets stayed open and the concessions survived intact. [source: 3, 10]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Bid forecasts were systematically high and competition rewarded them. The NSW Auditor-General (2006) found CCM's projections exceeded those of the RTA and both rivals and that the evaluation panel "should have seriously questioned" the viability of all CCM's bids; competing on the up-front payment amplified the reward for optimism and pushed up tolls. [source: 1]
- The models were not fit for revenue forecasting. The RTA's own 2010 review attributed the over-prediction to peak-hour planning models not built for toll behaviour, stale land-use inputs and no explicit ramp-up treatment, and recommended separate models for environmental assessment, design and revenue, more sensitivity testing and stress-testing of bidders' financial models. [source: 5]
- Toll level and road changes cut demand. The CCT's toll escalation and 15-cent increase made an expensive toll more expensive; the Auditor-General recommended assessing toll affordability and public acceptability and keeping toll-free alternative routes. [source: 1]
- Legal risk transfer worked; political risk transfer did not. Equity and lenders bore the traffic losses, and the RTA's legal position (no traffic representation, no compensation on default) held. But the government still bore reputational damage, multiple inquiries, road-change reversals and, on the LCT, a AUD 25 million payment for its own scope changes. The Auditor-General warned that if forecasts proved optimistic the government might need to step in to keep a tunnel open. [source: 1, 5, 10]
- Both assets ended up with Transurban at prices well below original cost, which shows the transfer of value from first-generation investors to a network owner able to integrate the asset. [source: 8, 13]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 48 (Technical, resource and market diligence): Use the CCT exhibit (CCM 87,088 vs RTA 82,347 vs rivals much lower, actual under 25,000) to show how to read a traffic study as a lender: compare the bid case with independent and competing forecasts, ask what toll elasticity, ramp-up and land-use assumptions drive the gap, and treat a winning bid's outlier forecast as a red flag. The RTA's admission that its models were built for peak-hour design, not daily revenue, is a clean example of a study used for the wrong purpose. Add the AMP lawsuit to show that forecaster liability is a weak backstop.
- Chapter 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels, Case T ramp-up): Contrast normal ramp-up (18 months to 3 years, per the Auditor-General) with the CCT's structural shortfall, where toll-free and half-price periods failed to restore demand. Show how full demand-risk transfer, up-front concession fees and indexed caps interact with debt sizing, and how lenders' going-concern enforcement and sale (twice for CCT) preserved the asset while equity was lost. Use the second CCT failure to show that a recapitalised asset at a lower price can still fail if refinancing risk and transaction taxes are mispriced.

## Do not state

- Do not state a single "90,000 vehicles a day" CCT forecast as the official number. The Auditor-General's figures are 87,088 (June 2006) and about 70,000 at opening; "90,000" appears in secondary sources only.
- Do not state the LCT "100,000 vehicles a day" forecast or the AMP-reported 187,700 figure as verified forecasts; they come from press reports, may refer to different road sections or years, and were not confirmed in RTA documents.
- Do not state the exact settlement amount of AMP's claim against Parsons Brinckerhoff (figures of AUD 121 million and AUD 144 million circulate) or which defendants paid.
- Do not state that "banks and all creditors were repaid in full" in the 2007 CCT sale or that original equity made a return; this appears in an unverified secondary summary and conflicts with other reporting.
- Do not state equity shareholdings in Connector Motorways (figures such as CKI 19.6%, AMP 15%, Leighton 11% appear in secondary sources only), or that Leighton and Mirvac "wrote off" interests, without a primary source.
- Do not state the exact debt quantum, number of banks or receiver identity for either project as primary-verified; they are press-reported.
- Do not state that the government lost money on traffic risk for either tunnel; the public sector's direct financial exposure was limited to items like the AUD 25 million LCT compensation.
- Do not state the cause of the 2013 CCT failure (stamp duty dispute) as established fact; it is secondary-sourced.
