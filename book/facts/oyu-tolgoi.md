# Oyu Tolgoi (South Gobi, Mongolia)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Rio Tinto and the Government of Mongolia agreement on the shareholder loan interest rate, 30 June 2026)

## Summary

Oyu Tolgoi is a copper-gold mine in Mongolia's South Gobi, owned by Oyu Tolgoi LLC: 66 percent Rio Tinto (held through Turquoise Hill Resources until Rio Tinto bought out Turquoise Hill's minority shareholders in December 2022) and 34 percent the Government of Mongolia through Erdenes Oyu Tolgoi. Rio Tinto has managed the project since 2010 under a 2009 Investment Agreement. The open pit, built in under 24 months for about USD 6.4 billion, began production in 2013, but more than 80 percent of the value lies in the underground block cave, whose development was halted in July 2013 amid disputes with the government. After a May 2015 Underground Mine Development and Financing Plan reset the relationship, Oyu Tolgoi signed on 15 December 2015 a USD 4.4 billion limited-recourse project financing that combined IFC and EBRD A and B loans, export credit (EDC, US EXIM, Efic), and 15 commercial banks with political risk insurance from MIGA, under a USD 6 billion senior debt cap. The underground was approved in May 2016 at USD 5.3 billion with first production expected in 2020; difficult ground conditions forced a redesign, and the definitive estimate of December 2020 put the cost at USD 6.75 billion (later USD 6.925 billion) and sustainable production in October 2022. Meanwhile disputes with the host intensified: a tax assessment of about USD 155 million taken to UNCITRAL arbitration in 2020, a December 2019 parliamentary resolution calling for the agreements to be improved, and a court challenge to the 2015 plan. A comprehensive agreement in January 2022 (including Turquoise Hill waiving the USD 2.4 billion carry account loan owed by the government's holding company, and a ban on new debt before sustainable production) unlocked the underground, which began production in March 2023. Rio Tinto now targets about 500,000 tonnes of copper a year on average from 2028 to 2036.

## Financing and structure details

- Borrower: Oyu Tolgoi LLC. Initial senior loans USD 4.4 billion (December 2015); debt cap USD 6 billion with USD 1.6 billion supplemental option. [source: 1]
- Multilaterals: IFC and EBRD (A loans plus B loans syndicated to commercial banks). ECAs: EDC, US EXIM, Efic. Commercial: 15 banks; USD 2.34 billion commercial-bank-funded facilities. [source: 1]
- Credit enhancement: MIGA USD 1 billion guarantee for commercial lenders (expropriation, transfer, war and civil disturbance, breach of contract), up to 12 years. [source: 5]
- Reported tranches: A USD 800 million (15 years); ECA USD 900 million (14 years) and US EXIM USD 400 million (13 years, fixed); MIGA-covered USD 700 million (12 years); B USD 1.6 billion (12 years). [source: 4]
- Shareholder funding: TRQ funded the government's 34 percent share through a carry account loan (USD 2.4 billion with interest by 2022, then waived). [source: 15]
- Later: debt re-profiling, supplemental debt and Rio Tinto co-lending sought in 2021–2022, subject to lender consent; whether and on what terms these closed was not verified. [source: 14, 15]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Multi-source structure worked as designed at closing: combining IFC and EBRD (preferred creditor and B-loan umbrella), three ECAs and MIGA cover brought 15 commercial banks into a Mongolian mining credit. [source: 1, 5]
- Cost and schedule: Rio Tinto attributed the 2019 setback to more difficult ground conditions and shaft construction complexity, leading to a mine redesign that left rock pillars in place. [source: 10, 13]
- Host relations: the record shows recurrent challenges: the 2013 halt, the 2018 tax assessment, court and parliamentary reviews of the agreements in 2019, a debt restriction in Resolution 103, and continuing negotiation over licences, fees and loan rates in 2025–2026. Resolution came through renegotiation (the carry loan waiver) rather than through lender enforcement or claims on political risk cover. [source: 9, 11, 12, 15, 19]
- Sponsor conflict: the funding gap caused by the overrun led to an arbitration between Rio Tinto and its listed subsidiary TRQ, resolved by funding agreements and finally by Rio Tinto buying out TRQ's minority. [source: 14, 16]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 60 (Political risk and its protection): Oyu Tolgoi is a textbook "obsolescing bargain": once the sponsor has invested billions, the host revisits terms through tax assessments, parliamentary resolutions and court challenges. Use the MIGA breach-of-contract cover and the World Bank Group presence to explain the "multilateral halo", then show that the actual fix was commercial renegotiation (waiving the USD 2.4 billion carry loan), because creeping pressure rarely meets the trigger for an insurance claim.
- Ch 78 (Mining and critical minerals): Teach how a mine financing differs from a power project financing: geological and geotechnical risk (the block-cave redesign), a debt cap with supplemental tranches, a staged open-pit-then-underground development, and the state partner's carried interest funded by the sponsor. Use the 2015 tranche stack (A/B, ECA, MIGA-covered) as the model of multi-source mining finance and the 2019–2022 overrun and funding plans as a case in completion risk and sponsor support.
