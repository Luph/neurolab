# Hub Power (Pakistan): early IPP, World Bank support, disputes

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: early termination of the Hub plant's power purchase agreement with effect from 1 October 2024; core coverage runs from 1987 to the December 2000 settlement)

## Summary

The Hub Power Project is a 1,292 MW plant of four units burning residual fuel oil, at Hub in Balochistan (Lasbela district). It was Pakistan's first private build-own-operate independent power project (IPP) and its first limited-recourse financing. The World Bank's Implementation Completion Report (ICR) calls it a landmark for the worldwide IPP industry. Development began in 1987. The power purchase agreement (PPA) with the Water and Power Development Authority (WAPDA) was signed in 1992, and financial close was reached around the turn of 1994/95 after setbacks that included the Gulf War and a Pakistani court ruling on interest under Shariah law. The plant was declared in commercial operation on schedule on 31 March 1997, at a cost of about USD 1.6 billion, within budget. Financing rested on a World Bank-funded long-term credit fund (LTCF) of subordinated loans with 23-year maturities. It also used the World Bank's first-ever partial risk guarantee (USD 240 million, alongside a parallel USD 120 million JEXIM guarantee), several export credit agency (ECA)-covered facilities and an international equity offering. From 1998 a new government and a cash-strapped WAPDA alleged that the 1993-94 PPA amendments raising early-year tariffs were corruptly obtained. Local courts blocked ICC arbitration, and the Supreme Court upheld that block by 3-2 on 14 June 2000. A settlement signed on 17 December 2000 cut the levelized tariff from 6.5 to 5.6 US cents/kWh and reaffirmed the PPA. In 2024 the government and Hubco agreed to end the PPA early, effective 1 October 2024, against its March 2027 expiry.

## Verified facts

1. The plant has four generating units totaling 1,292 MW and burns residual fuel oil supplied by Pakistan State Oil (PSO). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The development company, Hub River Power Group (HRPG) Limited, had five sponsors: Xenel (Saudi Arabia), National Power (UK), Mitsui (Japan), Ishikawajima-Harima Heavy Industries (IHI) and K&M Engineering and Consulting. National Power, later International Power, was the operator. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Work began in summer 1987, and the detailed feasibility study started in April 1988. The World Bank-supported private sector energy development project (PSEDP I) became effective in October 1988. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The PPA between WAPDA and Hubco was signed on 3 August 1992. It was supported by an Implementation Agreement (IA) with the Government of Pakistan (GOP) and a sovereign guarantee. Amendments No. 1 and No. 2 followed on 24 February 1994 and 17 September 1994, after a Supplemental Deed of 16 November 1993. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 5]
5. The turnkey construction contract was signed in July 1991 after the consortium was reconstituted. Ansaldo (Italy) replaced Toshiba for engineering and turbine generators, and Campenon Bernard (France) replaced Kumagai Gumi for civil works. IHI supplied the boilers. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. The contractor was mobilized in December 1992, two years before financial close. Mobilization finance of about USD 400 million came from the LTCF (World Bank, France and Italy), local bridge loans, the Commonwealth Development Corporation (CDC) and sponsor equity. The ICR gives the figure as "about US$423 million." [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. The financing package became irrevocably committed in September 1994. The ICR gives financial close as January 1995 in its subproject annex and as December 1994 in its main text. [confidence: high for the 1994/95 window] [source: 1]
8. Delays from the Gulf War (about 14 months, 1990-91), withdrawal of two contractors, six changes of government and the Federal Shariat Court ruling on interest (about seven months to resolve) raised the turnkey price by about USD 150 million. Senior lenders also required about USD 100 million of extra contingency and standby financing. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. The budgeted cost was USD 1.8 billion:
   - development and start-up costs of USD 217 million;
   - a turnkey contract of USD 1 billion;
   - financing costs of USD 355 million;
   - standby funds of USD 221 million.

   The actual completed cost was about USD 1.6 billion (about USD 1,238 per kW), because no standby was drawn. Of that, "soft" costs were USD 520 million, mostly interest during construction and financing costs, and development costs, mostly legal, were about USD 81 million. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. Equity of USD 372 million came from four sources: [confidence: high] [source: 1]
    - promoting sponsors, USD 163 million;
    - CDC, USD 8 million;
    - a Karachi Stock Exchange issue, USD 30 million;
    - Global and American Depositary Receipts, USD 170 million.

    The ICR describes it as the first international equity offering for a developing-country infrastructure project.
11. Senior debt used came to about USD 742 million across seven facilities: [confidence: high] [source: 1]
    - a 34-bank syndicate under the World Bank Expanded Cofinancing Operation (ECO) guarantee, USD 146 million;
    - a 19-bank syndicate under the JEXIM guarantee, USD 116 million;
    - COFACE-insured, seven banks, USD 52 million;
    - MITI-insured, 17 banks, USD 99 million;
    - SACE-insured, 18 banks, USD 224 million;
    - CDC, USD 39 million;
    - a rupee facility from nine Pakistani institutions, USD 66 million.
12. Subordinated debt came from the World Bank-funded LTCF, administered by the National Development Finance Corporation (NDFC), which lent USD 445 million in the event against USD 601 million planned. It included International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) funds of USD 146 million under PSEDP I and European Investment Bank (EIB) funds of USD 110 million under PSEDP II. LTCF loans had 23-year maturities; commercial banks would lend for at most 12 years. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. Hub used the World Bank's first partial risk guarantee, under its ECO program. After three years of preparation, the Bank and JEXIM issued parallel guarantees of USD 240 million (including USD 40 million standby, never used) and USD 120 million to protect commercial lenders against certain political risks. Through it the Bank supported 29% of senior term finance. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. The fourth unit was completed three weeks early, on 7 March 1997, and commercial operation was declared on schedule on 31 March 1997. The fuel pipeline from the oil terminal was commissioned in October 1996. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
15. The tariff has two parts. A capacity charge services debt, pays fixed costs and provides the equity return, and an energy charge covers fuel and variable operation and maintenance (O&M). The ICR gives an average of US cents 6.54/kWh for the first 10 years and US cents 5.80/kWh levelized over the project life. The tariff is front-loaded, falling from 7.42 cents (1999) to 5.63 cents (2005) and to 5.47 cents after the subordinated debt is repaid (2015). The front-loading funded required reserve and escrow accounts and the foreign exchange risk insurance premium paid to the State Bank of Pakistan. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
16. On 8 May 1998 a public-interest constitutional petition in the Lahore High Court challenged the PPA tariff. Interim orders, later modified by the Supreme Court, barred distributions and capped the fixed tariff element at PKR 845 million per month. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
17. On 9 July 1998 Hubco filed for ICC arbitration (London) seeking a declaration that PPA Amendment No. 2 was valid. On 11 October 1998 WAPDA alleged that the Supplemental Deed and Amendments No. 1 and No. 2 were void ab initio as procured by unlawful means, and claimed repayment of PKR 16 billion allegedly overpaid. Local court orders kept Hubco out of the arbitration. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
18. From 1998 the government, through the Private Power and Infrastructure Board (PPIB), issued seven Notices of Intent to Terminate on corruption grounds and two on technical grounds, covering about two-thirds of contracted private power capacity. The ICR notes that no evidence was produced in court. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
19. On 14 June 2000 the Supreme Court of Pakistan, by 3-2, dismissed Hubco's appeal and barred it from invoking ICC arbitration. It held that disputes turning on alleged criminality (corruption in procuring the contract) were, as a matter of public policy, for Pakistani courts. The case is reported as Hub Power Company Ltd v WAPDA, PLD 2000 SC 841. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4]
20. A Settlement Agreement among the GOP, WAPDA and Hubco was signed on 17 December 2000. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
    - The levelized tariff fell from 6.5 to 5.6 US cents/kWh by cutting the capacity purchase price from 4.45 to 3.36 cents/kWh, through the "Project Company Equity" element.
    - The World Bank's initial assessment was that the cut would be absorbed largely by reducing the equity return from 18% to around 12-14%.
    - The agreement reaffirmed the PPA and the right to arbitration, and provided for criminal investigations to be completed and cases disposed of, with approvals due by 31 March 2001.
21. In October 2024 the Government of Pakistan announced early termination of the PPAs of five IPPs, including the 1,200 MW residual-fuel-oil Hub plant, with effect from 1 October 2024; the Hub PPA had been due to expire in March 2027. The government said arrears would be paid without late-payment mark-up and no compensation for early termination would be paid. [confidence: medium, press reports of PSX disclosure and government statement] [source: 6, 7]

## Financing and structure details

- **Project company.** The Hub Power Company Ltd (Hubco), a build-own-operate structure listed in Karachi with GDRs. Contracts are a PPA with WAPDA, an IA with the GOP, a sovereign guarantee and a Fuel Supply Agreement with PSO. [source: 1, 4]
- **Capital structure at completion.** About USD 1.6 billion: equity of USD 372 million, senior debt of about USD 742 million, and LTCF subordinated debt of about USD 445 million, plus revenue during construction. [source: 1]

- **Maturities.** LTCF 23 years against a maximum of 12 years for commercial banks; the LTCF lengthened average maturity and lowered the levelized tariff. [source: 1]
- **Guarantees.** World Bank ECO partial risk guarantee of USD 240 million and a parallel JEXIM guarantee of USD 120 million, both backstopping specified government undertakings for commercial lenders. ECA cover came from COFACE, SACE and MITI. [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right.** The plant was built on time and within budget. The ICR calls Hub a prototype whose roughly 200 project documents became a reference worldwide. It also notes that the IA in effect played the role of enabling legislation, and that the project led to Pakistan's 1994 Private Power Policy. [source: 1]
- **Size and time.** The ICR concludes that the first IPP "should have been a smaller subproject," because its size drove complexity and an eight-year development. [source: 1]
- **Bank's roles.** The ICR says Bank involvement "went far beyond what was prudent for a development banker." The Bank was guarantor, indirect lender, lender to WAPDA and adviser to the government, which left it conflicted when the dispute came. [source: 1]
- **Offtaker and affordability.** WAPDA's finances deteriorated from about 1997. In hindsight WAPDA "was not fully committed to purchasing power from IPPs or to reforming itself." [source: 1]
- **Corruption allegations as leverage.** Developers widely saw the 1998-99 allegations as a means to extract tariff concessions. The ICR's lesson is that governments should pursue corruption according to law and due process, while honoring contractual obligations in the meantime. [source: 1]
- **Arbitration risk.** The Supreme Court's majority holding that corruption-tainted disputes are not arbitrable showed that a foreign arbitration clause can be neutralized locally. Pakistani commentators have discussed the case extensively since. [source: 1, 4]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 3 (History).** Hub bridges the PURPA-style US IPP model and the 1990s emerging-market IPP boom. It pioneered the PPA plus IA plus sovereign guarantee package, multilateral partial risk guarantees and subordinated multilateral-funded tenor extension. The ICR's own lessons (start smaller, avoid the lender acting as promoter, check affordability) are the era's lessons.
- **Ch 17 (Concessions, implementation agreements and government support).** Hub is the archetypal IA. Teach what the IA covered: foreign exchange convertibility and availability (per the ICR, the 1994 policy guaranteed foreign exchange availability and WAPDA's obligations), termination and compensation, and the sovereign guarantee. Show how the partial risk guarantee backstopped specific government undertakings rather than WAPDA's commercial payments, and how the 2024 early termination by agreement differs from termination for default under the IA.
- **Ch 60 (Political risk and its protection).** Use the 1998-2000 dispute for renegotiation under pressure. Cover allegations of corruption, local injunctions against ICC arbitration, the Supreme Court's 3-2 arbitrability ruling, the multilateral "halo" and its limits (the Bank was conflicted), and a negotiated settlement that cut equity return rather than debt service. Compare the outcome for lenders (debt protected) with that for equity (return cut from 18% to about 12-14%).

## Do not state

- That anyone was convicted of corruption over the Hub PPA amendments. The ICR records that no evidence was produced in court, and the settlement provided for investigations to be completed and cases disposed of.
- Names of individuals arrested or charged in 1998-99. Not verified.
- A single financial close date without qualification. The ICR gives both December 1994 and January 1995.
- That Hub was the "first IPP in Asia" or "first in the developing world." Use the ICR's specific "firsts."
- The "USD 1.6 billion" and "USD 1.8 billion" figures interchangeably. USD 1.8 billion was the budget including standby; about USD 1.6 billion was the actual cost.
- The exact amount Hubco received on the 2024 termination, or the dates of later PPA amendments (such as the 2020-21 IPP MoUs). Not verified for this sheet.
- That the World Bank's guarantee was called or paid. No evidence was found that it was.
- Any quotation from Pakistani officials or Hubco executives.
