# Triple Point v PTT (UK Supreme Court 2021): delay liquidated damages on termination

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Supreme Court judgment of 16 July 2021)

## Summary

Triple Point Technology, Inc v PTT Public Company Ltd [2021] UKSC 29 is the leading English authority on whether delay liquidated damages (LDs) survive termination of a contract when the work was never completed. PTT, a Thai company, hired Triple Point under an English-law contract dated 8 February 2013 to design, implement and license a commodity trading and risk management (CTRM) software system. The LD clause (article 5.3) ran "from the due date for delivery up to the date PTT accepts such work." Triple Point delivered the first payment milestone 149 days late, stopped work in May 2014 in a payment dispute, and PTT terminated on 23 March 2015. The trial judge awarded LDs for delay up to termination. The Court of Appeal held that no LDs were payable for work never completed and accepted, relying on the old House of Lords case British Glanzstoff. On 16 July 2021 the Supreme Court unanimously restored the conventional position. LDs accrue from the due date until termination, accrued LDs survive termination, and losses after termination are recovered as general damages. On two further issues about the contract's liability cap, the Court held unanimously that the LDs counted toward the cap. By a 3-2 majority it held that the carve-out from the cap for "negligence" covered negligent breach of the contractual duty of skill and care, not only the separate tort of negligence.

## Verified facts

1. Citation and court: Triple Point Technology, Inc (Respondent) v PTT Public Company Ltd (Appellant) [2021] UKSC 29, on appeal from [2019] EWCA Civ 230. The case was heard on 12 November 2020 and judgment was given on 16 July 2021. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. The bench was Lord Hodge (Deputy President), Lady Arden, Lord Sales, Lord Leggatt and Lord Burrows. Lady Arden gave the leading judgment, with which Lord Leggatt and Lord Burrows agreed. Lord Leggatt gave a concurring judgment, with which Lord Burrows agreed. Lord Sales, with whom Lord Hodge agreed, dissented on the negligence carve-out issue. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
3. The contract (the "CTRM Contract") was dated 8 February 2013 and governed by English law. Triple Point was to design, install (by data transmission), maintain and license customized software for PTT's commodity trading business, in two phases, paid by milestones. The agreed price for the Phase 1 services was USD 6,920,000. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. Article 5.2 required Triple Point to use best efforts to complete Phase 1 within 460 calendar days after the Effective Date. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. Article 5.3 read in material part: "If CONTRACTOR fails to deliver work within the time specified and the delay has not been introduced by PTT, CONTRACTOR shall be liable to pay the penalty at the rate of 0.1% (zero point one percent) of undelivered work per day of delay from the due date for delivery up to the date PTT accepts such work". It was common ground that, despite the word "penalty," this was a liquidated damages clause, not a penalty clause. [confidence: high, exact text from the judgment] [source: 1]
6. Article 12.3 capped Triple Point's total liability at "the Contract Price received by CONTRACTOR with respect to the services or deliverables involved under this Contract." Its final sentence said the limitation "shall not apply to CONTRACTOR's liability resulting from fraud, negligence, gross negligence or wilful misconduct". [confidence: high, exact text from the judgment] [source: 1]
7. Triple Point completed stages 1 and 2 of Phase 1 (the first payment milestone) on 19 March 2014, 149 days late, and PTT paid that invoice of USD 1,038,000. From May 2014 Triple Point refused to continue without payment of further invoices that PTT disputed. It completed no further stage of Phase 1 and no part of Phase 2. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
8. Triple Point began proceedings in the Technology and Construction Court (TCC) on 12 February 2015. PTT gave notice of termination on 23 March 2015 under articles 15.1 and 15.7 and counterclaimed. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. At trial, Jefford J (judgment of 23 August 2017) found the delay was caused by Triple Point's breach of its article 12.1 duty of reasonable skill, care and diligence. She dismissed Triple Point's claim and found PTT prima facie entitled to USD 3,459,278.40 of LDs for delay before termination, USD 630,000 of wasted hardware costs and USD 10,574,756.78 of termination loss (replacement system costs). She capped the wasted costs and termination loss at USD 1,038,000 (the amount PTT had paid) but held the LDs were outside the cap. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. The Court of Appeal (Lewison and Floyd LJJ and Sir Rupert Jackson) held that PTT could recover LDs only for work that had been completed and accepted. It also held that all damages, including LDs, were subject to the cap, and that the "negligence" carve-out applied only to freestanding torts or deliberate wrongdoing. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
11. Issue 1 (LDs for uncompleted work). The Supreme Court unanimously allowed PTT's appeal. Lady Arden held that the Court of Appeal's approach was "inconsistent with commercial reality and the accepted function of liquidated damages." Parties must be taken to know the general law that LDs stop accruing on termination, after which the employer claims general damages (para 35). The words "up to the date PTT accepts such work" were read as "up to the date (if any) PTT accepts such work" (para 38). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. On Glanzstoff, Lady Arden held that British Glanzstoff Manufacturing Co Ltd v General Accident, Fire and Life Assurance Corp [1913] AC 143 turned on the interpretation of its particular clause and created no special rule. The Court of Appeal had erred in being guided by it (paras 42, 72). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. Lord Leggatt added that a clause making LDs conditional on completion would give a contractor in serious delay an incentive not to complete the work (para 81). He also said it is not illogical to quantify damages for pre-termination and post-termination losses separately (para 79). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. Issue 3 (are LDs within the cap?). The Court unanimously dismissed PTT's appeal and agreed with the Court of Appeal that the overall cap in article 12.3 covered LDs (para 71). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
15. Issue 2 (the "negligence" carve-out). By 3-2 (Lady Arden, Lord Leggatt, Lord Burrows; Lord Sales and Lord Hodge dissenting), the Court held that "negligence" in the carve-out had its ordinary meaning. It covered breach of a contractual duty to exercise reasonable care and skill, not only the independent tort, so damages for Triple Point's negligent breach were outside the cap (para 68). The majority noted that the contract also imposed absolute obligations, such as meeting functionality specifications, so the cap still had substantial work to do (para 72). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
16. Lord Leggatt stated the principle that, absent clear words, parties are presumed not to intend to abandon remedies for breach that arise by operation of law (citing Lord Diplock in Gilbert-Ash and Photo Production). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
17. Lord Leggatt described the three categories of clause identified by Sir Rupert Jackson in the Court of Appeal: (a) the clause does not apply where the work is not completed; (b) it applies up to termination; (c) it continues after termination until completion by another contractor (as in Hall v Van der Heiden [2010] EWHC 586 (TCC)). The Supreme Court endorsed category (b) as the normal reading of a clause in this form. [confidence: high for categories; medium as a characterization of the overall holding] [source: 1]

## Financing and structure details

Not a project financing; the relevant structure is contractual:
- LD rate: 0.1% per day of the value of undelivered work, from the due date to acceptance (read as "if any"). [source: 1]
- Overall cap: Contract Price received with respect to the services or deliverables involved. Carve-outs for fraud, negligence, gross negligence and wilful misconduct. [source: 1]
- Trial-level figures: LDs USD 3,459,278.40; wasted costs USD 630,000; termination loss USD 10,574,756.78; cap applied by the judge USD 1,038,000. [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Court of Appeal's error.** Lady Arden and Lord Leggatt both said the Court of Appeal's reading would extinguish accrued rights and reward a defaulting contractor who never finishes. Commentators such as Crown Office Chambers described the judgment as a return to commercial reality and the settled understanding. [source: 1, 3]
- **Drafting lessons.** The case was litigated because the clause tied the LD end date to "acceptance" and the cap was ambiguous about LDs and negligence. Lady Arden noted parties may provide expressly for what happens on termination, though they need not. [source: 1]
- **Dissent.** Lord Sales and Lord Hodge would have read "negligence" as the independent tort. On their view the main purpose of the cap was to limit liability for ordinary breaches of the core duty of reasonable care and skill, which the majority reading would effectively nullify. [source: 1, 2]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 10 (Law for financiers).** Use the case to show how English courts interpret commercial clauses against their commercial purpose and the background law. Accrued rights survive termination, and clear words are needed to give up a remedy the law would otherwise provide. Pair it with Cavendish v Makdessi on the penalty rule, noting that in Triple Point the "penalty" label was irrelevant because both sides accepted the clause was genuine LDs. Use the 3-2 split on "negligence" to show why a cap carve-out's words matter.
- **Ch 22 (The EPC contract).** For a project financier, the point is that delay LDs typically fund debt service during a delayed completion. If the employer terminates a failing EPC contractor, accrued delay LDs remain recoverable up to termination, and post-termination losses (including replacement-contractor costs) fall to general damages. Teach students to draft and check: when LDs stop (completion, acceptance or termination), whether LDs count toward the overall cap and how that cap interacts with the LD sub-cap, and how carve-outs such as "negligence" are worded. The case also illustrates why lenders want delay LDs and termination remedies to fit together.

## Do not state

- That the Supreme Court held LDs continue to accrue after termination until a replacement contractor completes (category (c)). It did not. LDs ran to termination on this clause.
- The final net sum PTT recovered after the Supreme Court's decision. Not verified; the matter turned on the cap and carve-out applied to the trial findings.
- That the case concerned a construction or EPC contract. It was a software implementation contract; its reasoning is applied by analogy to construction contracts.
- That the press summary's reference to Lord Sales "dissenting on Issue 3" is correct. The judgment shows the dissent was on Issue 2 (the negligence carve-out). The Court was unanimous on Issue 3.
- Any quotation beyond those taken verbatim from the judgment above.
