# Critical minerals policy finance: US DOE/DFC/EXIM/DoD, the EU Critical Raw Materials Act and Japan's JOGMEC and JBIC

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest items: DFC Harena Rare Earths project development funding, August 2026; URIF approvals, 2 October 2026; EDF figures to 31 August 2026)

## Summary

Since about 2022, governments have moved from treating critical minerals as an ordinary commodity sector to financing them as a strategic supply chain. The tools differ by jurisdiction. In the US, DOE lends to domestic processing projects (the Thacker Pass and Rhyolite Ridge lithium loans of 2024–25). Under the 2025 administration these loans began to carry equity kickers: DOE took warrants for 5% of Lithium Americas and 5% of the Thacker Pass joint venture as a condition of the first draw. The Department of Defense went further with MP Materials in July 2025, combining preferred equity, a 10-year NdPr price floor of USD 110/kg and a magnet offtake guarantee. US EXIM's Supply Chain Resiliency Initiative (SCRI) finances foreign mines against signed offtake to US users, using its import rather than export authority. DFC takes equity stakes and funds project development abroad. The EU's Critical Raw Materials Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1252, in force 23 May 2024) sets 2030 benchmarks and a "Strategic Project" status that brings faster permitting and coordinated finance. The first 47 EU projects, with EUR 22.5 billion of expected capital investment, were selected on 25 March 2025, and RESourceEU (3 December 2025) added close to EUR 3 billion of financing over 12 months. Japan works through JOGMEC (equity, loans and debt guarantees for Japanese companies' mineral projects, and stockpiling) and JBIC (import and untied loans to producers). For project finance, these tools change the risk allocation that lenders see: price floors and guaranteed offtake replace merchant price risk, state equity sits alongside sponsors, and access to state finance becomes a condition of project bankability.

## Verified facts

### United States

1. DOE (LPO, now the Office of Energy Dominance Financing, EDF) announced in October 2024 a USD 2.26 billion ATVM loan (USD 1.97 billion principal plus USD 289.7 million capitalised interest) to Lithium Nevada Corp. (Lithium Americas) for lithium carbonate facilities at Thacker Pass, Nevada. Planned output is approximately 40,000 tonnes a year of battery-grade lithium carbonate. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. On 30 September 2025 Lithium Americas announced a non-binding agreement in principle with DOE and GM (its JV partner) on the first draw of USD 435 million. DOE would defer USD 182 million of debt service over the first five years. DOE would receive penny warrants for 5% of Lithium Americas and for a 5% economic (non-voting) interest in the Thacker Pass JV. Lithium Americas would post an additional USD 120 million to loan reserve accounts within 12 months of the first draw, and GM would amend its offtake agreement to allow third-party sales of volumes it was not forecast to buy. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. DOE closed in January 2025 a USD 996 million loan guarantee (USD 968 million principal plus USD 28 million capitalised interest) to Ioneer Rhyolite Ridge LLC for the Rhyolite Ridge lithium-boron project in Esmeralda County, Nevada. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. EDF describes its "Energy Dominance Financing Program" as including "securing critical mineral supply chains" among its pillars. In January 2026 it reported that more than USD 83.6 billion of Biden-era loans and commitments were being restructured, revised or eliminated (see facts/us-battery-doe.md). [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. On 10 July 2025 MP Materials announced a public-private partnership with the US Department of Defense. Its terms: a 10-year price floor of USD 110/kg for MP's NdPr products stockpiled or sold; a 10-year commitment that 100% of magnets from a new "10X" magnet facility would be bought by defence and commercial customers; DoD purchase of USD 400 million of convertible preferred stock plus a warrant (conversion and exercise price USD 30.03), representing about 15% on an as-converted, as-exercised basis; a USD 150 million DoD loan for heavy rare earth separation; and a USD 1.0 billion commitment letter from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs for the 10X facility. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
6. US EXIM's Supply Chain Resiliency Initiative finances international critical minerals projects that have signed long-term offtake contracts with US companies. EXIM says the financing is tied to its import authority, and the amount depends on the share of the project's offtake contracted to the US importer for products manufactured in the US. Projects controlled by the PRC, located there, relying on PRC technology, or with a significant share of PRC customers are ineligible. Domestic US projects use EXIM's Make More in America Initiative instead. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
7. DFC states that it invests in mining, processing and transport of critical minerals. Examples it gives: a USD 30 million equity investment supporting TechMet's Brazilian nickel-cobalt project (profiled in 2024); project development funding for Pensana (rare earth refining in Angola) and for Mkango's Songwe Hill rare earths mine in Malawi; and, in 2026, project development funding for Harena Rare Earths' Ampasindava ionic clay project in Madagascar, DFC's first mining investment there. DFC's release lists Serra Verde and Aclara among its rare earth portfolio. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8, 9]
8. The US–Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund (URIF; DFC with Ukraine, established under an April 2025 agreement) approved on 2 October 2026 a joint critical minerals investment platform with Ukraine's BGV Group Management, initially focused on rare earths, beryllium and zirconium deposits. [confidence: high] [source: 10]

### European Union

9. The Critical Raw Materials Act entered into force on 23 May 2024. It sets 2030 benchmarks for strategic raw materials: EU extraction of at least 10% of annual EU consumption, processing of at least 40%, recycling of at least 25%, and no more than 65% of annual consumption of any strategic raw material from a single third country. Where to find the current rule: Regulation (EU) 2024/1252 on EUR-Lex. [confidence: high] [source: 11, 12]
10. Strategic Projects receive coordinated support for access to finance and offtake, plus permitting deadlines of 27 months for extraction and 15 months for processing and recycling projects. The Commission notes that permitting currently takes 5 to 10 years. [confidence: high] [source: 11, 12]
11. On 25 March 2025 the Commission selected the first 47 Strategic Projects in 13 member states: 25 extraction, 24 processing, 10 recycling and 2 substitution projects, covering 14 of the 17 strategic raw materials, with 22 involving lithium. Expected capital investment is EUR 22.5 billion. The application cut-off was 22 August 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
12. RESourceEU (3 December 2025) commits to mobilising up to EUR 3 billion over the next 12 months for projects that can provide alternative supply in the short term. It also plans a European Critical Raw Materials Centre (early 2026) for joint purchasing, stockpiling and project finance, a stockpiling pilot, export restrictions on permanent-magnet scrap, and targeted CRMA amendments. It cites support for Vulcan's lithium project (Germany) and Greenland Resources' Malmbjerg molybdenum project, and 15 strategic partnerships with resource-rich countries. [confidence: high] [source: 13, 14]

### Japan

13. JOGMEC (Japan Organization for Metals and Energy Security) was formed in February 2004 by merging Japan National Oil Corporation and the Metal Mining Agency of Japan. [confidence: high] [source: 15]
14. For metals, JOGMEC provides equity or loans for exploration projects of Japanese companies, has offered equity participation in development and production-stage mining interests since 2010, and provides debt guarantees for development loans from private banks to Japanese companies. Since 2022 its equity and guarantees also cover domestic ore processing and smelting. Its examples include investment in niobium (Brazil) and ferrochrome (South Africa) producers, and a 2012 liability guarantee for part of the development funding of the Olaroz lithium project in Argentina. [confidence: high] [source: 16]
15. JOGMEC runs Japan's rare metals stockpile, established in 1983 as a public-private system, with releases on overseas supply interruption or domestic shortage. [confidence: high] [source: 17]
16. JBIC lent up to USD 466 million (JBIC portion; USD 666 million with co-financing) to Codelco on 28 March 2025 to secure copper concentrate imports. It also signed on 10 March 2026 a USD 1,175 million loan to Rio Tinto's Rincon Mining (Argentine lithium), of which JBIC's portion is USD 240 million, alongside IFC, Export Finance Australia, IDB Invest and commercial banks with a partial JBIC guarantee. JBIC cites Japan's March 2024 policy on securing a stable supply of critical minerals. See t-untied-resource-finance.md. [confidence: high] [source: 18, 19]

## Financing and structure details

- State as lender plus equity holder: DOE's Thacker Pass first-draw terms combine a debt-service deferral with penny warrants; DoD's MP package combines preferred equity, a loan, a price floor and an offtake guarantee. [source: 2, 5]
- Offtake-indexed sizing: EXIM SCRI sizes debt to the US share of offtake, an import-linked analogue of JBIC import loans. [source: 6, 18]
- Permitting and coordination as a form of finance: CRMA Strategic Project status gives deadlines and coordinated access to finance, not a subsidy in itself. [source: 11, 12]
- Japan: a guarantor and equity agency (JOGMEC) works alongside a lender (JBIC), conditioned on Japanese company participation or supply to Japan. [source: 16, 18]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The policy instruments target the gap the Commission and EXIM both name: dependence on supply chains controlled by a single third country (China). EXIM points to the PRC's November 2024 export ban on gallium, germanium and antimony. [source: 6, 12]
- Price-floor and equity structures shift price and dilution risk to the state. Whether they produce bankable projects without permanent support is not yet established. No outcome data was verified.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 78 (Mining and critical minerals): Teach the policy-finance toolkit as layers that change a lender's base case: price floors (MP), state equity and warrants (Thacker Pass), offtake-sized import financing (EXIM SCRI, JBIC import loans), guarantees for home-country sponsors (JOGMEC) and permitting deadlines (CRMA). Have students re-run a lithium or rare-earth cash-flow model with and without a floor and with a debt-service deferral.
- Chapter 88 (Frontier): Use the shift from grant and loan programmes to state equity (DOE warrants, DoD preferred, URIF) to discuss industrial policy as co-investor. Evaluate it by additionality, exit (GM's call right on the DOE JV units), policy durability across administrations, and crowding-in of private debt (the JPMorgan/Goldman commitment for MP).
