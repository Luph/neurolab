# SH 130 (Texas), Segments 5 and 6

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: 28 June 2017, effective date of the Chapter 11 plan of reorganization)

## Summary

State Highway 130 Segments 5 and 6 is a roughly 40–41 mile tolled extension of SH 130 running south-east of Austin, from Mustang Ridge to Seguin, forming the southern part of a 91-mile corridor built as a bypass of congested I-35. It was the first privately financed toll road concession in Texas. The Texas Department of Transportation (TxDOT) signed a comprehensive development agreement (CDA) with SH 130 Concession Company LLC, owned 65 percent by Cintra (Ferrovial group) and 35 percent by Zachry American Infrastructure, in March 2007, and the project reached financial close in March 2008. The total project cost of about USD 1.33 billion was financed with about USD 686 million of senior bank loans from a group of European banks, a USD 430 million federal TIFIA loan, and about USD 210 million of sponsor equity. The concessionaire received a 50-year concession from opening; TxDOT kept ownership of the road and contributed no funding. The road opened in late 2012 with an 85 mph speed limit, the highest in the United States, after the concessionaire paid TxDOT an extra USD 100 million tied to that limit. Traffic and toll revenue ran far below the forecasts on which the debt was sized; Moody's cut the senior debt from Baa3 to B1 in April 2013 and to Caa3 in October 2013. The company filed for Chapter 11 in the Western District of Texas in early March 2016. Under the plan that became effective on 28 June 2017, the sponsors' equity was cancelled, more than USD 1 billion of debt was removed, the senior lenders (led by distressed investor Strategic Value Partners) and the US Department of Transportation took ownership, and the reorganized company was refinanced with a USD 260 million senior term loan. Texas taxpayers bore no direct loss; the federal TIFIA lender took a large write-down in exchange for equity.

## Verified facts

1. SH 130 Segments 5 and 6 is an approximately 40–41 mile toll road (FHWA describes it as a 40-mile extension; contemporary press as 41 miles) forming the southern part of the 91-mile SH 130 corridor east of Austin, running from Mustang Ridge to Seguin. TxDOT operates the northern segments (about 50 miles). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 6]
2. The concessionaire, SH 130 Concession Company LLC, was owned 65 percent by Cintra (a subsidiary of the Spanish group Ferrovial) and 35 percent by Zachry American Infrastructure (San Antonio). [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
3. The comprehensive development agreement with TxDOT was signed on 22 March 2007; financial close was 7 March 2008; construction began in April 2009; the road opened to traffic on 24 October 2012, with service commencement recorded as 11 November 2012. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The concession runs for 50 years from the date of opening; TxDOT retained ownership of the road. The CDA included revenue sharing with the state. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6, 7]
5. Total project cost was USD 1,327.9 million, financed by senior bank loans of USD 685.8 million, a TIFIA loan of USD 430 million, private equity of USD 209.8 million and USD 2.3 million of interest income (FHWA figures). Press reports describe the bank loan as a 30-year facility and the TIFIA loan as a 35-year loan with USD 45.5 million of interest capitalised during construction; one press source gives shareholder equity of USD 197 million and the Bond Buyer gives a TIFIA figure of USD 438 million. [confidence: high for FHWA figures; medium for the other variants] [source: 1, 3, 4]
6. The senior bank lenders at close were a group of European banks led by Banco Santander, including Banco Espírito Santo, Caixa (Caixa-Banco de Investimento), Bankia and Fortis/BNP Paribas. The Bond Buyer reported the senior-lien debt's final maturity as 2038. [confidence: medium] [source: 3, 4]
7. The TIFIA direct loan was scheduled to begin principal repayment in 2018 with final maturity in June 2047, i.e. it was subordinated and back-ended relative to the bank debt. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. No state money was used; TxDOT stated it had no liability for the concessionaire's debt. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 6]
9. The concessionaire paid TxDOT USD 25 million at the outset and agreed an additional payment whose size depended on the posted speed limit: USD 67 million for an 80 mph limit or USD 100 million for 85 mph. The Texas Transportation Commission approved the 85 mph limit on 6 September 2012, the highest posted limit in the United States. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7]
10. Moody's rated the senior debt Baa3 and the TIFIA loan Ba1 in 2008. It downgraded the senior debt to B1 in April 2013 and to Caa3 in October 2013, warning of default. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
11. Traffic grew every year after opening (including about 15 percent year-on-year growth in 2015) but stayed well below the original forecasts; the Bond Buyer reported traffic and revenue at roughly half of the projected levels. The company attributed the shortfall to the lingering effects of the recession, which reduced regional traffic and delayed development along the rural corridor. [confidence: high for direction; medium for "roughly half"] [source: 2, 3, 4]
12. SH 130 Concession Company filed for Chapter 11 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Western District of Texas on 2 March 2016 (some reports give 3 or 4 March, reflecting announcement dates). Operations continued as normal throughout. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3, 5]
13. A plan of reorganization filed in August 2016 cancelled the equity held by Cintra and Zachry and gave the lenders new membership interests. The Modified Second Amended Joint Plan of Reorganization was confirmed by the bankruptcy court in May 2017 (company press release dated 19 May 2017). [confidence: high] [source: 3, 8]
14. The plan became effective and the company emerged from bankruptcy on 28 June 2017. More than USD 1 billion of debt was removed, and the reorganized company's only debt was a new USD 260 million senior secured term loan facility led by Goldman Sachs. A group of funds led by Strategic Value Partners, a distressed-debt investor, took control; Cintra and Zachry no longer hold any interest. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 8]
15. According to USDOT data reported by the Congressional Research Service, the TIFIA loan was converted into about 34 percent of the equity of the reorganized company (which holds the concession until 2062), a USD 15 million cash payment to the government, and a new USD 87 million subordinated loan. SH 130 was one of two TIFIA-assisted projects to enter bankruptcy through FY2018. [confidence: medium] [source: 9]

## Timeline

- 22 March 2007: CDA signed between TxDOT and SH 130 Concession Company.
- 7 March 2008: Financial close (bank loans of about USD 686 million, TIFIA USD 430 million).
- April 2009: Construction begins.
- 6 September 2012: Texas Transportation Commission approves 85 mph limit, triggering the USD 100 million payment to TxDOT.
- 24 October 2012: Road opens to traffic (service commencement 11 November 2012).
- April 2013: Moody's downgrades senior debt to B1.
- October 2013: Moody's downgrades senior debt to Caa3.
- 2014: Company comes close to default (press reports).

- 15 August 2016: Plan of reorganization filed.
- May 2017: Modified Second Amended Joint Plan confirmed.
- 28 June 2017: Plan effective; lenders and USDOT take ownership; new USD 260 million term loan.

## Financing and structure details

- Concession: 50-year design-build-finance-operate-maintain concession from opening (to 2062), with toll-setting and revenue-sharing provisions; TxDOT owner of the asset; full traffic (demand) risk with the concessionaire. [source: 1, 6, 9]
- Upfront payments to the state: USD 25 million plus USD 100 million linked to the 85 mph limit. [source: 7]
- Sponsors: Cintra 65 percent, Zachry 35 percent; equity about USD 210 million (about 16 percent of project cost). [source: 1, 3]
- Senior debt: about USD 686 million, 30-year bank loans, European bank club led by Santander. [source: 1, 4]
- Subordinated public debt: TIFIA USD 430 million direct loan, 35 years, capitalised interest during construction, principal deferred to 2018, maturing 2047. [source: 1, 4]
- Gearing at close: debt about 84 percent of project cost (computed from FHWA figures: (685.8 + 430) / 1,327.9 = 84.0 percent).
- Post-reorganization: USD 260 million senior term loan (Goldman Sachs-led); equity held by former senior lenders (Strategic Value Partners-led group) and USDOT (about 34 percent); USDOT also holds an USD 87 million subordinated loan. [source: 8, 9]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Traffic forecasting and ramp-up risk. The company itself attributed the failure to traffic below original expectations because of the recession and slower corridor development (CEO Alfonso Orol said the early-year challenges made the debt payment schedule unsustainable, as reported by InfraPPP World). Moody's successive downgrades in 2013, within months of opening, show that the gap was evident almost immediately. [source: 2, 3, 4]
- Debt sized on optimistic forecasts with thin equity. With about 84 percent debt, including a large back-ended federal loan, the structure had little cushion for a greenfield road with no traffic history. (This is an inference from the verified capital structure; attribute to the book, not to a named source.)
- The concession structure protected the public sponsor. TxDOT contributed no money, kept its upfront payments, and the road continued operating; the loss fell on equity, the senior banks (most of whose positions passed to distressed investors) and the federal TIFIA lender. [source: 2, 6]
- Federal credit exposure. The TIFIA loan was converted into equity plus a small subordinated loan, making SH 130 one of the program's first bankruptcy losses and an input to how USDOT's Build America Bureau underwrites demand-risk toll projects. [source: 9]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 64 (Distress, restructuring and enforcement): A compact US Chapter 11 case in which senior lenders credit-bid in effect by taking the equity, the sponsors were wiped out, and a public subordinated lender (TIFIA) converted debt to equity. Use the sequence (early downgrades, near-default, filing, plan, emergence in about 16 months, new USD 260 million debt against over USD 1 billion before) to teach restructuring math, the role of distressed-debt buyers such as Strategic Value Partners (which also held Indiana Toll Road debt), and why the public owner was insulated.
- Ch 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels): The textbook greenfield traffic-risk failure. Contrast a parallel free alternative (I-35) and a rural corridor that depended on future development with debt sized on forecasts that came in at roughly half. Use the 85 mph payment to discuss how concession terms and upfront payments to the grantor raise leverage, and set it beside Indiana Toll Road (brownfield, leverage-driven) to show two different routes to the same Chapter 11.

## Do not state

- That SH 130 traffic was "X percent" of forecast to a precise figure; only "well below" and the Bond Buyer's "approximately half" are sourced.
- An exact TIFIA recovery or loss amount in dollars; only the conversion terms (about 34 percent equity, USD 15 million payment, USD 87 million subordinated loan) are reported, from a CRS summary of USDOT data, and were not checked against a USDOT primary document.
- That the bankruptcy cost Texas taxpayers money; TxDOT had no liability and contributed no funding (though critics argued the state lost future revenue sharing).
- The division of the court: the Bond Buyer says San Antonio, the company press release says Austin Division; say only "Western District of Texas".
- A single definitive TIFIA principal (USD 430 million per FHWA, USD 438 million per the Bond Buyer, likely reflecting capitalised interest).
- Claims that the 85 mph limit caused the bankruptcy or specific accident statistics on the road; not verified here.
- The exact revenue-sharing percentages in the CDA; not verified.
