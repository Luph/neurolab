# West African Gas Pipeline (WAGP): supply interruptions, Ghana's thermal plants, and take-or-pay risk

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: WAPCo planned partial shutdown, 28 August–1 September 2026; Ghana 2026 Mid-Year Fiscal Policy Review, 23 July 2026)

Cross-references: the World Bank Group's role is in facts/t-dfis.md. Partial risk guarantees in Africa are also in facts/azura-edo.md (Nigeria) and facts/lake-turkana.md. Market norms for gas-fired IPPs are in facts/t-market-norms-2.md.

## Summary

The West African Gas Pipeline is a 678 km system, mostly offshore, that runs from Itoki in Nigeria to Takoradi in Ghana, with laterals to Cotonou (Benin), Lomé (Togo) and Tema (Ghana). It is owned and operated by the West African Gas Pipeline Company (WAPCo), whose shareholders are Chevron, NNPC, Shell, Ghana's Takoradi Power Company and the Togolese and Beninese state gas companies.

**Financing.** The project was appraised at about USD 590 million. Financing depended on a USD 50 million IDA partial risk guarantee (PRG) covering Ghana's Volta River Authority (VRA) as the main gas buyer, plus MIGA cover. Actual cost reached about USD 950 million, funded largely through extra shareholder loans.

**Volumes.** First gas reached Ghana in December 2008. Volumes never matched the 133 MMscf/d of contracted "foundation" gas: deliveries averaged 84 MMscf/d in 2011 and 38 MMscf/d in 2013. The causes were:
- gas off-specification on moisture (2009–2010, and again in February 2012);
- a ship's anchor severing the pipeline off Lomé (August 2012 to July 2013);
- Nigerian domestic gas demand, constraints on the Escravos-Lagos pipeline and Niger Delta insecurity;
- later, by the World Bank's account, Ghana's non-payment.

**Contract terms not enforced.** Take-or-pay, send-or-pay and make-up provisions were not enforced; supplies ran under force majeure, and the buyers relied on the supplier's goodwill.

**Consequences for Ghana.** Ghana's thermal plants at Aboadze (Takoradi) and Tema burned expensive light crude oil, and blackouts followed. The government contracted emergency power and built domestic gas supply, the Sankofa project. That project carried its own take-or-pay obligation of about USD 40 million a month from 2018, which Ghana struggled to pay. WAGP was converted to bidirectional flow in 2019–2020 to carry Ghanaian gas east to Tema.

**Interruptions since 2023.** These have come from:
- supply-side outages in Nigeria (producer maintenance in June 2024, a third-party pipeline breach in December 2025);
- Ghana Gas's Atuabo plant outage (July 2023);
- a payment dispute in which WAPCo briefly suspended transport for GNPC (1 July 2023);
- planned maintenance in 2025 and 2026.

## Verified facts

### A. Physical system, ownership and contracts

1. WAGP is 678 km long from the Itoki Natural Gas Export Terminal in Nigeria, where it links to the Escravos-Lagos Pipeline System (ELPS), to Takoradi (Aboadze) in Ghana.
   - **Laterals:** to Cotonou, Lomé and Tema.
   - **Diameters:** the main line is 20 inches; the Cotonou and Lomé laterals are 8 inches and the Tema lateral 18 inches.
   - **Capacity:** WAPCo describes initial capacity of 170 MMscf/d, rising over time to 460 MMscf/d. The World Bank ICR gives a maximum of 474 MMscf/d with additional compression.
   - **Foundation customers:** VRA's Takoradi Thermal Power Plant in Ghana and CEB (Communauté Électrique du Bénin, serving Benin and Togo).
   [confidence: high] [source: 1, 5]
2. Ownership as stated by WAPCo in September 2022:
   - Chevron West African Gas Pipeline Ltd: 36.9%
   - NNPC: 24.9%
   - Shell Overseas Holdings: 17.9%
   - Takoradi Power Company: 16.3%
   - Société Togolaise de Gaz: 2%
   - Société BenGaz: 2%
   The World Bank ICR's equity table at appraisal gives Chevron Nigeria USD 64.9 million, NNPC 44.2, Shell (SPDC) 31.8, Government of Ghana 28.8, SoBeGaz 3.5 and SoToGaz 3.5. That is USD 176.7 million in total, or about 36.7/25.0/18.0/16.3/2/2% (computed). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6g]
3. Commercial structure (World Bank ICR):
   - N-Gas, owned by NNPC, Chevron and Shell, buys gas from the Chevron and Shell joint ventures with NNPC on the ELPS. It ships the gas on ELPS and WAGP and sells it to buyers in Benin, Togo and Ghana.
   - VRA's Gas Sales Agreement includes the portion of VRA's payment that goes to WAPCo for transport.
   - If VRA persistently fails to pay, N-Gas may terminate. Termination makes VRA liable for a "termination payment" to N-Gas and WAPCo.
   - Over 92% of foundation gas volume was for VRA plants. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. Risk mitigation:
   - **IDA PRG.** The USD 50 million IDA partial risk guarantee (Board approval 23 November 2004) covered payment by Ghana of liquidated damages arising from early termination of the Takoradi Gas Sales Agreement through VRA's breach.
   - **MIGA.** The ICR cites a USD 75 million MIGA political risk guarantee to WAPCo. The World Bank's 2018 Ghana energy PAD lists a MIGA "guarantee for breach of contract for the West Africa Gas Pipeline" of USD 217 million in the Group's exposure.
   - **Private cover.** WAPCo also had similar insurance from Zurich, reinsured by OPIC, covering the payment obligations of Ghana, Togo and Benin.
   - **Dependence.** The IEG states that sponsor financing "was contingent on the IDA and MIGA guarantees". [confidence: high; the two MIGA figures differ by source and date] [source: 1, 2, 4]

### B. Cost, schedule and returns

5. Cost:
   - Appraisal cost was USD 589.6 million: EPC contracts USD 517.7 million and development costs USD 71.9 million.
   - The ICR gives actual cost as USD 950 million. The IEG review says the cost of pipeline commissioning was higher than estimated "by nearly $360 million" and that, including interest during the delayed construction, total cost had "more than doubled".
   - Overruns were funded by additional shareholder loans. The ICR records WAPCo's Business Plan 2014–18 as saying shareholder loans had reached "nearly 85% of investments versus 70% at the time of project appraisal". [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
6. Key dates (World Bank ICR timeline):
   - WAGP Treaty ratified by the four states in October 2004; final investment decision in December 2004.
   - Offshore construction began in August 2005.
   - First gas through WAGP arrived in Ghana in December 2008, and VRA generated with WAGP gas in April 2009.
   - Gas compression began in February–March 2011.
   - The Commercial Operation Date was reached on 1 November 2011, per the ICR text (the ICR's timeline table says October 2011).
   Before COD, gas was supplied on a "best-endeavor" basis without take-or-pay. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. The ICR's ex-post economic returns were 16.0% (low case) and 18.6% (high case). Its ex-post financial returns were 4.4% and 0.9%, against a 15% WACC, and it concluded WAPCo "will likely face problems to sustain operations and service its debt to the shareholders". The IEG re-estimated the economic return at closer to 6% and rated the outcome Moderately Unsatisfactory, with High risk to development outcome. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]

### C. Supply shortfalls and interruptions, 2009–2013

8. Contracted foundation volume was 133 MMscf/d, of which 123 MMscf/d was for Ghana per the World Bank's Sankofa ICR; the 2018 Ghana energy PAD refers to a contractual 120 MMcf/d for Ghana. Average gas received into WAGP:
   - 2009: about 1 MMscf/d;
   - 2010: 40 MMscf/d;
   - 2011: 84 MMscf/d;
   - 2012: 49 MMscf/d;
   - 2013: 38 MMscf/d.
   Averages include days of no flow. Average delivery in October–December 2013 was 80.5 MMscf/d, about 60% of contracted volume. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 4]
9. Causes the ICR identifies:
   - Delivered gas did not meet WAGP specifications (a moisture-content interruption from March 2009 to February 2010).
   - The pipeline ruptured near Lomé (August 2012 to June 2013).
   - Rising domestic Nigerian demand, slow Nigerian gas infrastructure development, ELPS capacity limits and Niger Delta unrest, including "frequent vandalization of gas supply infrastructure".
   During construction, illegal anchoring had also broken the pipeline at Cotonou (2008) and Lomé (2010). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. WAPCo press releases:
    - It "has been forced to shut off gas receipts" because gas at Itoki exceeded moisture specifications (February 2012), and it resumed receipts with less than contractual volume (March 2012).
    - It shut down the pipeline after a loss of pressure around the Lomé segment (dated 28 August 2012).
    - Its July 2013 release states the line "was severed by a ship's anchor in Togolese waters". The force majeure notice of 28 August 2012 was lifted on 11 July 2013, and transport resumed on 12 July 2013. [confidence: high] [source: 6a, 6b, 6c]
11. Contract enforcement:
    - VRA and CEB were "reluctant to invoke the pertinent clauses of their supply contracts, and instead have been relying on the goodwill of N-Gas".
    - Supplies "have been under Force Majeure since re-commissioning of the pipeline in July 2013, so 'send or pay' has been suspended".
    - The contractual framework of "take-or-pay provisions; make-up volumes; penalties" was "not ... enforced due to Force Majeure events". [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. The IEG review faults project design for not addressing supply-side risk from Nigeria. It notes "extensive analysis on the demand-side risks arising in Ghana". The PRG covered VRA's payment risk, not Nigerian supply. The ICR says supply risks "were not foreseen, given the large resource availability and gas flaring in Nigeria". [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]

### D. Effect on Ghana's thermal plants and policy response

13. The IEG review quotes an October 2013 statement by Ghana's Public Utilities Regulatory Commission. The Commission said unavailability of WAGP gas "resulted in an acute load shedding situation" and was "forcing the VRA to largely depend on expensive imported light crude oil for generation". [confidence: high] [source: 2]
14. Takoradi:
    - The Takoradi Thermal Power Plant at Aboadze was built as two 330 MW units.
    - T1 has operated since 1999 and is owned by VRA's subsidiary Takoradi Power Company.
    - T2 is owned by Takoradi International Company (TICO), 90% TAQA and 10% VRA.
    - IFC's 2015 brief says WAGP gas and offshore Ghanaian gas made dual-fuel operation feasible. T2's conversion to combined cycle (adding a 110 MW steam turbine, from 220 to 330 MW) cost approximately USD 325 million, with IFC USD 80 million plus USD 15 million, OPEC Fund USD 22.5 million and an FMO-led DFI consortium USD 330 million. Output is sold to VRA under a revised 25-year PPA. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
15. The World Bank's 2018 Ghana Energy Sector Transformation PAD gives the following picture:
    - Supply shortfalls from low dam levels and "erratic gas supply from the West African Gas Pipeline" led the government to contract three emergency power producers without competition during 2014–2017.
    - "Both supply shortages in Nigeria and nonpayment for gas by Ghana have caused supply interruptions."
    - In the second half of 2017, WAGP deliveries averaged 50 MMcf/d "on a pay-as-use basis". [confidence: high] [source: 4]
16. Sankofa take-or-pay (World Bank ICR, 2020):
    - The Sankofa gas project (offshore OCTP block, partners led by Eni; supported by a USD 500 million World Bank partial risk guarantee) was developed partly because WAGP supply was "increasingly unreliable".
    - Its gas contracts carry take-or-pay at a minimum of 170 MMscf/d. From 2018 these required payments of approximately USD 40 million a month. GNPC could not pay, so the government became contractually liable.
    - Actual offtake averaged 41 MMscf/d in 2018 and 90 MMscf/d in 2019.
    - The government paid, with delays, from June 2018 to September 2019, after which arrears accumulated.
    - Delay in agreeing reverse flow on WAGP kept Sankofa gas from reaching Tema until April 2019. [confidence: high] [source: 3]

### E. Bidirectional WAGP and recent interruptions, 2019–2026

17. WAPCo milestones:
    - Construction Management Agreement with Eni for the Takoradi–Tema Interconnection Project (TTIP) signed 2 May 2018.
    - GNPC registered as shipper and the WAPCo–GNPC Gas Transportation Agreement executed in April 2019.
    - Reverse flow from Ghana's Western Region to Tema began in June 2019 (WAPCo press release dated 6 August 2019).
    - Bidirectional transformation completed in July 2020, with Tema delivery capacity expanded from 144 to 235 MMscfd.
    - WAPCo declared WAGP an "open access" system in July 2012. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6d]
18. In March 2022 WAGP set a record monthly average delivery of 215 MMscfd and a one-day peak of 258 MMscfd. [confidence: high] [source: 6g]
19. On 8 July 2023 WAPCo said the previous night's power problems in Ghana were not due to any WAPCo technical issue. "Ghana National Gas Company's plant at Atuabo went down and therefore there was no gas available for WAPCo to transport." [confidence: high] [source: 6e]
20. On 12 July 2023 WAPCo stated that at 05:00 on 1 July 2023 it "suspended gas transportation on behalf of GNPC due to the GNPC's failure to fully pay for services received". It resumed limited transport the same day after a new agreement under the Ministry of Energy's auspices, allowing GNPC to nominate up to its contractual firm volume. [confidence: high] [source: 6f]
21. On 9 October 2023 WAPCo said its facilities were available but "there are some challenges with supply from Nigeria resulting in the shortfall currently experienced by offtakers". [confidence: high] [source: 6h]
22. On 12 June 2024 WAPCo announced a drop in volumes because a Nigerian gas producer had shut its facility for three weeks of maintenance. West-to-east flow in Ghana continued. In January 2025 WAPCo said 2024 had been "a year of significant progress in gas supply reliability" and credited NNPC. [confidence: high] [source: 6i, 6j]
23. Planned maintenance:
    - Pigging and in-line inspection of the 569 km offshore pipeline, plus subsea valve replacement, ran from 5 February to about 28 February 2025. WAPCo says such inspection is required every five years or on a risk-based schedule.
    - The laterals to Cotonou, Lomé and Tema and the west-to-east reverse flow were suspended during the work.
    - WAPCo announced completion "ahead of schedule" on 28 February 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 6k, 6l]
24. On 12 December 2025 WAPCo confirmed WAGP remained operational after "a breach incident affecting one of the pipelines from one of our main gas supply sources" in Nigeria. [confidence: high] [source: 6m]
25. WAPCo announced a planned partial shutdown from 28 August to 1 September 2026 for maintenance and integrity works in Togo, Benin and Nigeria. Gas transport from Nigeria was suspended, and Ghana received gas only via the western flow, so overall availability in Ghana was reduced. [confidence: high] [source: 6n]
26. In February 2025 WAPCo reported that it had sought amendments to WAGP's harmonised legal and fiscal regime since 2019. Nigeria and Benin had supported them (Benin's parliament voted in 2024), but Togo and Ghana had yet to act. All four states must approve any amendment. [confidence: high] [source: 6o]

### F. Ghana's fiscal exposure, 2024–2026

27. Ghana's 2025 Budget (11 March 2025) lists the 2024 energy-sector shortfall payments:
    - Sankofa gas (LC drawdown repayments and invoices) USD 605.36 million;
    - WAPCo USD 10.00 million.
    It also commits to raising N-Gas supply from 60 to 100 MMscf/d as a "liquid fuel to gas swap". [confidence: high] [source: 8]
28. Ghana's 2026 Budget (13 November 2025) lists the 2025 shortfall payments:
    - Sankofa gas USD 754.9 million;
    - N-Gas USD 35.0 million;
    - WAPCo USD 10.0 million.
    It reports a payment plan agreed with the World Bank to restore drawn standby LCs (on the Sankofa structure) and clear monthly gas invoices. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
29. Ghana's 2026 Mid-Year Review (23 July 2026) reports that by end-June 2026 gas for power had risen by 35 MMscf/d, to about 490 MMscf/d in total. Of the increase, 10 MMscf/d came from the OCTP partners led by Eni and 25 MMscf/d from N-Gas. [confidence: high] [source: 10]

## Financing and structure details

- **Equity and loans.** At appraisal, total funding of USD 589.6 million was equity USD 176.7 million plus shareholder loans USD 412.9 million (computed from the ICR table). Actual funding of USD 950 million comprised the same equity plus shareholder loans of USD 773.3 million (computed). No third-party project debt is shown. [1]
- **Guarantees.**
  - IDA PRG of USD 50 million, covering Ghana's payment of termination LDs under the Takoradi Gas Sales Agreement.
  - MIGA cover (USD 75 million per the ICR; USD 217 million listed as exposure in 2018).
  - Zurich/OPIC insurance on government payment obligations. [1, 4]
- **Contract chain.** Producers (Chevron and Shell JVs with NNPC) sell to N-Gas, which ships on ELPS and WAGP to VRA and CEB. WAPCo is the transporter, paid out of the gas sales price. Since 2019, GNPC and others have been shippers in a multi-shipper, open-access system. [1, 5, 6d]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **The guarantee covered the wrong risk.** The World Bank's own evaluation says the PRG addressed VRA's payment risk while the binding risk was Nigerian supply. "There is a subtle and brief mention of supply risk in the project documents ... sidelined with extensive analysis on the demand-side risks arising in Ghana." (IEG) [2]
- **Take-or-pay was never enforced.** Buyers would not invoke remedies against the only gas source, and force majeure suspended send-or-pay. A take-or-pay or send-or-pay clause is only as strong as the buyer's willingness and ability to walk away. (World Bank ICR) [1]
- **The fix created its own take-or-pay problem.** Domestic Sankofa gas solved availability but created a USD 40 million a month take-or-pay obligation. Ghana could not absorb it until the WAGP reverse-flow interconnection was agreed. It became a recurring fiscal cost, with Sankofa payments of USD 605 million in 2024 and USD 755 million in 2025. (World Bank Sankofa ICR; Ghana MoF) [3, 8, 9]
- **Payment discipline cuts both ways.** By 2017–2023, Ghana's non-payment had itself become a cause of interruptions (World Bank 2018 PAD; WAPCo July 2023 suspension). [4, 6f]
- **Bidirectional flow added resilience.** Since 2020 Ghana has had supply from both ends. Nigerian outages and maintenance (2024, 2025, 2026) reduce rather than eliminate gas to Tema. [5, 6i, 6n]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 25 (Inputs and access):**
  - Use WAGP to show that fuel supply risk sits upstream of the GSA: in producer capacity, transporter integrity, specification compliance and the producing country's own demand.
  - Contrast take-or-pay (the buyer pays for gas not taken) with send-or-pay (the supplier pays for gas not delivered), and show why neither was enforced (force majeure and dependence on a single source).
  - Pair with Sankofa to show a take-or-pay obligation crystallising before downstream infrastructure was ready, and its fiscal cost.
  - For Case P's GSA negotiation, draw out three points: specification-failure remedies, force majeure definitions that exclude the seller's domestic diversion, and alternative-fuel cost recovery in the PPA (VRA burned light crude oil).

## Do not state

- A single COD date without qualification. The ICR text gives 1 November 2011 and its table October 2011; a 2015 WAPCo release says commercial operations began in March 2011; WAPCo's 2022 anniversary release celebrated 10 years of commercial operations in September 2022.
- That the 2012 incident was caused by a specific named vessel, or any repair cost. WAPCo's release says only "a ship's anchor in Togolese waters".
- Specific take-or-pay or send-or-pay prices, deficiency payments or the amount of any claim between VRA, N-Gas and WAPCo. None were found in primary sources.
- The cumulative size of Ghana's or VRA's debts to N-Gas or WAPCo, beyond the payment lines in the budgets. Press figures vary and were not verified.
- A single MIGA guarantee amount. Sources give USD 75 million (ICR, construction-phase political risk) and USD 217 million (2018 PAD, breach of contract exposure).
- That WAGP has carried LNG or gas from sources other than Nigerian producers and GNPC/Ghana Gas, beyond WAPCo's promotional statements.
