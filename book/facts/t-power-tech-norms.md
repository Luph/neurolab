# Power technology norms: heat rates, capacity factors, storage performance, capex, O&M and LCOE

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest data covered: Lazard LCOE+ 2026, published July 2026; EIA Electric Power Monthly with data for July 2026, released 24 September 2026; World Nuclear Association performance data for 2024, page updated 20 March 2026)

## Summary

This sheet gives indicative performance and cost benchmarks for the main generating and storage technologies, each tied to a named source, market and date. Four sources carry most of the weight. Lazard's LCOE+ (Levelized Cost of Energy+) 2026 gives US new-build cost and performance assumptions: Version 19.0 of its LCOE and Version 11.0 of its levelized cost of storage (LCOS). US Energy Information Administration (EIA) statistics give fleet-wide heat rates and capacity factors that have actually been achieved. The IEA/NEA Projected Costs of Generating Electricity, 2020 edition, gives an international, multi-country view of overnight costs and LCOE conventions. The World Nuclear Association gives global nuclear capacity factors.

Fleet averages and new-build assumptions are different things, and a financier must not confuse them. In 2024, US natural gas plants averaged about 7,754 Btu/kWh across all gas technologies, while Lazard assumes about 6,475–6,550 Btu/kWh for a new CCGT. US combined-cycle plants ran at a 58.4% capacity factor in 2025 against Lazard's 30–90% new-build range. US nuclear ran at about 91%, and the global average was 83% in 2024. Renewables remain the cheapest new-build energy on an unsubsidized LCOE basis in Lazard's 2026 study. However, LCOEs for every technology rose year on year, and storage costs reversed their earlier decline.

NREL's Annual Technology Baseline (ATB) could not be reached during this research session (DNS failure), so this sheet cites no ATB figures. Writers should check ATB directly before relying on it.

## Verified facts

Heat rates (efficiency)

1. EIA fleet-average operating heat rates for US utility and IPP electric-power plants (CHP and commercial/industrial plants excluded), in Btu/kWh, 2024: coal 10,777; petroleum 11,200; natural gas 7,754; nuclear 10,443. Comparable 2014 values: coal 10,428; natural gas 7,907. Over the decade the gas heat rate improved, mainly because more combined-cycle capacity entered the fleet, while the coal heat rate worsened. For nuclear, EIA uses the weighted-average tested heat rate. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
2. Lazard LCOE v19.0 new-build heat-rate assumptions (Btu/kWh, low–high case): gas peaking 10,275–11,175; US nuclear 10,450; coal 8,750–12,000; gas combined cycle 6,475–6,550. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Lazard's assumptions for existing, fully depreciated plants (marginal-cost analysis) in Btu/kWh: gas peaking 10,100–12,125; nuclear 10,400; coal 10,400–11,850; gas combined cycle 7,050–7,700. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. Conversion rule: thermal efficiency = 3,412 Btu/kWh ÷ heat rate. Computed for this sheet: 6,475 Btu/kWh ≈ 52.7%; 7,754 ≈ 44.0%; 10,275 ≈ 33.2%; 10,777 ≈ 31.7%; 12,000 ≈ 28.4%. The sources do not state whether their heat rates are on a higher or lower heating value basis, so writers must not mix them with OEM efficiency quotes without checking the basis. [confidence: high for arithmetic] [source: computed from 1, 2]

Capacity factors

5. EIA annual capacity factors for US utility-scale generators, 2025 (Table 6.07.A/B, data release of 24 September 2026; the latest year may be revised): coal 48.7%; natural gas combined cycle 58.4%; natural gas combustion turbine 14.1%; natural gas steam turbine 19.8%; natural gas internal combustion 17.4%; nuclear 91.0%; conventional hydro 35.3%; geothermal 65.9%; wind 34.2%; solar PV 24.4%; solar thermal 23.6%. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
6. EIA 2024 comparators: coal 42.6%; combined cycle 60.5%; combustion turbine 13.9%; nuclear 90.8%; wind 34.3%; solar PV 23.2%. Coal's capacity factor ranged from 40.5% (2020) to 53.6% (2018) over 2016–2025. Combined cycle ranged from 51.2% (2017) to 60.5% (2024). [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
7. Lazard v19.0 new-build capacity factor ranges (low-cost case – high-cost case): utility solar 30%–20%; community and C&I solar 20%–15%; onshore wind 55%–30%; offshore wind 55%–45%; geothermal 90%–80%; gas peaking 15%–10%; US nuclear 94%–89%; coal 85%–65%; gas combined cycle 90%–30%. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. The IEA/NEA 2020 edition uses a standard 85% capacity factor for nuclear, coal and CCGT plants, on the basis that they run as baseload, and 30% for OCGTs. It also shows sensitivities at 50% for flexible coal, nuclear and CCGT and at 10% for OCGTs. The report says coal plants "habitually" achieve only 50–60% load factors in major markets, and that the 85% assumption is higher than observed averages, particularly for CCGTs. For other technologies it uses country-specific capacity factors. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
9. Nuclear, global: the World Nuclear Association reports a global average capacity factor of 83% in 2024, up from 82% in 2023. North American reactors averaged 90%, and more than 60% of reactors exceeded 80%. WNA finds no overall age-related decline in performance. Global nuclear generation reached a record 2,667 TWh in 2024. (A different passage on the same page gives 81.5% for 2023, so use 82% only as a rounded figure.) [confidence: high for 2024; medium for 2023 figure] [source: 6]
10. Nuclear construction times (WNA): the seven reactors grid-connected in 2024 averaged 114 months from first concrete to grid connection. They ranged from 61 months (Zhangzhou 1, Hualong One, China) to 204 months (Flamanville 3 EPR, France). [confidence: high] [source: 6]

Storage: round-trip efficiency and degradation

11. EIA: in 2019 the US utility-scale battery fleet had an average monthly round-trip efficiency of 82%, and pumped-storage facilities averaged 79%. Weighted average battery duration rose from just over 46 minutes in 2015 to 1.5 hours in 2019. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
12. Lazard LCOS v11.0 (utility-scale standalone, 100 MW): assumed efficiency 91%–87% for 2-hour systems and 92%–86% for 4-hour systems; 1 cycle a day at 90% depth of discharge, 350 operating days a year; 20-year project life; 10% overbuild. Total capital cost is $270–580/kWh (2-hour) and $270–365/kWh (4-hour). Storage O&M is $5.00–8.75/kWh-year (2-hour) and $3.75–7.75/kWh-year (4-hour). Charging cost is $40/MWh, escalating at 2% a year. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. Lazard models degradation through available capacity and covers it with augmentation costs, which sit in O&M and are "incurred in years needed to maintain usable energy at original storage module cost". In its illustrative sample calculation for a 100 MW, 2-hour system with 10% upsizing, available capacity is 110 MW at year 0, then 109, 107, 104 and 102 MW in years 1–4. This is an illustration, not a market norm for degradation. [confidence: high as illustration] [source: 1]
14. Lazard unsubsidized LCOS v11.0: 100 MW 2-hour $215–414/MWh; 100 MW 4-hour $210–292/MWh. With the ITC including the bonus adder: 2-hour $152–282/MWh and 4-hour $148–209/MWh. LCOS uses a different capital structure from the LCOE study (20% debt at 8%, 80% equity at 12%). Lazard says storage costs rose this year, reversing the previous year's declines, because tariffs on lithium-ion imports cut access to low-cost Chinese cells. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

Capex and O&M (new build)

15. Lazard v19.0 capital cost ($/kW) and fixed O&M ($/kW-year), low–high:
- utility solar: $1,250–1,850 capex; $8.25–26.25 fixed O&M
- community and C&I solar: $1,750–2,950; $13.00–20.00
- onshore wind: $1,900–2,750; $24.50–40.00
- offshore wind: $5,600–7,100; $60.00–91.50
- geothermal: $5,135–6,635; $14.50–15.75 (plus variable O&M of $9.05–24.80/MWh)
- gas peaking: $1,100–1,650; $10.00–17.00
- US nuclear: $12,300–17,570; $136–158
- coal: $3,495–7,405; $40.85–94.35
- gas combined cycle: $1,450–2,100; $10.00–25.50

[confidence: high] [source: 1]
16. Lazard's illustrative CCGT high case uses $2,400–2,600/kW, based on market quotes for early-stage projects with commercial operation after 2028. Lazard says CCGT capital costs "have not yet reached the levels of recently observed quotes" and expects further cost increases in future studies. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
17. Lazard's nuclear LCOE is based on public cost estimates for Vogtle Units 3 and 4. The high end reflects Unit 3. The low end assumes, on the advice of selected industry experts, about 30% learning-curve savings between Units 3 and 4. Assumed construction time is 84 months, and facility life is 60–80 years. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
18. IEA/NEA 2020 overnight costs (USD/kWe; 243 plants in 24 countries, for commissioning in 2025), median (range): CCGT 955 (254–1,109); coal 1,785 (800–4,382); nuclear new build 3,370 (2,157–6,920); nuclear long-term operation 497 (391–629); utility-scale solar PV 923 (534–2,006); onshore wind ≥1 MW 1,439 (877–3,022); offshore wind 2,740 (1,721–4,039); lithium-ion battery 655 (452–1,967); OCGT/internal combustion 668 (325–1,141). [confidence: high] [source: 5]

LCOE

19. Lazard LCOE v19.0, unsubsidized, US$/MWh (average in brackets): utility solar 40–98 (69); community and C&I solar 88–197 (142); utility solar plus storage 61–156 (109); geothermal 67–111 (89); onshore wind 37–99 (68); onshore wind plus storage 49–140 (95); offshore wind 105–167 (136); gas peaking 144–276 (210); US nuclear 175–255 (215); coal 72–177 (125); gas combined cycle 51–129 (90). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
20. Lazard's base financing assumption is 60% debt at 8% interest and 40% equity at a 12% cost. Fuel prices are $3.45/MMBtu for gas, $1.47/MMBtu for coal and $0.85/MMBtu for nuclear. The coal and geothermal LCOEs are Lazard's v14.0 results adjusted for inflation, because there is too little new-build data. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
21. Lazard's marginal cost of existing, fully depreciated conventional plants (US$/MWh, average in brackets): gas peaking 52–122 (87); nuclear 26–36 (31); coal 34–69 (51); combined cycle 32–51 (42). Lazard says existing plants have become relatively more competitive as new-build costs rise and higher load growth spreads their fixed costs over more output. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
22. Lazard reports that, on its own historical data, the unsubsidized LCOE fell by 52% for onshore wind and 81% for utility solar between 2009 and 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
23. IEA/NEA 2020 conventions: real discount rates of 3%, 7% and 10%, with 7% as the headline; a carbon cost of USD 30/tCO2 for fossil plants; plant-level costs only, so system integration costs are excluded. It found that variable renewables were the least-cost option in China and India, and, at median plant level with a USD 30/t carbon cost, in the United States. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

Drivers that move these norms

24. Lazard attributes the 2026 rise in LCOE across technologies to higher capital costs, sustained interest rates, tariff pass-through and supply-chain repricing. For gas, it points to constrained gas-turbine supply that extends development timelines. For storage, it cites tariffs on Chinese cells and Foreign Entity of Concern restrictions under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), which kept the storage ITC through 2033. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
25. Other existing sheets cover related figures: Fraunhofer ISE German LCOE financing assumptions are in t-market-norms (item 24); gas-turbine and transformer lead times are in t-frontier-data (item 4). [confidence: n/a, cross-reference]

## Financing and structure details

- Lazard's LCOE capital structure (60/40 debt/equity, 8% debt, 12% equity) and its LCOS capital structure (20/80, same costs) are modelling conventions, not market quotes. Lazard solves for the $/MWh that gives a levered IRR equal to the cost of equity. [source: 1]
- The IEA/NEA 2020 headline uses a 7% real discount rate, applied as a social discount rate with no capital-structure split. [source: 5]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Lazard states that LCOE is "a snapshot" and "not a forecasting tool". It also says the analysis excludes integration costs, congestion, curtailment and transmission. Its Cost of Firming Intermittency analysis adds firming costs based on capacity accreditation, and concludes that renewables remain broadly competitive with CCGTs even after these firming costs. [source: 1]
- The IEA/NEA explicitly warns that the 85% baseload capacity factor overstates what CCGTs and coal plants actually achieve. This is why an LCOE that relies on a baseload assumption overstates the competitiveness of mid-merit plant. [source: 5]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 11 (how power assets and markets work): Teach heat rate as fuel per unit of output and convert it to efficiency with 3,412/HR. Contrast the new-build CCGT assumption (6,475 Btu/kWh) with the US gas fleet average (7,754 Btu/kWh) to show why the vintage of a plant decides where it sits in the merit order. Use the EIA 2025 capacity factors to show how dispatch position, not just technology, sets a plant's running hours: CCGT 58%, peakers 14%, coal 49%, nuclear 91%.
- Ch 69 (thermal power): Build a dispatch-cost stack from Lazard's existing-plant marginal-cost table, then show how the clean spark spread depends on heat rate and gas price. Use the gap between the Lazard CCGT high case ($2,400–2,600/kW quotes) and the base range ($1,450–2,100/kW) to discuss turbine supply scarcity and EPC price risk.
- Ch 70 (onshore wind and solar): The LCOE ranges are driven by capex, capacity factor and cost of capital. Show the sensitivity of LCOE to capacity factor (for example, solar at 20% versus 30%) and to the cost of capital, and tie this to the P50/P90 energy yield used in debt sizing.
- Ch 71 (offshore wind): Lazard's $5,600–7,100/kW capex and $105–167/MWh LCOE frame why offshore wind depends on CfDs or revenue support. Emphasize that capacity factor and O&M (about $60–92/kW-year) dominate after capex.
- Ch 72 (hydropower and geothermal): Use EIA geothermal (66%) and hydro (35%) capacity factors and IEA overnight-cost spreads to show site-specificity. Lazard's geothermal figures are inflation-adjusted v14.0 results, a reminder that thin data makes estimates fragile.
- Ch 73 (storage, transmission, interconnectors): Teach round-trip efficiency (EIA fleet 82% in 2019; Lazard 86–92%), augmentation versus degradation, and why the LCOS for 2-hour and 4-hour systems differs. Note that Lazard's LCOS assumes much lower gearing than its generation LCOE.
- Ch 74 (nuclear): Contrast the operating performance of existing plant (91% US capacity factor, 83% global) with new-build economics (Vogtle-based $12,300–17,570/kW; LCOE $175–255/MWh). Use WNA construction times (61–204 months) to frame completion risk.

## Do not state

- Any NREL ATB figure (cost, capacity factor, degradation rate or round-trip efficiency): the ATB was not reachable in this session.
- A single "industry-standard" battery degradation rate per year (for example "2% a year"). Degradation depends on chemistry, cycling, temperature and warranty terms. Cite a named source if one is used.
- That Lazard's heat rates or the IEA/NEA efficiencies are LHV or HHV: neither source states the basis on the pages reviewed.
- That the IEA/NEA has published a newer (tenth) edition of Projected Costs. None was verified in this session, so check iea.org and oecd-nea.org.
- Lazard's coal and geothermal LCOEs presented as current market observations: they are inflation-adjusted older results.
- EIA 2025 annual capacity factors as final: the latest year is subject to revision.
