# SAF mandates: ReFuelEU Aviation (Regulation (EU) 2023/2405), the UK SAF Mandate (2024 Order) and the UK Revenue Certainty Mechanism

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest documents covered: DfT "SAF Revenue Certainty Mechanism: Contract Allocation Strategy", July 2026; Sustainable Aviation Fuel Act 2026, Royal Assent 5 March 2026; Commission report on the ReFuelEU flexibility mechanism, 28 February 2025)

Cross-references: facts/saf-project.md (Montana Renewables and its DOE loan guarantee, a US SAF project financing; US tax credits are in facts/t-us-tax-credits.md); facts/t-hydrogen-support.md (hydrogen support schemes relevant to power-to-liquid SAF); facts/t-uk-cfd-rab.md (the CfD model that the UK RCM resembles); facts/h2global.md (a double-auction alternative for e-fuels).

## Summary

The EU and the UK both create SAF demand through an obligation on jet fuel suppliers rather than on airlines.

ReFuelEU Aviation, Regulation (EU) 2023/2405 of 18 October 2023:
- requires fuel supplied at Union airports to contain at least 2% SAF from 2025, 6% from 2030, 20% from 2035, 34% from 2040, 42% from 2045 and 70% from 2050;
- includes a rising synthetic (e-fuel, RFNBO) sub-mandate from 2030 (1.2% average over 2030-31, 5% from 2035, 35% from 2050);
- excludes food and feed crops, palm and soy-derived materials and similar feedstocks;
- sets fines of at least twice the price gap multiplied by the shortfall volume, and requires the shortfall to be made up in the next period.

The ETS amendment (Directive (EU) 2023/958) reserves up to 20 million allowances (2024 to 2030) to cover part of the SAF price gap for airlines.

The UK SAF Mandate, the Renewable Transport Fuel Obligations (Sustainable Aviation Fuel) Order 2024 (SI 2024/1187, in force 1 January 2025), works through tradable certificates:
- the main obligation is equivalent to 2% of jet fuel in 2025, 10% in 2030 and 22% from 2040;
- a separate power-to-liquid (PtL) obligation starts in 2028 and reaches 3.5% in 2040;
- a "HEFA cap" limits how much of the main obligation can be met from used cooking oil and tallow fuels from 2027;
- buy-out prices of GBP 0.137 per MJ (main) and GBP 0.145 per MJ (PtL) act as a price ceiling.

The mandate gives demand but not a bankable price. The UK has therefore legislated a Revenue Certainty Mechanism (Sustainable Aviation Fuel Act 2026): a guaranteed-strike-price contract for difference with a government-owned counterparty, funded by a levy on aviation fuel suppliers. The first allocation round (AR1) targets up to 230,000 tonnes a year of non-HEFA capacity, with applications opening from Q1 2027 and contract awards from Q4 2028.

## Verified facts

### EU: ReFuelEU Aviation

1. Regulation (EU) 2023/2405 of 18 October 2023, "on ensuring a level playing field for sustainable air transport (ReFuelEU Aviation)", was published in the Official Journal on 31 October 2023. It applies from 1 January 2024, with the supply, uplift, airport and reporting obligations (Articles 4, 5, 6, 8 and 10) applying from 1 January 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Article 4 and Annex I set the minimum SAF shares that aviation fuel suppliers must meet in all aviation fuel made available at each Union airport:

   | From | Minimum SAF share | Synthetic aviation fuel within it |
   |---|---|---|
   | 2025 | 2% | none |
   | 2030 | 6% | 1.2% average over 2030-31, minimum 0.7% each year |
   | 2032 | 6% | 2.0% average over 2032-34; minimum 1.2% in 2032-33 and 2.0% in 2034 |
   | 2035 | 20% | 5% |
   | 2040 | 34% | 10% |
   | 2045 | 42% | 15% |
   | 2050 | 70% | 35% |

   [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. SAF means synthetic aviation fuels (RFNBOs meeting the RED lifecycle threshold), aviation biofuels or recycled carbon aviation fuels. Aviation biofuels include:
   - advanced biofuels;
   - Annex IX Part B biofuels (such as used cooking oil and animal fats);
   - other biofuels meeting the RED sustainability and lifecycle criteria, except those from food and feed crops.

   Article 4(5) excludes from the minimum shares SAF made from food and feed crops, intermediate crops, palm fatty acid distillate, palm and soy-derived materials, and soap stock and its derivatives, unless the feedstock is listed in RED Annex IX. Renewable hydrogen for aviation and low-carbon aviation fuels (70% lifecycle saving threshold) can also count towards the minimum shares. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. Scope: a "Union airport" has more than 800,000 passengers or more than 100,000 tonnes of freight in the previous reporting period and is not in an outermost region. An "aircraft operator" has at least 500 commercial passenger flights or 52 all-cargo flights departing Union airports in the previous period. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. Anti-tankering: aircraft operators must uplift at least 90% of the yearly aviation fuel required at a given Union airport (Article 5). The fine is at least twice the yearly average aviation fuel price per tonne multiplied by the non-tanked quantity (Article 12(2)). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. Supplier penalties (Article 12(4) and (5)): fines of at least twice the difference between the yearly average price of SAF (or of synthetic fuel, for the sub-mandate) and conventional fuel, multiplied by the non-compliant quantity. Under Article 4(7) the supplier must also make up the shortfall in the next reporting period; for the 2030-31 synthetic average, the shortfall must be made up by the end of 2034. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. Flexibility (Article 15): from 1 January 2025 to 31 December 2034, suppliers may meet the minimum shares as a weighted average across all the fuel they supply at Union airports in a reporting period. Compliance may use the RED mass balance system (Article 4(6)). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. On 28 February 2025 the Commission published its Article 15 report on the flexibility mechanism. It concluded that the mechanism, together with expanding EU production, was driving progress toward the 2025 (2%) and 2030 (6%) targets, and that "complex additional flexibility mechanisms appear to be unwarranted at this stage". It flagged further work on SAF accounting, including virtual trading of certificates, and a Sustainable Transport Investment Plan. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
9. Article 17 requires a Commission review report by 1 January 2027 and every four years thereafter. The report will cover the shares, the SAF definition, fine levels and possible support mechanisms. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. Directive (EU) 2023/958, amending the EU ETS Directive (new Article 3c(6)), reserves a maximum of 20 million allowances for 2024 to 2030 for commercial aircraft operators' use of eligible SAF. Their purpose is to cover part of the remaining price gap between fossil kerosene and SAF. [confidence: high] [source: 3]

### UK: SAF Mandate

11. The Renewable Transport Fuel Obligations (Sustainable Aviation Fuel) Order 2024, SI 2024/1187, was made on 18 November 2024 and came into force on 1 January 2025. The Secretary of State for Transport is the Administrator. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
12. The Order places two annual (calendar-year) obligations on suppliers of fossil jet fuel, at the point where the fuel can only be supplied to UK aviation: a main obligation, and a PtL obligation that only PtL fuel can discharge. Obligations are measured in energy terms (MJ). Suppliers below 344 TJ a year deduct the first 15.9 TJ. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
13. The Order sets percentages of the obligated (fossil) amount. DfT guidance converts them to shares of total aviation fuel:

    | Year | Main obligation (% of total fuel) | PtL obligation (% of total fuel) | Total |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 2025 | 2.0% | none | 2.0% |
    | 2028 | 6.6% | 0.2% | 6.8% |
    | 2030 | 9.5% | 0.5% | 10.0% |
    | 2035 | 13.5% | 1.5% | 15.0% |
    | 2040 onwards | 18.5% | 3.5% | 22.0% |

    The Order's own percentages for the same years are 2.041%; 7.082% plus 0.215%; 10.556% plus 0.556%; 15.882% plus 1.765%; and 23.718% plus 4.487%. Levels stay flat after 2040 until reviewed. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 6]
14. Certificates are awarded per unit of energy multiplied by a carbon-intensity factor, so fuels with deeper emission savings earn more certificates. Certificates are tradable. PtL certificates can count against the main obligation, but standard certificates cannot discharge the PtL obligation. SAF that has received support under another country's scheme cannot earn certificates. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
15. Buy-out: GBP 0.137 per MJ for the main obligation and GBP 0.145 per MJ for the PtL obligation. The government set these above expected SAF costs, as a ceiling that also protects consumers, and imposes no separate penalty for shortfall discharged by buy-out. At DfT's illustrative 34 MJ per litre, they equal approximately GBP 4.66 and GBP 4.93 per litre; secondary sources round these to GBP 4.70 and GBP 5.00. [confidence: high for the per-MJ figures; per-litre figures are this sheet's arithmetic] [source: 4, 5, 6]
16. HEFA cap (Article 22): the share of the main obligation that may be met with certificates from SAF derived from segregated oils and fats (used cooking oil, tallow) is:
    - 100% in 2025 and 2026;
    - 92.31% in 2027;
    - 74.74% in 2030;
    - 57.78% in 2035;
    - 42.16% from 2040. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
17. Civil penalties apply for failing to register within 28 days, failing to pay the buy-out, and giving inaccurate information. DfT guidance cites a maximum of GBP 100,000 or 10% of relevant turnover, whichever is lower. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]

### UK: Revenue Certainty Mechanism

18. Milestones listed by DfT:
    - April 2024: design consultation;
    - January 2025: decision on a Guaranteed Strike Price model, with the first contracts for non-HEFA UK projects;
    - May 2025: SAF Bill introduced; decision on a variable levy on aviation fuel suppliers;
    - October 2025 and June 2026: levy design consultations;
    - January 2026: consultation on indicative heads of terms;
    - 5 March 2026: Royal Assent of the Sustainable Aviation Fuel Act 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
19. Mechanism: a private-law contract between a UK SAF producer and a wholly government-owned counterparty sets a strike price per litre of eligible SAF over a fixed period. If the reference price is below the strike price, the counterparty pays the difference. If it is above, the producer pays the difference back. The counterparty's payments are funded by a levy on aviation fuel suppliers. A floor price caps the difference payments, acting as a minimum reference price. A "price discovery mechanism" is intended to reward producers for selling above the floor. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
20. AR1 design (July 2026 strategy):
    - a tendered bid process with bilateral negotiations rather than a sealed-bid auction (91% of consultation respondents supported this);
    - up to 230 ktpa of supported capacity (approximately 0.74 MtCO2e a year of savings);
    - no ringfence for PtL;
    - pre-launch engagement from Q4 2026, applications from Q1 2027, shortlisting from Q4 2027 and awards from Q4 2028;
    - commissioning by the end of Q1 2033 at the latest.

    DfT reserves the right to change all of these. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
21. DfT's illustrative levy analysis assumes 15-year contracts, which DfT says is not a definitive position. On that basis, AR1 could cost levied suppliers up to approximately GBP 3 billion in a low SAF price scenario. In a high-price scenario, producers would pay up to approximately GBP 1 billion back to the counterparty. If price discovery fails and SAF sells at a floor modelled at HEFA or Jet A-1 prices, difference payments could reach approximately GBP 7 to 8 billion over 15 years. [confidence: high (as government illustrative modelling)] [source: 7]
22. Grant support runs alongside: the Advanced Fuels Fund allocated GBP 198 million across three windows to 21 projects. Its successor, the Low Carbon Fuels Fund, is to allocate up to GBP 219 million over four years, subject to business cases. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

## Financing and structure details (what lenders look at)

- Revenue sources for a SAF plant:
  - the physical fuel price;
  - in the UK, the value of mandate certificates, capped in effect by the buy-out;
  - in the EU, an offtaker's willingness to pay to avoid fines of twice the price gap plus make-up;
  - ETS allowances that airlines can claim (EU, to 2030);
  - in the UK from about 2028 to 2033, an RCM contract for difference.
- Price risk under a pure mandate: certificate prices can collapse in oversupply and have only a soft ceiling. The UK government's stated rationale for the RCM is that first-of-a-kind non-HEFA plants cannot raise finance without a predictable revenue floor (source 7).
- Feedstock caps (the UK HEFA cap and the EU exclusions) shape which technologies the mandates actually fund. They push support towards alcohol-to-jet, gasification Fischer-Tropsch and PtL, which carry higher technology risk.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The Commission's 2025 report judged the EU mandate on track for 2% in 2025 (source 2). The UK government judged that mandate demand alone left a gap in investor confidence for new technology. It responded with the RCM, which uses a CfD architecture familiar from power (source 7). DfT's own range of AR1 levy outcomes (from GBP 1 billion paid back to GBP 7 to 8 billion paid out) shows how sensitive the cost of a CfD is to reference-price and floor design.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 83 (hydrogen, CCS and sustainable fuels): teach the SAF revenue stack as layered policy. A volume mandate on suppliers (EU and UK) creates demand. A penalty or buy-out ceiling bounds certificate value. Feedstock caps steer technology choice. In the UK, a CfD-style RCM converts the merchant certificate price into a bankable strike price. Have readers compute a supplier's UK obligation (the DfT example is 15,000,000 kg x 43 MJ/kg x 2.041% = 13.164 TJ) and compare the buy-out cost with a SAF cost premium. Use the market-risk-holder trace: under ReFuelEU the price risk sits with suppliers and airlines, while under the RCM it moves to levy payers, the aviation fuel suppliers who pass it on.

## Do not state

- Do not state a final UK RCM contract length. DfT uses 15 years "for analytical purposes" only, and law firm commentary citing "up to 10 years plus 5" was not verified against the Act or the final heads of terms.
- Do not state any final RCM strike, reference or floor price, the levy rate, or the counterparty's identity. These were not finalised as of July 2026.
- Do not state individual EU member state penalty levels. They were not researched.
- Do not state actual 2025 compliance outcomes (EU or UK SAF volumes supplied in 2025). EASA and DfT statistics were not checked.
- Do not state that the EU has adopted a book-and-claim system or amended Regulation 2023/2405. No amendment was found as of the as-of date, and a Commission feasibility study was only reported (secondary source).
- Do not state "GBP 4.70 per litre" or "GBP 5.00 per litre" as the legal buy-out prices. The law sets prices per MJ.
