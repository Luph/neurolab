# IFC Performance Standards (2012), Equator Principles EP4, World Bank ESF

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Swedish Export Credit Agency (EKN) becomes an Equator Principles signatory, 24 September 2026; IFC Sustainability Framework update Phase I due to conclude October 2026)

## Summary

Three linked rulebooks govern environmental and social (E&S) risk in international project finance. The IFC Performance Standards on Environmental and Social Sustainability (2012 edition, effective 1 January 2012) are eight standards that IFC's clients must meet for the life of an investment, from risk assessment (PS1) through labour, pollution, community safety, resettlement, biodiversity, Indigenous Peoples and cultural heritage (PS2-PS8). They are the de facto global benchmark, and other lenders apply them too. The Equator Principles (EP) are a voluntary risk-management framework for financial institutions, launched on 4 June 2003. The current version, EP4, was published in July 2020 and applied to all signatories from 1 October 2020. EP4 applies the IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank Group EHS Guidelines to projects in "Non-Designated Countries." In "Designated Countries" it applies host-country law, with the Performance Standards as guidance for specific risks. EP4 also adds climate change risk assessment, human rights and FPIC (free, prior and informed consent) requirements. The World Bank's own lending (Investment Project Financing to governments) uses a different instrument: the Environmental and Social Framework (ESF). It was approved on 4 August 2016, applies to projects initiated on or after 1 October 2018, and has ten Environmental and Social Standards (ESS1-ESS10). IFC is now updating its Sustainability Framework. Phase I (dialogue) is expected to conclude in October 2026 and a public consultation phase to start in October-December 2026, so the 2012 Performance Standards remain in force for now.

## Verified facts

**IFC Performance Standards (2012)**

1. The Performance Standards on Environmental and Social Sustainability are dated and effective 1 January 2012. Together with IFC's Policy on Environmental and Social Sustainability and its Access to Information Policy, they form IFC's Sustainability Framework. The 2006 edition is archived. Guidance Notes accompany the standards; the page shows them as last updated 14 June 2021. Current text: ifc.org, "IFC Performance Standards." [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. The eight standards: [confidence: high] [source: 1]
   - PS1: Assessment and Management of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts;
   - PS2: Labor and Working Conditions;
   - PS3: Resource Efficiency and Pollution Prevention;
   - PS4: Community Health, Safety, and Security;
   - PS5: Land Acquisition and Involuntary Resettlement;
   - PS6: Biodiversity Conservation and Sustainable Management of Living Natural Resources;
   - PS7: Indigenous Peoples;
   - PS8: Cultural Heritage.
3. PS1 sets out the overarching approach: [confidence: high] [source: 1]
   - an integrated assessment of risks and impacts;
   - an environmental and social management system (ESMS);
   - stakeholder engagement, including a Stakeholder Engagement Plan scaled to the project;
   - a grievance mechanism for Affected Communities.

   PS2-PS8 follow a mitigation hierarchy: avoid, then minimise, and where residual impacts remain, compensate or offset.
4. PS2 bars economically exploitative or hazardous child labour, and bars employing anyone under 18 in hazardous work. It bars forced labour, requires a grievance mechanism for workers, and requires the client to identify child and forced labour risks in the primary supply chain where these are high. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. PS3 requires projects expected to produce, or currently producing, more than 25,000 tonnes of CO2-equivalent a year to quantify their direct emissions and their indirect emissions from off-site energy use. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. PS5 requires the client to avoid or minimise displacement and to compensate for loss of assets at full replacement cost. Resettlement Action Plans or Livelihood Restoration Plans must aim to improve, or at least restore, livelihoods and living standards. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. PS6 classifies habitats as modified, natural and critical. It seeks no net loss of biodiversity where feasible in natural habitats, and requires net gains in critical habitats. In critical habitat the client may not proceed unless a set of conditions (paragraph 17) is met, including achieving net gains in the biodiversity values for which the habitat was designated. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. PS7 requires Informed Consultation and Participation with affected Indigenous Peoples in all cases. It requires their FPIC in special circumstances (paragraphs 13-17): [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
   - impacts on lands and natural resources under traditional ownership or customary use;
   - relocation from such lands;
   - significant impacts on critical cultural heritage;
   - commercial use of their cultural heritage.

   FPIC "does not necessarily require unanimity" (PS7 wording). EP4 adds that FPIC does not confer veto rights on individuals or sub-groups.

**Equator Principles (EP4)**

9. The Equator Principles were launched in Washington, D.C. on 4 June 2003. EP3 launched on 4 June 2013. EP4 was adopted by the EP Association in November 2019 and published in July 2020. All EP Financial Institutions (EPFIs) had to implement it by 1 October 2020, after a three-month COVID-19 grace period. Current text: equator-principles.com. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4, 5]
10. EP4 applies globally, to all sectors, and to five products: [confidence: high] [source: 3]
    - Project Finance Advisory Services where total project capital costs are USD 10 million or more;
    - Project Finance with total project capital costs of USD 10 million or more;
    - Project-Related Corporate Loans that meet three tests: the majority of the loan relates to a project over which the client has Effective Operational Control; the total aggregate loan and the EPFI's individual commitment (before syndication or sell-down) are each at least USD 50 million; and the tenor is at least two years;
    - Bridge Loans with a tenor under two years that are intended to be refinanced by in-scope Project Finance or Project-Related Corporate Loans;
    - Project-Related Refinance and Acquisition Finance, where the underlying project was financed under the EP framework, there has been no material change in scale or scope, and Project Completion has not yet occurred.
11. EP4 Principle 1 categories: [confidence: high] [source: 3]
    - Category A: potential significant adverse E&S risks and/or impacts that are diverse, irreversible or unprecedented;
    - Category B: limited adverse risks and/or impacts that are few in number, generally site-specific, largely reversible and readily addressed through mitigation;
    - Category C: minimal or no adverse risks and/or impacts.
12. EP4 Principle 2 requires a Climate Change Risk Assessment, aligned with the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) physical and transition risk categories, in two cases: [confidence: high] [source: 3]
    - for all Category A and, as appropriate, Category B projects, covering physical risks;
    - for all projects in all locations whose combined Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions are expected to exceed 100,000 tonnes CO2e a year, covering transition risks plus an alternatives analysis of lower-GHG options.

    Human rights risks should be assessed with reference to the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights (UNGPs).
13. Principle 3 (applicable standards): [confidence: high] [source: 3]
    - in Non-Designated Countries, compliance with the IFC Performance Standards and the World Bank Group EHS Guidelines;
    - in Designated Countries, compliance with host-country law, regulations and permits, with the EPFI also evaluating whether one or more Performance Standards should be used as guidance for the project's specific risks. This addition was new in EP4.
14. A Designated Country must be both an OECD member and on the World Bank High Income list. The EP Secretariat reviews the list quarterly. The EP website states that 37 countries are currently designated. [confidence: high for the test; medium for the count of 37] [source: 3, 6]
15. EP4 extends FPIC to projects in all countries (including Designated Countries) that meet the PS7 special circumstances. A qualified independent consultant must evaluate the consultation with Indigenous Peoples against host-country law and PS7. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
16. Principles 4-10 require: [confidence: high] [source: 3]
    - an ESMS and an Equator Principles Action Plan;
    - stakeholder engagement;
    - a grievance mechanism;
    - an Independent Environmental and Social Consultant review for all Category A and, as appropriate, Category B projects;
    - covenants in the financing documents (compliance, reporting, remedies);
    - independent monitoring;
    - reporting and transparency. Clients must publish an ESIA summary online, and projects emitting over 100,000 tonnes CO2e a year must report GHG emissions publicly each year during operations. Clients are also encouraged to share biodiversity data with the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF).
17. The EP framework is voluntary and creates no rights or liabilities for third parties. Where laws conflict with the EP, host-country law prevails. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
18. The EP website states that 126 financial institutions are signatories. It also states that the EP Association legal entity "was dissolved in 2024," and that references to it may since then be read as the collective group of signatories. [confidence: high (as stated on the site, accessed 2026-10-03)] [source: 6, 7]
19. Recent EP activity: [confidence: high] [source: 6, 8]
    - new signatories: Banco de Bogotá (27 September 2024), Bank of Kaohsiung (25 March 2025), Bpifrance Assurance Export (1 April 2025), Commerzbank (2 April 2025), Garanti BBVA (17 July 2025) and EKN (24 September 2026);
    - a joint EP/UNEP FI publication on nature-related risk assessment (22 January 2026);
    - new guidance for data centre projects (15 June 2026);
    - the 2025 annual meeting in Istanbul (24 October 2025).

**World Bank Environmental and Social Framework (ESF)**

20. The World Bank Board approved the ESF on 4 August 2016. It applies to all Investment Project Financing (IPF) projects initiated on or after 1 October 2018; earlier projects continue under the legacy Safeguard Policies. It does not govern IFC or MIGA, which use the Performance Standards. Current text: worldbank.org, "Environmental and Social Framework." [confidence: high] [source: 9, 10]
21. The ten Environmental and Social Standards: [confidence: high] [source: 9]
    - ESS1: Assessment and Management of E&S Risks and Impacts;
    - ESS2: Labor and Working Conditions;
    - ESS3: Resource Efficiency and Pollution Prevention and Management;
    - ESS4: Community Health and Safety;
    - ESS5: Land Acquisition, Restrictions on Land Use and Involuntary Resettlement;
    - ESS6: Biodiversity Conservation and Sustainable Management of Living Natural Resources;
    - ESS7: Indigenous Peoples/Sub-Saharan African Historically Underserved Traditional Local Communities;
    - ESS8: Cultural Heritage;
    - ESS9: Financial Intermediaries;
    - ESS10: Stakeholder Engagement and Information Disclosure.
22. Under the ESF the Bank classifies projects as High, Substantial, Moderate or Low Risk, rather than A/B/C. The borrower's commitments are set out in a legally binding Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP). ESS7 requires FPIC in specified circumstances. [confidence: high] [source: 10]

**IFC framework update**

23. IFC states that it is updating its Sustainability Framework, including the Performance Standards. Phase I (dialogue) is anticipated to conclude in October 2026, and Phase II (public consultation on a first draft) to start in October-December 2026. No Board approval or effective date has been published. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 11]

## Financing and structure details

How the standards enter a financing (EP4 Principles 4, 7, 8, 9):

- **Due diligence:** an Independent E&S Consultant (IESC) reviews the ESIA, ESMS, ESMP and Stakeholder Engagement Plan, and drafts the Equator Principles Action Plan (EPAP) or Environmental and Social Action Plan (ESAP) for Category A and, as appropriate, B projects.
- **Covenants:** the client covenants in the loan documents to comply with host-country E&S law and the EPAP/ESAP, to report periodically (at least annually), and to decommission per an agreed plan. Non-compliance not remedied within an agreed grace period gives lenders remedies, which can include an event of default.
- **Monitoring:** an independent monitor reports after financial close and over the loan's life.
- **Multi-lender deals:** IFC/MIGA clients apply the Performance Standards. Projects with World Bank IPF components apply the ESF, which binds the borrowing government. The OECD Common Approaches bind export credit agencies (not verified in detail here). Arrangers harmonise these into one ESAP.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Critics (as summarised in the Wikipedia entry and NGO campaigns such as BankTrack) argue that the EP lacks enforcement and accountability. They cite cases where signatory banks financed controversial projects, such as the Dakota Access Pipeline, where 13 of 17 financing banks were signatories. Present this as criticism, attributed. [source: 5]
- EP4's FPIC-everywhere rule and its Designated-Country "evaluate specific risks" step responded to the criticism that host-country law in high-income countries did not protect Indigenous Peoples, for example in North American pipeline cases. Law-firm commentary links EP4's changes to these concerns (see O'Melveny, Norton Rose Fulbright, A&O Shearman commentary of 2019-2020, not re-read for this sheet).

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 50 (Environmental and social risk and standards):** Use the three frameworks as layers: the IFC Performance Standards are the substance; EP4 is how commercial banks adopt and enforce it through scope thresholds, categorisation, IESC, covenants and reporting; the ESF is the sovereign-lending analogue with its own risk classes and the ESCP. Teach the scope tests as a decision tree (product type, USD 10 million / USD 50 million thresholds, tenor, Effective Operational Control). Show how PS5 "full replacement cost" and livelihood restoration, PS6 "net gain in critical habitat" and PS7 FPIC become cost, schedule and conditions-precedent items. Tie this to Case P's resettlement and ESAP. Flag the as-of date: IFC is updating the Performance Standards, with consultation from late 2026.
- **Credit-risk angle (also Chapter 50):** E&S failure becomes credit risk through permit challenges, community blockades, lender default triggers and reputational withdrawal. Covenants and the EPAP are the levers that let lenders act before it does.

## Do not state

- That EP4 took effect on 1 July 2020 or in 2022/2023. All EPFIs had to implement it by 1 October 2020.
- That FPIC equals a community veto. Both PS7 and EP4 say it does not require unanimity, and EP4 says it confers no veto on individuals or sub-groups.
- A precise current number of Designated Countries without checking the EP website; it changes with OECD and World Bank status.
- Signatory counts from older sources (for example 116 institutions in 37 countries as of March 2021) as current. The EP site gives 126 signatories as of October 2026.
- Which major US or Japanese banks are currently signatories, or claims that banks withdrew from the EP in 2024-2026. Not verified.
- A completion date for the IFC Performance Standards update. None has been published.
- Detailed OECD Common Approaches or ECA requirements. Not verified here.
- The 2006 Performance Standards effective date (30 April 2006). Treat as unverified unless checked.
- Any statement that the ESF applies to IFC or MIGA.
