# Anti-corruption and sanctions: FCPA (including 2025 developments), UK Bribery Act, ECCTA failure to prevent fraud, OFAC 50 percent rule, EU sanctions packages

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: EU 21st Russia sanctions package, 23 July 2026; UK Crime and Policing Act 2026 senior-manager attribution rule in force 29 June 2026; DOJ department-wide Corporate Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy, March 2026)

## Summary

Project finance transactions cross borders, use agents and local partners, and depend on state counterparties. That makes bribery, fraud and sanctions risks central to lender diligence (Ch 49) and to political-risk management (Ch 60). Three liability regimes matter most.

1. **United States: the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA).** It has anti-bribery provisions and accounting provisions (books and records, internal controls). The penalties are severe, and the Alternative Fines Act allows fines of up to twice the gain or loss. On 10 February 2025 Executive Order (EO) 14209 paused new FCPA investigations for 180 days pending new guidelines. On 9 June 2025 the Deputy Attorney General issued those guidelines. Enforcement now centres on four things: misconduct linked to cartels and transnational criminal organisations (TCOs), harm to identifiable US companies' fair competition, national security (including "key infrastructure or assets"), and serious misconduct with individual culpability. Routine, low-value courtesies are deprioritised. New investigations need sign-off from the Assistant Attorney General of the Criminal Division. The statute itself is unchanged. The DOJ's corporate self-disclosure policy, revised and made department-wide in March 2026, offers a declination path for voluntary self-disclosure, full cooperation and remediation.
2. **United Kingdom: Bribery Act 2010 and the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 (ECCTA).** The Bribery Act (in force 1 July 2011) creates general bribery offences, a foreign public official offence and the section 7 strict-liability offence of failing to prevent bribery. The only defence to section 7 is "adequate procedures". ECCTA's failure-to-prevent-fraud offence, in force since 1 September 2025, applies to large organisations and allows a "reasonable procedures" defence. Since 29 June 2026 the Crime and Policing Act 2026 has attributed any offence committed by a senior manager acting within their authority to the organisation. It replaced ECCTA's narrower economic-crime rule.
3. **Sanctions.** Under the US Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) 50 percent rule (guidance of 13 August 2014), any entity owned 50% or more in aggregate, directly or indirectly, by blocked persons is itself blocked. The UK uses "more than 50%" plus a control test and does not simply aggregate holdings. The EU has adopted 21 numbered Russia packages. The 21st, of 23 July 2026, brought more than 100 Russian banks under transaction bans. The 19th banned Russian liquefied natural gas (LNG) imports (long-term contracts from 1 January 2027), and the 18th and 21st set and then froze a dynamic oil price cap.

## Verified facts

### United States: FCPA statute and penalties (jurisdiction: US federal; current rule at 15 U.S.C. §§ 78dd-1, 78dd-2, 78dd-3, 78m, 78ff)

1. Criminal penalties under 15 U.S.C. § 78ff. For willful violations of the Securities Exchange Act (which includes the FCPA accounting provisions), the fine is up to USD 5,000,000 and imprisonment up to 20 years for individuals, and up to USD 25,000,000 for entities. For anti-bribery violations by issuers, the statutory fine is up to USD 2,000,000 per violation for entities, and up to USD 100,000 and 5 years' imprisonment for individuals. There is also a civil penalty of up to USD 10,000 (inflation-adjusted in practice). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Under the Alternative Fines Act (18 U.S.C. § 3571(d)), a defendant may be fined up to the greater of twice the gross gain or twice the gross loss from the offence. This is why actual FCPA corporate resolutions often far exceed the per-violation statutory caps. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. The FCPA has an exception for "facilitating and expediting payments" (for example 15 U.S.C. § 78dd-1(b)). It also has affirmative defences for reasonable and bona fide expenditures and for payments lawful under the written laws of the foreign country (§ 78dd-1(c)). The June 2025 DOJ guidelines expressly remind prosecutors of these provisions. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. The Foreign Extortion Prevention Act (FEPA, 18 U.S.C. § 1352) criminalises the "demand side" of foreign bribery, meaning foreign officials who demand or accept bribes. The DOJ released a FEPA addendum to the FCPA Resource Guide on 13 December 2024. The current Resource Guide is the Second Edition (November 2012, updated July 2020). [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]

### United States: 2025-2026 policy changes

5. On 10 February 2025 President Trump signed EO 14209, "Pausing Foreign Corrupt Practices Act Enforcement to Further American Economic and National Security". It was published in the Federal Register (document 2025-02736). [confidence: high] [source: 3, 5]
6. EO 14209 § 2 directed the Attorney General, for 180 days, to: (i) cease initiating new FCPA investigations or enforcement actions unless an individual exception is made; (ii) review all existing FCPA investigations and enforcement actions; and (iii) issue updated guidelines or policies. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. On 9 June 2025 Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche sent the Head of the Criminal Division a memorandum, "Guidelines for Investigations and Enforcement of the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA)", effective that day. Prosecutors must focus on individuals rather than "attribute nonspecific malfeasance to corporate structures", proceed expeditiously, and consider collateral consequences throughout an investigation. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
8. Under the June 2025 guidelines, all new FCPA investigations or enforcement actions must be authorised by the Assistant Attorney General for the Criminal Division or a more senior official. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
9. The guidelines set four non-exhaustive factors (paraphrased). [confidence: high] [source: 3]
   - (A) Cartels and TCOs: misconduct linked to cartels or TCOs, their money launderers or shell companies, or officials bribed by them.
   - (B) Fair opportunities for US companies: whether specific and identifiable US entities were deprived of fair access to compete or suffered economic injury.
   - (C) US national security: bribery involving "key infrastructure or assets", with the memo citing the EO's references to critical minerals and deep-water ports.
   - (D) Serious misconduct: substantial bribes, sophisticated concealment, fraud or obstruction tied to particular individuals, not "routine business practices" or "de minimis or low-dollar, generally accepted business courtesies".
10. The guidelines also tell prosecutors to weigh whether an appropriate foreign authority is willing and able to prosecute the same conduct. They state that the most significant FCPA actions historically have been "overwhelmingly" against foreign companies. They also state that the DOJ will not target companies on the basis of nationality. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
11. The DOJ Corporate Enforcement and Voluntary Self-Disclosure Policy (CEP) was updated in March 2026 (web page updated 10 March 2026). It applies to all corporate criminal matters handled by the DOJ except antitrust (15 U.S.C. §§ 1-38). Its three parts are as follows. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
   - Part I: declination if the company voluntarily self-disclosed, fully cooperated, timely and appropriately remediated, and has no aggravating circumstances. The company must still pay disgorgement or forfeiture and restitution, and all CEP declinations are public.
   - Part II ("near miss", for example a good-faith self-report that does not qualify as voluntary): a non-prosecution agreement absent particularly egregious or multiple aggravating circumstances, a term of under three years, no monitor, and a 50-75% reduction off the low end of the US Sentencing Guidelines fine range.
   - Part III: prosecutorial discretion, with a fine reduction of no more than 50%.
12. Under the CEP whistleblower exception, a company still qualifies for a declination if it self-reports within 120 days of receiving a whistleblower's internal report, even if the whistleblower reported to the DOJ first. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

### United Kingdom: Bribery Act 2010 (jurisdiction: England and Wales, Scotland, Northern Ireland; current text at legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/23)

13. The Act came into force on 1 July 2011. The Ministry of Justice guidance on adequate procedures was published in March 2011. Its six principles are proportionate procedures, top-level commitment, risk assessment, due diligence, communication (including training), and monitoring and review. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
14. Under section 7, a "relevant commercial organisation" commits an offence if a person "associated" with it bribes another person intending to obtain or retain business, or an advantage in the conduct of business, for it. The organisation's only defence is to prove it "had in place adequate procedures". [confidence: high] [source: 7]
15. "Relevant commercial organisation" covers UK-incorporated bodies and UK partnerships wherever they do business. It also covers any other body corporate or partnership, wherever formed, that "carries on a business, or part of a business, in any part of the United Kingdom". Under section 12(5) the section 7 offence is committed "irrespective of whether the acts or omissions ... take place in the United Kingdom or elsewhere". [confidence: high] [source: 7]
16. Penalties under section 11. For individuals (sections 1, 2 and 6 offences), up to 10 years' imprisonment on indictment and/or a fine. For organisations, a fine on indictment, which is unlimited in amount. Section 7 is punishable on indictment by a fine. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
17. Under section 12(4), the section 1, 2 and 6 offences reach acts done abroad by persons with a "close connection" to the UK. This includes British citizens, individuals ordinarily resident in the UK, and bodies incorporated in the UK. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

### United Kingdom: ECCTA failure to prevent fraud and senior-manager attribution

18. Under ECCTA section 199, a large organisation commits an offence if an associated person commits a fraud offence intending to benefit, directly or indirectly, the organisation, or a person to whom the associate provides services on its behalf. A parent is also liable where a subsidiary's employee commits fraud intending to benefit the parent. The organisation does not need to have actually benefited. [confidence: high] [source: 9, 10]
19. The offence came into force on 1 September 2025. Home Office guidance was first published on 6 November 2024 and updated on 10 October 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
20. "Large organisation" (ECCTA section 201) means meeting two or more of these in the financial year before the fraud: turnover of more than GBP 36 million, balance sheet total of more than GBP 18 million, more than 250 employees. Subsidiaries count towards group thresholds (section 202). [confidence: high] [source: 9, 10]
21. Defence: the organisation must prove, on the balance of probabilities, that it had reasonable prevention procedures, or that it was not reasonable to expect it to have any. The guidance sets out six principles: top-level commitment, risk assessment, proportionate risk-based procedures, due diligence, communication and training, and monitoring and review. Penalty: an unlimited fine on conviction on indictment. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
22. Base offences are listed in ECCTA Schedule 13 and include Fraud Act 2006 offences, false accounting, fraudulent trading and cheating the public revenue. Territorial nexus: one of the acts making up the fraud took place in the UK, or the gain or loss occurred in the UK. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
23. Associated persons include employees, agents, subsidiaries and anyone providing services "for or on behalf of" the organisation. Per Home Office guidance, those who merely provide services "to" the organisation (for example external lawyers or engineers) are excluded. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
24. Since 29 June 2026, section 250 of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 has applied a general rule. Where a senior manager of a body corporate or partnership, acting within the actual or apparent scope of their authority, commits any offence under UK law, the organisation also commits the offence. There is an exception where all the conduct occurs outside the UK and the organisation would not itself have committed the offence. The same Act omitted ECCTA sections 196-198, the earlier economic-crime-only version. [confidence: high] [source: 11, 12]

### Sanctions: OFAC 50 percent rule (jurisdiction: US; guidance at ofac.treasury.gov)

25. Under OFAC's "Revised Guidance on Entities Owned by Persons Whose Property and Interests in Property Are Blocked" (13 August 2014), any entity owned "in the aggregate, directly or indirectly, 50 percent or more by one or more blocked persons is itself considered to be a blocked person", whether or not it is listed. US persons generally may not deal with it unless authorised. [confidence: high] [source: 13]
26. The rule is ownership-based, not control-based. OFAC nonetheless advises caution for entities in which blocked persons have significant ownership below 50%, or which they may control by other means, because such entities "may be the subject of future designation or enforcement action". [confidence: high] [source: 13]
27. The UK (Office of Financial Sanctions Implementation, OFSI) treats an entity as owned or controlled if a designated person holds, directly or indirectly, "more than 50%" of shares or voting rights. It may also be owned or controlled through the right to appoint or remove a board majority, or where it is reasonable to expect the person could ensure affairs are conducted according to their wishes. OFSI does not simply aggregate different designated persons' holdings unless the holdings are subject to a joint arrangement. Guidance last updated 12 May 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 14]

### Sanctions: EU Russia packages (jurisdiction: EU; current rules in consolidated Council Regulations (EU) No 833/2014 and No 269/2014 on EUR-Lex)

28. Adoption dates of the numbered packages since 2022:
    - 2022: 1st on 23 February; 2nd on 25 February; 3rd on 28 February and 2 March; 4th on 15 March; 5th on 8 April; 6th on 3 June; 8th on 5 October; 9th on 16 December. A "maintenance and alignment" package on 21 July 2022 took the place of a numbered 7th.
    - 2023: 10th on 25 February; 11th on 23 June; 12th on 18 December.
    - 2024: 13th on 23 February; 14th on 24 June; 15th on 16 December.
    - 2025: 16th on 24 February; 17th on 20 May; 18th on 18 July; 19th on 23 October.
    - 2026: 20th on 23 April; 21st on 23 July.

    [confidence: high] [source: 15]
29. The 12th package (18 December 2023) required operators to prohibit by contract the re-export to Russia of certain sensitive goods (the "no Russia" clause). [confidence: high] [source: 16]
30. The 14th package (24 June 2024) added three measures. EU companies must use their best efforts to ensure that their third-country subsidiaries do not undermine EU sanctions. A "no Russia" clause applies to IP transfers. And EU operators gained a legal basis to claim compensation in Member State courts for damages caused by Russian companies linked to sanctions implementation. [confidence: high] [source: 17]
31. The 18th package (18 July 2025) did the following. [confidence: high] [source: 18]
    - It lowered the crude oil price cap from USD 60 to USD 47.6 per barrel and introduced an automatic, dynamic mechanism setting the cap 15% below the average Urals market price over the previous six months.
    - It imposed a transaction ban on Nord Stream 1 and 2.
    - It banned imports of refined products made from Russian crude in third countries.
    - It turned the messaging ban on certain Russian banks into a full transaction ban covering 45 banks.
    - It banned transactions with the Russian Direct Investment Fund (RDIF) and entities in which it invests.
    - It brought listed shadow-fleet vessels to 444.
32. The 19th package (23 October 2025) did the following. [confidence: high] [source: 19]
    - It banned imports of Russian LNG from 1 January 2027 for long-term contracts, and within six months of entry into force for short-term contracts.
    - It imposed full transaction bans on Rosneft and Gazprom Neft.
    - It added five Russian banks to the transaction ban.
    - It banned the Mir and SBP payment systems.
    - It sanctioned the A7A5 rouble-backed stablecoin.
    - It brought listed vessels to 557.
33. The 20th package (23 April 2026) did the following. [confidence: high] [source: 20]
    - It listed two Russian ports and, for the first time, a third-country port (Karimun Oil Terminal, Indonesia).
    - It laid the legal basis for a future maritime-services ban on Russian oil, with timing for the Council to decide in coordination with the G7.
    - It banned LNG terminal services, allowing EU operators to terminate long-term contracts with Russian operators.
    - It extended bank transaction bans to 70 Russian banks.
    - It imposed a sectoral ban on Russian crypto-asset service providers.
    - It activated the "anti-circumvention" tool for the first time.
    - It brought listed vessels to 632.
34. The 21st package (23 July 2026) did the following. [confidence: high] [source: 21]
    - It suspended the 18th-package price-cap adjustment mechanism for a year, until July 2027.
    - It extended the infrastructure transaction ban to refineries processing Russian oil, listing the Kulevi refinery with a six-month delay.
    - It brought more than 100 Russian banks under transaction bans, including a prohibition on financial messaging.
    - It created a third-country crypto-services ban.
    - It added legal protections for EU operators against retaliatory lawsuits. These include suing non-Russian counterparties in the EU for damages, EU court injunctions against enforcement of unlawful Russian judgments, and non-recognition of Russian sanctions-linked judgments.
35. Directive (EU) 2024/1226 (24 April 2024) requires Member States to criminalise violations of EU restrictive measures. The transposition deadline was 20 May 2025. For legal persons it requires maximum fines of at least 5% of total worldwide turnover or EUR 40 million for most offences, and 1% or EUR 8 million for certain others. [confidence: high] [source: 22]

## Financing and structure details (parties, tranches, amounts, tenors, guarantees, where verified)

Not a transaction sheet. These regimes show up in project documents in the following ways. The descriptions are standard market practice, not verified facts from a single source; writers should present them as illustrative.
- Loan agreements contain anti-corruption, anti-money-laundering and sanctions representations and covenants.
- Lenders require sanctions and ownership screening of sponsors, contractors and offtakers. Because of the 50 percent rule (US) and the more-than-50%-plus-control test (UK and EU), this screening must look through the ownership chain.
- Mandatory prepayment and event-of-default provisions are triggered by sanctions designation.
- Lenders run due diligence on agents and local partners. Under ECCTA and the Bribery Act, an agent "associated" with the borrower can create liability for the borrower itself.

## What went wrong or right, and why (well-established analysis only, attributed to named sources)

- The DOJ itself, in the June 2025 memo, frames FCPA enforcement as protecting US companies' fair access to compete and US national security in "key infrastructure or assets". This is a direct signal that infrastructure and critical-minerals bribery remains a priority area even under the narrowed policy. [source: 3]
- The UK has moved in the opposite direction to the US pause. It has widened corporate liability through a new failure-to-prevent offence (2025) and a general senior-manager attribution rule (2026). For multinational sponsors the practical effect is that compliance programmes cannot be relaxed on the basis of US policy alone. This is an inference from sources 9-12, not a named commentator's view.

## Teaching angles by chapter (for each listed chapter: the transferable lesson and the angle to take, 2–4 sentences)

- **Chapter 49 (Legal, insurance, model, tax and integrity diligence):** Teach integrity diligence as an ownership-and-agency mapping exercise. The steps are to trace beneficial ownership to apply the US "50% or more aggregate" test and the UK "more than 50% or control" test, to identify every "associated person" (agents, joint-venture partners, subcontractors acting on the project company's behalf), and to test the borrower's "adequate" or "reasonable" procedures against the six-principle frameworks. Use the 2025 DOJ guidelines to show that enforcement priorities change while statutes do not, so diligence must be built on the statutes.
- **Chapter 60 (Political risk and its protection):** Present corruption and sanctions as forms of political risk that can strand a project overnight. The 19th package's LNG ban and the 20th package's terminal-services ban both cut long-term contracts by law. Contrast the US policy shift (EO 14209 and the June 2025 guidelines) with the UK's expansion of corporate liability, and teach the reader to plan for the strictest regime that applies. Use the 21st package's anti-retaliation protections to show how sanctions now shape dispute strategy.

## Do not state (claims commonly repeated but unverified or wrong, and uncertain details)

- Do not say the FCPA was "repealed" or "suspended". The statute is unchanged. Only new DOJ enforcement initiation was paused for 180 days, and the June 2025 guidelines then governed enforcement.
- Do not state whether the EO's 180-day period was extended, how many existing cases were closed under the review, or SEC FCPA enforcement policy since 2025. None of these was verified.
- Do not describe the OFAC rule as covering "control". It does not, although OFAC warns about control.
- Do not state the exact USD figure of the individual anti-bribery fine as "USD 250,000" without explaining that this comes from the Alternative Fines Act general felony cap (18 U.S.C. § 3571(b)). The FCPA text says USD 100,000. The 18 U.S.C. § 3571(b) cap was not separately verified here.
- Do not give the Federal Register publication date of EO 14209 as definitive. It is inferred from the document URL (14 February 2025); medium confidence.
- Do not state the date of Royal Assent of the Crime and Policing Act 2026 (not verified).
- Do not state current OFAC or EU listing counts beyond those quoted above for each package.
- Do not state any EU anti-corruption directive as adopted or in force; it was not verified here.
