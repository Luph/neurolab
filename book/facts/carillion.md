# Carillion collapse (2018) and its PPP portfolio

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: FCA Final Notices to Carillion plc and former chief executive Richard Howson, 16 February 2026; latest project event: Midland Metropolitan University Hospital opened 6 October 2024)

## Summary

Carillion plc was a UK construction, facilities-management and project-finance group with operations in the UK, Canada and the Middle East. It described itself as a world leader in PPPs, having financially closed 66 PPP projects, and it typically invested equity in a PPP project company and also took the construction and long-term services subcontracts. On 10 July 2017 it announced an £845 million contract provision, most of it tied to construction contracts. It raised the provision to £1,045 million in September 2017 and warned of covenant breaches in November 2017. It went into compulsory liquidation on 15 January 2018 with liabilities of nearly £7 billion and £29 million in cash. Three of its biggest loss-makers were PPP construction contracts: the Royal Liverpool University Hospital, the Midland Metropolitan Hospital and the Aberdeen Western Peripheral Route (AWPR). The two PFI hospitals show what happens when a thin-equity project company loses its fixed-price contractor in mid-construction. Neither project company could fund completion. The government refused a bailout and terminated both PFI contracts in 2018. It paid £42 million to the Liverpool lenders and nothing to the Midland lenders, and finished both hospitals with public money years late. The NAO estimated that investors, their insurers and Carillion lost at least £603 million on the two hospitals. On the AWPR, a joint-venture road project, the surviving partners Balfour Beatty and Galliford Try were obliged to finish the work and absorb Carillion's share.

## Verified facts

1. Carillion plc went into liquidation on 15 January 2018. The Official Receiver began liquidating its assets and contracts. The group employed around 45,000 people (NAO), including around 18,200 in the UK at liquidation. The parliamentary committees' report gives about 43,000 employees, 19,000 of them in the UK. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. At liquidation Carillion had around 420 UK public-sector contracts, including direct contracts, subcontracts and special purpose vehicles delivering private finance schemes. By value it was central government's sixth-largest supplier in 2017. Public-sector contracts made up 33% of its total revenue and 45% of its UK revenue. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Carillion's 2016 total revenue (including its share of joint ventures) was £5,214.2 million. Its PPP projects segment had revenue of £313.0 million (2015: £192.8 million) and underlying operating profit of £28.3 million (2015: £49.3 million). [confidence: high] [source: 4]
4. In the 2016 Annual Report, Carillion said it had financially closed 66 PPP projects since the procurement form began. At 31 December 2016 it held a portfolio of 16 financially closed projects. It had invested £11.8 million of equity in them and was committed to invest a further £67.7 million. The directors valued the portfolio at £31.3 million at a 9% discount rate, or £50.2 million at 7%, which the company said was close to the average discount rate it achieved on equity sales. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. PPP equity sales were a recurring source of profit. In 2016 Carillion sold equity in three projects for cash proceeds of approximately £48 million (£48.2 million), a discount rate of just under 7%, and booked a pre-tax profit of £12.7 million. The comparable 2015 profit was £37.7 million. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
6. In its H1 2017 results (29 September 2017), Carillion said it had made a strategic decision to stop bidding for PPP construction and to exit all PPP construction markets once existing contracts were completed. At 30 June 2017 it held 15 financially closed PPP projects with £27 million of equity invested and a further £52 million committed. The directors valued these at about £46 million at a 9% discount rate and £65 million at 7%. In H1 2017 it invested £15 million of equity in the Royal Liverpool Hospital PPP project. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
7. On 10 July 2017 Carillion announced an £845 million contract provision. The FCA records that £375 million related to Carillion Construction Services (CCS), the UK construction business, and £470 million related to overseas markets. Carillion's share price fell 39% on the day and 70% within three days. On 29 September 2017 the H1 results added a further £200 million provision for support services contracts, which brought the total to £1,045 million. On 17 November 2017 Carillion announced that it expected to breach its loan covenants. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 5, 6]
8. The FCA found that £240 million of the £375 million CCS provision was taken against four projects: Royal Liverpool University Hospital, Battersea Power Station Phase 1, Midland Metropolitan Hospital and AWPR. Internal project teams had flagged the gaps between their assessments and budget well before July 2017. By April 2017, the gap was £72 million at Royal Liverpool, over £33 million at Midland Metropolitan and over £85 million at AWPR. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
9. The NAO reported Carillion's estimated 2017 losses as £91 million on its joint-venture project to build the Aberdeen bypass (AWPR), £83 million on the Royal Liverpool University Hospital and £48 million on the Midland Metropolitan Hospital. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. In early January 2018 Carillion asked the government for £223 million to see it through to April 2018. This comprised a £160 million loan or guarantee and a £63 million tax deferral. It also asked the Cabinet Office for up to £125 million towards completing the Midland Metropolitan PFI scheme in return for an equity stake. The Cabinet Office refused and instead funded a "trading liquidation", with £150 million of initial liquidity to the Official Receiver. The NAO estimated the loss on the liquidation at £148 million. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. The joint committee report (HC 769, published 16 May 2018) found that Carillion left a pension liability of around £2.6 billion affecting 27,000 defined-benefit members. It owed around £2 billion to some 30,000 suppliers, subcontractors and other short-term creditors, and enforced standard payment terms of 120 days. It paid a record dividend of £79 million on its 2016 results. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
12. Royal Liverpool and Midland Metropolitan were PFI projects. Their project companies were The Hospital Company (Liverpool) Ltd and The Hospital Company (Sandwell) Ltd. Together they were to be financed by £57 million from shareholders (including Carillion), £395 million of loans (mainly banks) and £215 million of government funding. Each project company had a fixed-price contract with Carillion. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
13. Status when Carillion collapsed: Midland Metropolitan (669 beds, Sandwell) was about two-thirds complete, with an original opening date of October 2018. Royal Liverpool (646 beds) was almost complete, with an original opening date of June 2017. Both were already running about a year late. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
14. The NAO found that the project companies had been designed with very little contingency funding of their own and relied on Carillion to absorb cost rises. Once Carillion failed, neither project company could fund completion. At Royal Liverpool, Arup's review (commissioned April 2018) found that the structure needed major strengthening and also found fire-protection and cladding problems. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
15. HM Treasury, the Cabinet Office, the Department of Health & Social Care and NHS Improvement rejected rescue packages for the project companies. They did not want to set a bailout precedent or risk PFI debt being reclassified as national debt. They also found no investor or contractor appetite for a new PFI. The Sandwell Trust terminated its PFI contract in July 2018 and the Liverpool Trust in October 2018. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
16. Termination compensation differed between the two. The Midland lenders received no payment. The Department of Health & Social Care funded a £42 million payment to the Royal Liverpool lenders, settled in September 2018 on a cost-to-complete estimate of £117 million (present value). That estimate rose to £164 million by January 2019, a level at which, the NAO said, no termination payment would have been due under the contract. A later estimate put the cost to complete at £293 million. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
17. Balfour Beatty was the only viable bidder to complete Midland Metropolitan. It signed with the Sandwell Trust in December 2019 at a target price. Laing O'Rourke was appointed at Royal Liverpool as a managing contractor paid a fee, without a public procurement. The NAO noted that it had no contractual incentive to manage cost. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
18. The NAO estimated total cost to build and run in January 2020 as follows. Midland Metropolitan went from £686 million to £988 million (+44%), with the public-sector share up 3%. Royal Liverpool went from £746 million to £1,063 million (+42%), with the public-sector share down 1%. Private-sector losses were estimated at £279 million and £324 million respectively, at least £603 million in total. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
19. Royal Liverpool University Hospital opened to patients in October 2022, after Laing O'Rourke handed it over on 28 September 2022. Midland Metropolitan University Hospital opened on 6 October 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 9, 10]
20. Aberdeen Roads Limited delivered AWPR as a design-build-finance-operate project. Balfour Beatty, Carillion and Galliford Try were equal partners in the construction joint venture. After Carillion's collapse, Balfour Beatty and Galliford Try were obliged to complete the contract and shared the funding shortfall equally. The full 58 km route opened on 19 February 2019. [confidence: high for opening date and JV composition; medium for the equal sharing of the shortfall, which rests on trade press] [source: 7, 8]
21. On 12 October 2023 the Financial Reporting Council fined KPMG £21 million over its Carillion audits, reduced from £30 million for cooperation. Former audit partner Peter Meehan was fined £350,000 and excluded from ICAEW membership for 10 years. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
22. On 16 February 2026 the FCA publicly censured Carillion plc (in liquidation) for market abuse and Listing Rules breaches between 1 July 2016 and 10 July 2017. It said it would otherwise have imposed a penalty of £37,910,000. The FCA found that overly aggressive contract-accounting judgements had not complied with IAS 11. Former finance directors Richard Adam and Zafar Khan were fined £232,800 and £138,900 (Final Notices first published 7 January 2026). Former chief executive Richard Howson settled for £237,700 (Final Notice February 2026). [confidence: high for the company censure and Adam/Khan fines; medium for the exact Howson figure, sourced from a law-firm bulletin] [source: 6, 12, 13]

## Financing and structure details

- **Carillion's PPP model:** Carillion took a minority equity stake in each project company and also held the construction and support-services subcontracts. It recycled capital by selling mature equity stakes, at discount rates of about 7% in 2016–2017 [4, 5]. Its PPP equity book was small: about £27 million invested at mid-2017 [5]. The exposure that sank it was the construction subcontracts, not the equity.
- **The two PFI hospitals combined:** £57 million of shareholder equity, £395 million of senior loans (mainly banks) and £215 million of public capital contribution. The public contribution was unusually high for PFI, intended to reduce private finance and improve affordability. That is why the public sector's total cost moved little when the PFIs were terminated [2].
- **Termination mechanics (Royal Liverpool):** Under the contract, lender compensation on termination during construction depended mainly on the estimated cost to complete, before that cost was known. The contract also let lenders delay construction for several months while compensation was negotiated. The departments chose a quick consensual settlement (£42 million) to recover the site quickly [2].
- **AWPR:** this was a joint-venture construction structure, unlike the hospitals' single contractor. Under the joint-venture obligations, the surviving partners had to complete the works [1, 7].

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Thin project companies with fixed-price contracts move risk to the contractor's balance sheet, not away from the project.** The NAO said the PFI companies "pass the risks to their contractors" and keep little contingency. They could carry on only until the cost to complete exceeded the funds available. Past that tipping point the risk came back to the public sector [2].
- **Design and pricing.** The NAO could not establish why the design problems arose. It recorded NHS England and NHS Improvement's view that Carillion's original pricing may have been too low for the specification [2].
- **Contract accounting and governance.** The FCA found that aggressive IAS 11 judgements on a few large contracts (including three PPP construction contracts) kept reported profit up while internal teams were flagging losses [6]. The parliamentary committees called the company's business model "a relentless dash for cash" and criticized its board, its auditor KPMG and the FRC and Pensions Regulator [3].
- **Badly designed construction-phase termination.** The NAO concluded that the PFI contracts were not well designed for termination during construction. If the departments had better understood the Liverpool cost to complete, they might have paid nothing [2].
- **Joint-venture structures contained the damage.** The NAO noted that in all but one case of the new contracts awarded after the July 2017 warning, JV partners were obliged to finish if Carillion failed [1]. AWPR was completed by the surviving partners [7].

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 23 (Construction structures beyond the single EPC):** Compare the single-contractor PFI hospitals with the three-party AWPR joint venture. Joint-and-several JV obligations meant AWPR's surviving partners had to finish, while the hospitals' project companies had no second balance sheet to call on. Use this to teach why lenders look through a fixed price to the contractor's credit, why they require parent guarantees, bonds and wraps, and why JV structure matters.
- **Ch 61 (Construction to completion):** This is the definitive contractor-distress case. Walk through the sequence: warning signs in internal cost-to-complete data, the collapse, site security funded by the Cabinet Office, the search for a replacement, the discovery of latent defects, and termination. Stress three lessons. Lenders' technical advisers tracked progress but gave no assurance on design (NAO). A replacement contractor will not take latent-defect risk on another firm's work. Cost-to-complete estimates made in a hurry drive compensation.
- **Ch 81 (Social infrastructure):** Show the limits of PFI risk transfer in hospitals. The public sector paid about 1% more in real terms while private parties lost at least £603 million. Even so, patients waited roughly four to six years longer than planned, and the trusts took on uncapped latent-defect risk. Discuss why government would not rescue the project companies (precedent and national-accounts classification) and how a high public capital contribution changes the economics of termination.

## Do not state

- Do not give a single "total cost of Carillion's collapse to the taxpayer". The NAO's £148 million is the estimated liquidation loss covered by the Cabinet Office. It explicitly said total taxpayer cost would be higher [1].
- Do not say Carillion owned the hospital project companies outright, or give its percentage stakes in The Hospital Company (Liverpool) or (Sandwell). The NAO says only that the shareholders included Carillion.
- Do not state Carillion's PPP equity sale prices after 2017 or what the liquidators realized on the PPP equity portfolio. Not verified.
- Do not state the AWPR final cost precisely. "At least £1 billion" against an original £745 million is trade-press reporting (medium). Do not attribute specific write-down figures to Galliford Try's 2018 rights issue as AWPR-only.
- Do not state director disqualification outcomes from the Insolvency Service proceedings. Not verified here.
- The FCA final notice states the July 2017 provision "as at 30 July 2017". Other sources say the provision was at 30 June 2017. Avoid stating the as-at date.
- Do not give Midland Metropolitan's final out-turn cost as "£988 million". That is the NAO's January 2020 estimated cost to build and run, which some press reports repeat as the hospital's cost.
- Do not quote parliamentary language beyond the short verified phrases, such as "a relentless dash for cash" (HC 769 summary).
