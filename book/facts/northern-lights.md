# Northern Lights CCS (Norway) and UK CCS clusters

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: Northern Lights' agreement with Öresundskraft, 1 October 2026; delivery of the fourth Phase 1 ship, Northern Purpose, July 2026; UK Viking CCUS development-fund subsidy report by the CMA Subsidy Advice Unit, 15 April 2026)

## Summary

Northern Lights is the CO2 transport and storage part of Longship, the Norwegian state's full-scale carbon capture and storage demonstration. It is a joint venture owned equally by Equinor, Shell and TotalEnergies. Ships carry liquefied CO2 from emitters to a receiving terminal at Øygarden near Bergen, and from there a pipeline of roughly 100 to 110 km takes it to the Aurora storage site, where it is injected about 2,600 metres below the North Sea seabed. Phase 1 has 1.5 million tonnes a year of capacity and was fully booked. The partners made a conditional investment decision in May 2020 and a final one in December 2020, once the Storting (parliament) had approved state funding. Under state-aid agreements the Norwegian state covers 80 percent of Northern Lights' base investment, 50 percent of specified additional investment, and 75 to 100 percent of eligible operating costs for ten years, up to caps. At the investment decision Longship as a whole was estimated at NOK 25.1 billion (2021 money) including ten years of operation, with a state share of NOK 16.8 billion. The facilities opened in September 2024, and the first CO2 (from Heidelberg Materials' Brevik cement plant) was injected on 25 August 2025. In March 2025 the partners took a NOK 7.5 billion final investment decision on Phase 2, which takes capacity to at least 5 Mtpa from the second half of 2028. Phase 2 is supported by a EUR 131 million EU Connecting Europe Facility grant and anchored by a 15-year, 900,000 tonnes-a-year contract with Stockholm Exergi. The UK took a different route. It selected the HyNet and East Coast clusters as "Track-1" in 2021, and on 4 October 2024 the government committed GBP 21.7 billion over 25 years. Net Zero Teesside Power and the Northern Endurance Partnership reached financial close on 10 December 2024, under contracts with the Low Carbon Contracts Company and an Ofgem economic licence. Eni's HyNet transport and storage network followed on 24 April 2025. The June 2025 Spending Review then allocated GBP 9.4 billion to CCUS and development funding for the Track-2 Acorn and Viking projects.

## Verified facts

### Northern Lights and Longship

1. Longship comprises Heidelberg Materials' Brevik CCS capture project (cement), the Northern Lights JV's transport and storage project, and Hafslund Celsio's Oslo CCS capture project (waste-to-energy). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Equinor, Shell and Total made a conditional investment decision on Northern Lights in May 2020. The Norwegian government announced funding on 15 December 2020 after the vote in parliament, and the investment decision was then confirmed. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
3. At the investment decision, Longship's total cost was estimated at NOK 25.1 billion (in 2021 currency), covering investment plus ten years of operation for all three participants. The state's management framework (P50) was NOK 16.8 billion, roughly two-thirds of total cost, as set out in White Paper No. 33 (2019–2020) and the 2020 KS2 external quality assurance by Oslo Economics and Atkins (24 June 2020). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4]
4. At the investment decision (Prop. 1 S (2020–2021)) the Northern Lights investment estimate was NOK 9.1 billion including base and additional investments, with annual operating costs of NOK 477 million. Brevik CCS was estimated at NOK 3.2 billion investment and NOK 119 million a year in operating costs. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. The state signed a separate state-aid agreement with each participant. There are no bilateral agreements between the participants: the state carries the cross-chain risk that another part of the chain fails to progress. For Northern Lights the state covers 80 percent of base investments and 50 percent of specifically defined additional investments. For Heidelberg Materials it covers 100 percent of investment up to approximately NOK 1.2 billion and 75 percent beyond that, up to a defined maximum. It also covers 75 to 100 percent of specifically defined eligible operating costs for ten years, up to a specified maximum. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. Supplementary quality assurance reviews were carried out in 2022 and 2023. The 2023 review found that Northern Lights appeared to be staying within its previously estimated expected investment costs once price inflation was taken into account. Brevik's costs, by contrast, rose by approximately NOK 850 million compared with KS2 by 2022, and further after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. In 2023 Heidelberg Materials agreed to complete Brevik and cover the increased costs in exchange for keeping a larger share of potential returns, and the state committed a start-up grant of up to NOK 150 million. Gassnova puts average price increases from 2020 to 2024 at 19 percent. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. Gassnova's autumn 2024 forecast of Northern Lights base investments: transport ships NOK 1.3 billion, CO2 receiving terminal NOK 2.8 billion, permanent storage NOK 4.1 billion (NOK 8.2 billion in total). The Longship total including Brevik (integration NOK 2.2 billion; capture, compression and intermediate storage NOK 2.7 billion) is NOK 13.1 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. Capacities are deliberately mismatched. Brevik emits 0.8 Mtpa and captures 0.4 Mtpa. The Øygarden terminal can take up to 1.5 Mtpa. The pipeline to the storage site is designed for 5 Mtpa. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. The Northern Lights JV is equally owned by Equinor, Shell and TotalEnergies, and Equinor acts as Technical Service Provider. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
10. Exploitation licence EL001 "Aurora" was awarded in January 2019, and the Eos confirmation well was drilled in March 2020. Facilities were completed in 2024 and officially opened in September 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
11. In 2022 Northern Lights signed what it describes as the world's first commercial agreement for cross-border CO2 transport and storage, with Yara International (for CO2 from the Netherlands). [confidence: high for the agreement; volumes not verified here] [source: 6, 7]
12. On 25 August 2025 the first CO2, transported by ship from Heidelberg Materials' Brevik cement plant, was injected into the Aurora reservoir about 2,600 metres below the seabed. CO2 travels from the Øygarden terminal by pipeline (described as 100 km in the August 2025 release and as 110 km offshore in the October 2026 release). [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8, 11]
13. Phase 1 capacity is 1.5 Mtpa and was described as fully booked by March 2025. The Norwegian government covers approximately 80 percent of Phase 1 costs (Equinor's wording). [confidence: high] [source: 5, 8]
14. On 27 March 2025 Equinor, Shell and TotalEnergies took the final investment decision on Phase 2. The investment is NOK 7.5 billion, which includes a EUR 131 million (approximately NOK 1.5 billion) grant from the EU Connecting Europe Facility (CEF Energy), approved in June 2024. Phase 2 lifts capacity from 1.5 Mtpa to at least 5 Mtpa and adds onshore tanks, a new jetty, more injection wells and ships. It is expected to be operational in the second half of 2028. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 9]
15. Phase 2 is anchored by a commercial agreement with Stockholm Exergi for 900,000 tonnes a year of biogenic CO2 for 15 years from 2028. At that time Stockholm Exergi was the fifth customer, after Heidelberg Materials, Hafslund Celsio, Yara and Ørsted. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 9]
16. Four Phase 1 ships, Northern Pioneer, Northern Pathfinder, Northern Phoenix and Northern Purpose, were built by Dalian Shipbuilding Offshore Company. The fourth, Northern Purpose, arrived in Norway in July 2026. Larger CO2 ships are planned for Phase 2 from 2028. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
17. On 1 October 2026 Northern Lights signed an agreement with Öresundskraft for up to 200,000 tonnes a year of CO2 from the Filbornaverket waste-to-energy plant in Helsingborg, Sweden. The CO2 will be trucked to the Port of Halland (Halmstad) and shipped to Øygarden, with start-up targeted for Q4 2029. Northern Lights says it will then have seven industrial customers in four countries: Heidelberg Materials, Hafslund Celsio and Inherit (Norway); Yara (Netherlands); Ørsted (Denmark); and Stockholm Exergi and Öresundskraft (Sweden). [confidence: high] [source: 11]

### UK CCS clusters

18. The UK used a "cluster sequencing" process. Phase 1, launched in 2021, selected HyNet (North West England and North Wales) and the East Coast Cluster (Teesside and Humber) as Track-1. In March 2023 the previous government named eight emitter projects in ECC and HyNet to go forward to commercial negotiation. In July 2023 it named Acorn (North East Scotland) and Viking (Humber) as the Track-2 clusters. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
19. On 4 October 2024 the government announced GBP 21.7 billion available over 25 years for the first CCUS projects in Teesside and Merseyside. It said this would bring approximately GBP 8 billion of private investment and remove over 8.5 million tonnes of emissions a year. [confidence: high] [source: 13]
20. On 10 December 2024 contracts were signed and financial close reached for the East Coast Cluster's first projects: Net Zero Teesside (NZT) Power, a gas-fired power station with carbon capture, and the Northern Endurance Partnership (NEP), the CO2 transport and storage network. This was the first CCUS financial close in the UK. Construction was due to start in mid-2025, and NZT Power was expected to provide low-carbon power from 2028. [confidence: high] [source: 12, 14]
21. Ownership as of December 2024: NZT Power was 75 percent bp and 25 percent Equinor. NEP was 45 percent bp, 45 percent Equinor and 10 percent TotalEnergies, with bp providing operating services to both. [confidence: high] [source: 14]
22. The Low Carbon Contracts Company (LCCC) is the government-owned counterparty to the CCUS business models, namely the Dispatchable Power Agreement (DPA) for NZT Power and the Revenue Support Agreement for transport and storage. Ofgem is the economic regulator for the CCUS transport and storage licence. On 10 December 2024 the North Sea Transition Authority (NSTA) awarded the UK's first carbon storage permit, for the Endurance store. The government said about GBP 4 billion of supply-chain contracts would be awarded by ECC projects. It stressed that this figure was not government funding. [confidence: high] [source: 14]
23. The CCS Network Code's implementation date is 10 December 2024. The government publishes and updates the business-model documents: DPA (full contract November 2022), Industrial Carbon Capture (ICC) and Waste ICC contracts (updated November 2025), and a Transition Access Agreement policy position (July 2026). [confidence: high] [source: 15]
24. On 24 April 2025 Eni finalized a deal with the government for its Liverpool Bay CCS transport and storage project (HyNet), which reached financial close. Eni was expected to award around GBP 2 billion in supply-chain contracts, and the NSTA awarded Eni three carbon storage permits the same day. [confidence: high] [source: 12, 16]
25. The June 2025 Spending Review (announced 11–12 June 2025) gave UK carbon capture GBP 9.4 billion over the Parliament. It met in full a development-funding request of around GBP 200 million for Acorn, subject to business case, and provided development funding for Viking. [confidence: high] [source: 17]
26. On 25 September 2025 the first two HyNet anchor capture projects signed contracts with LCCC, which marked their final investment decisions. They are Heidelberg Materials UK's Padeswood cement plant (approximately 800,000 tonnes of CO2 a year) and Encyclis's Protos waste-to-energy facility at Ellesmere Port, together approximately 1.2 Mtpa. [confidence: high] [source: 18]
27. In March 2026 DESNZ referred a proposed direct grant subsidy (the "Viking CCUS Development Fund") to the CMA's Subsidy Advice Unit. The grant would go to Chrysaor Production UK Ltd (owned by Harbour Energy), the Viking transport and storage developer, for about 2.5 years from April 2026, paid on costs actually incurred. The SAU published its report on 15 April 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 19]

## Financing and structure details

- Northern Lights Phase 1: equity-funded by the three JV partners (one-third each) with Norwegian state aid. The state pays 80 percent of base investment and 50 percent of defined additional investment, plus 75 to 100 percent of eligible operating costs for ten years, all capped. No project-finance debt has been identified. Investment estimate NOK 9.1 billion at FID, with the autumn 2024 base-investment forecast at NOK 8.2 billion. [source: 1]
- Chain risk allocation: separate state-aid agreements and no bilateral contracts between capture and storage participants, so the state absorbs the cross-chain interface risk. [source: 1]
- Phase 2: NOK 7.5 billion, including the EUR 131 million CEF Energy grant, with a 15-year Stockholm Exergi contract as anchor. [source: 5, 9]
- UK model: an Ofgem-regulated economic licence for transport and storage, revenue support through LCCC (Revenue Support Agreement for T&S; DPA for power; ICC contracts for industrial emitters), and a government budget envelope of GBP 21.7 billion over 25 years. Lenders and debt amounts for NZT Power and NEP are not verified here. [source: 12, 13, 14, 15]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Right, per Gassnova: separate state-aid agreements in which the state takes cross-chain risk made the chain investable even though no participant would accept the others' delivery risk. The transport and storage element (Northern Lights) stayed within expected cost after allowing for inflation, according to the 2023 supplementary quality assurance. [source: 1]
- Cost growth landed on capture, not on storage. Brevik's overrun was blamed on overconfidence in project maturity at KS2, underestimated complexity, unfamiliarity with site conditions, COVID-19 and price rises. The fix was to renegotiate the risk and return split with Heidelberg Materials. [source: 1]
- Oversizing the infrastructure was deliberate. The terminal and the 5 Mtpa pipeline were built ahead of contracted demand, so Phase 2 could be sanctioned without new pipe. Gassnova shows how scaling changes unit costs. [source: 1]
- Schedule: the 2020 plan foresaw Phase 1 completion in mid-2024, but first injection waited until August 2025, when capture at Brevik was ready. Chain dependency, not storage readiness, set the start date. [source: 3, 8]
- The UK reached its first CCUS financial close only in December 2024; the current government's own releases refer to "years of delay" under previous governments. Structurally, the UK model supports projects through long-term revenue contracts with LCCC and an Ofgem-regulated transport and storage licence, whereas Norway used capped capital and operating grants. Which allocates more risk to the taxpayer is a matter for analysis; do not present it as a sourced finding. [source: 14, 17, 18]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 83 (Hydrogen and derivatives, CCS and sustainable fuels): use Northern Lights to teach cross-chain risk in CCS. Each link (capture, shipping, terminal, storage) has different owners, and no one will finance its own piece if another link can strand it. Show how Norway solved this with capped state aid covering capex and opex and the state absorbing interface risk. Contrast that with the UK's contract-based model: an Ofgem-licensed T&S company paid through a Revenue Support Agreement, emitters on DPA or ICC contracts with LCCC, and the GBP 21.7 billion budget envelope. Then use Phase 2 (third-party customers, a 15-year transport-and-storage service contract, EU grant) to show how a demonstration becomes a commercial "storage as a service" business. Include the Brevik overrun and its renegotiation to show that capture-side cost risk is where projects break.

## Do not state

- That Northern Lights used project-finance debt, or any lender names, for either Northern Lights or the UK's NZT Power and NEP: not verified.
- Yara and Ørsted contract volumes and start years (often cited as 800,000 tonnes a year from 2025 and 430,000 tonnes a year from 2026): not verified here.
- "Approximately USD 700 million" or similar dollar figures for Phase 2 or Phase 1: convert only from verified NOK figures with a stated date and rate.
- That the NOK 25.1 billion is the cost of Northern Lights alone: it is the whole Longship estimate including ten years of operation.
- That Hafslund Celsio's Oslo capture plant is operating or its cost: not covered by Gassnova's cost report and not verified here.
- A specific date for the formal establishment of the JV (often cited as March 2021): not verified.
- Storage depth: sources variously say 2,500 m and 2,600 m below the seabed. Use "about 2,600 metres" (Northern Lights and Equinor releases), and do not mix the two.
- Totals of CO2 injected to date: not published in the sources reviewed.
- Contract prices or tariffs per tonne for any Northern Lights customer, or LCCC strike prices for UK projects: not public or not verified.
- Any claim that the GBP 21.7 billion is a cash grant: it is a 25-year funding commitment across revenue-support mechanisms.
