# UK Private Finance Initiative (PFI), PF2, the 2018 end of new PFI, and refinancing gains

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: April 2023 publication of HM Treasury/IPA portfolio data as at 31 March 2021; policy events end with the 29 October 2018 Budget)

## Summary

The Private Finance Initiative was the United Kingdom's main public-private partnership (PPP) model for social and transport infrastructure for about 25 years. HM Treasury opened the door in 1989 by retiring the "Ryrie Rules" for privately financed road investment, and in 1992 extended the approach to other sectors under the name "Private Finance Initiative" (NAO). In a PFI deal a special purpose vehicle (SPV) borrows to design, build, finance and usually maintain an asset such as a school, hospital or road, and the public authority pays a performance-related "unitary charge" over a contract of typically 25 to 30 years. Use fell after the 2008 financial crisis raised private finance costs. HM Treasury consulted on reform in 2011 and relaunched the model as PF2 on 5 December 2012, adding a minority public equity stake (typically 10%), equity-return transparency and equity funding competitions. PF2 was used only six times. In the 29 October 2018 Budget the Chancellor, Philip Hammond, announced that the government would no longer use PF2 or PFI for new projects in the UK government's (non-devolved) remit, while honouring existing contracts. Refinancing gains were a recurring controversy: early contracts gave the public sector no right to share, the Fazakerley prison (1999) and Norfolk and Norwich hospital (2003) refinancings drew sharp criticism from the National Audit Office (NAO) and the Public Accounts Committee (PAC), and gain-sharing was progressively hard-wired into standard contract terms (a voluntary 30% code for early deals, at least 50% in later contracts, and 70% above GBP 3 million for deals agreed since 2009, before Eurostat accounting rules pushed HM Treasury to cap the PF2 public share at 33%). As at 31 March 2021, 694 PFI/PF2 projects with a capital value of GBP 54.7 billion remained, with GBP 152.2 billion of unitary charge payments still to be paid; contract expiries are expected to peak at 59 in 2036, and the last two expire in 2048.

## Verified facts

1. HM Treasury made PFI possible in 1989 when it retired the "Ryrie Rules" (which had discouraged privately financed public-sector projects) and allowed additional privately financed investment in roads. In 1992 the approach was extended to other sectors and the name "Private Finance Initiative" was used for the first time. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. In a PFI or PF2 deal an SPV borrows to construct the asset, and the taxpayer makes payments over the contract term (typically 25 to 30 years) that cover debt repayment, financing costs, maintenance and any other services. More than 90% of UK government capital investment is publicly financed. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. The Department of Health and Social Care provides a Deed of Safeguard for PFI health deals, which guarantees PFI payments. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. NAO report "PFI and PF2" (HC 718, Session 2017-2019; signed by the Comptroller and Auditor General on 12 January 2018 and published 18 January 2018): more than 700 operational PFI and PF2 deals had a capital value of around GBP 60 billion (716 projects under construction or in operation, GBP 59.4 billion), annual charges were GBP 10.3 billion in 2016-17, and future charges to the 2040s totalled GBP 199 billion even with no new deals. The figures were based on HM Treasury's database as at 31 March 2016. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. The NAO noted that the briefing was prepared before the 15 January 2018 announcement that the construction company Carillion had gone into liquidation. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. HM Treasury published "PF2: A new approach to public private partnerships" on 5 December 2012, following the reform of PFI. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. PF2 changes (NAO): a public minority equity stake in all deals, typically 10% of the equity, managed by the Infrastructure and Projects Authority (IPA); publication of actual and forecast equity returns; optional equity funding competitions; the public sector retaining risk of change in law, utilities costs and site contamination; an 18-month maximum tendering phase; and a control total limiting PFI/PF2 commitments to GBP 70 billion (nominal) over the five years from 2015-16. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. PF2 had planned higher equity of 20% to 25%, to make the debt lower-risk and attract pension funds. This was not implemented. As at September 2017 all six PF2 projects (five education, one health) had about 10% equity and 90% debt, the same structure as PFI, and no pension funds had invested in PF2 debt. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. The six PF2 projects were five batches of the Priority School Building Programme (46 schools, capital value GBP 623 million) and the Midland Metropolitan Hospital (GBP 297 million). The government had first intended to raise GBP 1.75 billion of PSBP financing through PF2. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. The Midland Metropolitan Hospital was the only PF2 project to use an equity funding competition. There were five bidders for a 40% equity stake, and the winning expected return of 8.6% reduced the price of the project equity from 12% to 10%. The winning bidder was Richardson's. The government held 10% and Carillion, the primary contractor, 50%. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. The NAO reported expected equity rates of return of 10.3% to 12.4% for the PSBP batches and 10% for Midland Metropolitan. The projected after-tax return to all investors (debt and equity) in the six PF2 deals was 4.5% to 5%, approximately double the cost of government borrowing when they were agreed. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. NAO cost comparisons: the cumulative cash costs of a group of PF2 schools were around 40% higher than a publicly financed comparator that assumed government borrowing at 2.5%. The NAO also cited an estimate that a privately financed hospital cost 70% more than its public sector comparator. These are cash-cost comparisons and do not by themselves measure value for money. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. The Operational PFI Savings Programme was launched in July 2011 with a GBP 1.5 billion target. By June 2013 departments reported GBP 1.6 billion of signed savings, and by end-2016 a further GBP 1.6 billion of unaudited savings had been reported. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. The M25 DBFO (Connect Plus), signed in 2009 for 30 years with capital investment of around GBP 1 billion: Balfour Beatty and Skanska each held 40% and Atkins and Egis 10% each. In 2016-17 Skanska and Atkins sold their combined 50% for GBP 330 million. The NAO estimated an annual return of around 31% over 2009-10 to 2016-17 on the GBP 100 million invested by the sellers. The bank debt was priced at LIBOR + 250 bp, stepping to +300 bp in 2017 and +350 bp in 2020. Highways England negotiated a 50% to 90% share of refinancing gains, depending on the size of the gain, and the NAO estimated in 2010 that around GBP 100 million could be recovered. As of January 2018 the refinancing had not occurred. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
15. Fazakerley prison (Liverpool): the contract was let in December 1995 to Fazakerley Prison Services Ltd (formed by Group 4 and Tarmac), and the project was refinanced in November 1999. The contract gave the Prison Service no right to share in refinancing gains. It accepted GBP 1 million of the expected GBP 10.7 million increase in shareholder returns as compensation for the extra risk. The NAO reported in June 2000 and the PAC in March 2001 (HC 372). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 9]
16. Norfolk and Norwich University Hospital: the PFI contract with the Octagon consortium reached financial close in 1998, and the hospital opened in 2001. Octagon refinanced in 2003. The refinancing gain was GBP 115 million in NPV terms, of which GBP 34 million (about 29%) was shared with the Trust and paid over the life of the contract rather than up front. Octagon's debt rose from GBP 200 million to GBP 306 million, and the projected equity IRR rose from 16% to 60%. The Trust agreed to extend the contract from 34 to 39 years and accepted that its termination liabilities could rise by up to GBP 257 million. NAO report HC 78 (2005-06) was published on 10 June 2005, and the PAC reported in 2006 (Thirty-fifth Report, HC 694). [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
17. The 2002 voluntary code of conduct under which the private sector shared 30% of refinancing gains on early PFI deals had produced GBP 72 million by April 2006. In total the public sector secured GBP 137 million from refinancings, of which GBP 102 million came from four major refinancings (a London Underground contract and the Norfolk and Norwich, Darent Valley and Bromley hospitals). In about 40% of projects there had been a change of investors (NAO HC 1040, 21 April 2006). [confidence: high] [source: 6]
18. Gain-share terms in standard contracts (NAO 2018): PFI and PF2 contracts gave the public sector at least 50% of refinancing gains, and deals agreed since 2009 gave 70% of gains above GBP 3 million. After Eurostat's September 2016 guidance on statistical treatment of PPPs, HM Treasury capped the PF2 public share at 33% so that projects would stay off the government balance sheet. It also removed a planned lifecycle-fund gain-share. HM Treasury acknowledged these changes could have a moderate negative impact on value for money. As of January 2018, 12 projects had reported refinancing savings to HM Treasury. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
19. The Budget of 29 October 2018, presented by Chancellor Philip Hammond, announced that the government would no longer use PF2 (or PFI) for new projects. Existing contracts would be honoured because voluntary termination is rarely value for money given compensation costs. A "Centre of Best Practice" would be piloted in the Department of Health and Social Care to support NHS trust contract managers. The policy does not apply to the devolved administrations. The brief stated that 86% of PFI and PF2 contracts were signed before 2010 and cited Public Accounts Committee criticism (June 2018) and the Office for Budget Responsibility's Fiscal Risks Report (July 2017). [confidence: high] [source: 2]
20. The Budget brief stated that PF2 had previously been considered for the A303 and Lower Thames Crossing road projects, and that the financing model would not decide whether those schemes proceeded. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
21. In his Budget speech Hammond said he had never signed off a PFI contract as Chancellor and never would, and announced the abolition of PFI and PF2 for future projects (wording in the official HM Treasury transcript). [confidence: high] [source: 10]
22. HM Treasury/IPA data as at 31 March 2021 (published April 2023): 694 PFI/PF2 projects with a total capital value of GBP 54.7 billion (down from 704); GBP 152.2 billion of unitary charge payments remaining from 2021-22; 2021-22 unitary charges of GBP 10.0 billion, the highest remaining year; and GBP 92.4 billion (61%) due in the next ten financial years. Expiries were expected to rise from 5 in 2021 to a peak of 59 in 2036, with the last two in 2048. Schools and hospitals made up 379 projects (55%). [confidence: high] [source: 7]
23. Updated PF2 equity IRR data (nominal, including fees, as at 31 March 2021) for the schools batches were: North East 12.2%, North West 12.4%, Yorkshire 11.1%, Hertfordshire/Luton/Reading 10.2% and Midlands 7.5%. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
24. Contract expiry: the NAO report "Managing PFI assets and services as contracts end" (5 June 2020) covered 571 English PFI contracts. It warned that without strategic support and preparation, assets might not be returned in the right condition. The IPA published "Managing the Risks of PFI Contract Expiry" (summer 2021) and "Preparing for PFI contract expiry" (February 2022), and by April 2023 it had conducted more than 100 Expiry Health Checks. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
25. Hexham General Hospital (2014): Northumbria Healthcare NHS Foundation Trust bought out its PFI contract using a loan of approximately GBP 114 million from Northumberland County Council, which borrowed from the Public Works Loan Board. This was reported as the first NHS PFI buyout, with projected savings of about GBP 3.5 million a year. [confidence: medium, trade press] [source: 11]

## Financing and structure details

- Typical PFI/PF2 capital structure: about 10% equity (largely shareholder loans) and 90% senior debt (bank loans or, pre-2008, monoline-wrapped bonds). Contract terms are 25 to 30 years, paid through a performance-adjusted unitary charge (NAO 2018). Much bank debt was hedged with long-dated interest rate swaps, and the NAO notes that the high cost of breaking swaps reduces the savings available from refinancing or termination. [source: 1]
- PF2: public equity stake of typically 10% held via the IPA, equity funding competitions (used only at Midland Metropolitan: 40% competed, 50% Carillion, 10% government), and the PSBP batches used an aggregator, Aggregator Vehicle plc. [source: 1]
- Refinancing gain-share: the public share was 0% for the earliest deals (Fazakerley), then 30% under the 2002 voluntary code, then at least 50% in later standard terms, then 70% above GBP 3 million for deals agreed from 2009, then capped at 33% for PF2 after Eurostat's 2016 guidance (NAO). [source: 1, 6]
- Norfolk and Norwich: debt rose from GBP 200 million to GBP 306 million at refinancing, the term was extended from 34 to 39 years, and termination exposure rose by up to GBP 257 million. [source: 4, 5]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- NAO (2018): PF2 was "similar to PFI" in its fundamentals. Budgetary and balance-sheet incentives that have nothing to do with value for money continued to drive the use of private finance, because spreading costs over time relieves capital budgets. HM Treasury considered removing those incentives but chose not to. [source: 1]
- NAO (2018): savings from operational contracts are hard to achieve because public bodies must negotiate through the SPV, refinancing decisions rest with the SPV's investors, and some SPVs had not shared contractually owed insurance savings. [source: 1]
- NAO (2018): the NAO was not aware of any operational PFI deal where debt holders had suffered loss once the asset was built and operating. That raises the question of why the public sector chose to take the riskiest tranche (equity) under PF2 rather than lower-risk debt. [source: 1]
- PAC and NAO (2005-06): the Norfolk and Norwich refinancing tripled the projected equity return within two years of opening and increased the Trust's termination exposure. This was the case that pushed refinancing gain-sharing into standard terms. [source: 4, 5]
- HM Treasury (2018): PFI was ended because it was inflexible, overly complex and a source of significant fiscal risk to government (as summarised in the 2023 data note). [source: 2, 7]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 3 (How project finance evolved): use the UK PFI as the defining example of the PPP era. It began as a fiscal and efficiency tool in 1992, scaled to more than 700 projects, stalled after 2008 when bank and monoline funding became expensive, was rebranded as PF2 in 2012 and was closed to new projects in 2018. The lesson of the era is that a model sold on risk transfer can be undone by its cost of capital, its accounting motives and its public legitimacy, not only by its engineering.
- Chapter 57 (The public-sector case for PPPs): teach value for money against the public sector comparator through the NAO's figures: PF2 investor returns of 4.5% to 5% against gilts of about 2.5%, schools about 40% dearer in cash terms, and benefits needing to offset the higher cost. Pair this with the fiscal-treatment story (off-balance-sheet incentive, the GBP 70 billion control total, Eurostat 2016) and the GBP 199 billion of committed future payments as a contingent-liability case. Close with the 2018 decision and the expiry challenge (a peak of 59 expiries in 2036) as unfinished public-sector business.
- Chapter 58 (Procuring and designing PPPs): use Fazakerley and Norfolk and Norwich to motivate refinancing gain-share clauses. Show the evolution 0% to 30% to 50% to 70% above GBP 3 million to a 33% cap, and why termination compensation linked to outstanding debt must be protected against debt increases at refinancing. Use the Midland Metropolitan equity competition (12% to 10%) and the M25 equity sale (about 31% IRR) to teach equity-return transparency and secondary-market gains. Use the Hexham buyout and the NAO/IPA expiry work for voluntary termination economics and handback.

## Do not state

- Do not attribute exact words to Hammond beyond the official transcript wording. Paraphrase is safer.
- Do not quote the PAC or its chair as calling Octagon "the unacceptable face of capitalism" unless the exact report text is checked. The phrase appears in secondary coverage, but its precise source (report, chair's statement or press release) was not verified here.
- Do not give a single figure for total lifetime PFI cost (claims such as "GBP 300 billion+") unless the basis and source are cited. The verified figures are GBP 199 billion of future charges (as at 31 March 2016) and GBP 152.2 billion remaining (as at 31 March 2021).
- Do not say that the 2002 code applied to all contracts, or that 50% sharing applied to all pre-2009 deals. The NAO's summary is "at least 50%" in standard terms, and early deals had no contractual right.
- Do not state the Norfolk and Norwich equity IRR uplift as a realised return. The 16% and 60% figures are projected IRRs.
- Do not state that Scotland's NPD/hub programmes or the Welsh Mutual Investment Model ended in 2018. The 2018 decision did not apply to the devolved administrations.
- Do not give an exact current count of operational PFI projects for 2026. The latest verified dataset is as at 31 March 2021 (694). Later IPA data exist but were not checked here.
- Do not state the Hexham buyout figures as audited. They come from trade press and trust statements.
