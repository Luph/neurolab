# Infrastructure equity returns: expectations and realized returns by risk bucket (core, core-plus, value-add, opportunistic, greenfield)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest items covered: EDHEC Infrastructure & Private Assets Research Institute release on exit uplifts, October 3, 2026; HICL Infrastructure PLC Annual Report for the year to March 31, 2026; Brookfield Asset Management Ltd. Form 10-K for 2025, filed March 2, 2026; J.P. Morgan Asset Management 2026 Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions, October 2025)

## Summary

Infrastructure equity is priced in risk buckets whose labels are industry convention, not regulation. A US registered fund's prospectus (CION Grosvenor, 2024) describes the standard four buckets:
- **Core:** availability-based or regulated, low operating risk, strong cash yield.
- **Core-plus:** contracted, but with recontracting or volume exposure.
- **Value-add:** growth- or construction-driven, with meaningful volume or merchant exposure.
- **Opportunistic:** private-equity-like risk.

Greenfield (construction-stage) investing cuts across these buckets and is usually priced above operating "brownfield/secondary" assets.

**Target returns.** Published targets cluster by bucket. As of December 31, 2025, Brookfield's long-term private funds targeted:
- core and core-plus: 9–13 percent gross;
- value-add: 15–16 percent gross;
- opportunistic: 20 percent or more, gross.

At the listed core end, HICL (UK social and regulated infrastructure) values its portfolio at a weighted average discount rate of 8.5 percent (March 2026), which implies an equity risk premium of about 3.5 percent. HICL's long-term IRR target has been 7–8 percent since its IPO. J.P. Morgan's 2026 capital market assumptions put global core infrastructure at 6.5 percent a year (leveraged, net of fees), up from 6.3 percent.

**Greenfield vs operating.** The UK NAO found that PFI equity bid at financial close typically targeted 12–15 percent. Secondary buyers of operating PFI equity required about 12 percent in 2003, just above 7 percent in 2005, just above 5 percent in 2006–07, and about 8 percent after the crisis.

**Realized returns.** For closed-end funds the evidence is sobering. For 2002–2018 vintages, Andonov, Kräussl and Rauh (Review of Financial Studies, 2021) found a mean net IRR of 9.9 percent (Preqin) or 5.6 percent (Burgiss), and a public market equivalent (PME) of 0.93 against the S&P 500. Infrastructure funds were as volatile as buyout funds and underperformed them.

**Drivers that move these numbers:**
- risk-free rates (HICL's premium is quoted over gilts);
- leverage;
- the share of contracted or regulated revenue;
- construction and volume exposure;
- competition for assets;
- fee load (gross vs net).

## Verified facts

**Definitions of the buckets (market convention)**
1. **CION Grosvenor bucket descriptions.** The CION Grosvenor Infrastructure Fund registration statement (N-2/A, November 2024) says "the infrastructure industry generally characterizes investments as core, core plus, value added and opportunistic." It describes them as follows:
   - **Core:** low operating risk; availability-based revenues from creditworthy counterparties; strong cash yield, "viewed as an investment alternative to debt"; typically fixed concessions (PPPs) or perpetual regulated utilities.
   - **Core-plus:** contracted cash flows with optimization or growth upside; potentially more volume or recontracting risk; the shorter duration of contractual protection "requires some assumptions around recontracting."
   - **Value-add:** returns "driven much more by growth"; may require the buyer to assume construction risk; meaningful volume or merchant exposure.
   - **Opportunistic:** "private equity-like risks" and meaningful merchant exposure.

   [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. **Greenfield, brownfield and secondary.** The same filing defines "Brownfield" investments as previously developed, cash-generating assets with improvement potential, and "Greenfield" as new projects requiring development and construction that may not yet be cash flowing. Andonov et al. (2021) use a parallel three-way split:
   - greenfield: assets that did not previously exist;
   - brownfield: partly operational, needing substantial improvement;
   - secondary: fully operational.

   In their Preqin sample, 73.5 percent of investor-deal observations were secondary-stage, 18.1 percent greenfield and 8.4 percent brownfield. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]

**Target and expected returns (dated)**
3. **Brookfield targets.** Brookfield Asset Management's 10-K for 2025 states that its long-term private funds "target opportunistic (20%+, gross), value-add (15%-16%, gross), core and core plus (9%-13%, gross) returns." These are manager targets across all its asset classes, not infrastructure only, and are gross of fees and carry. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. **J.P. Morgan 2026 LTCMA.** J.P. Morgan Asset Management's 2026 Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions (estimates as of September 30, 2025) give global core infrastructure (USD) an expected return of 6.5 percent, up from 6.3 percent in the 2025 edition. The figure is leveraged and net of fees; the asset class volatility assumption is 10.25 percent. J.P. Morgan cites annualized capital growth of 4.9 percent for global infrastructure over 2009–24. It also says investors "have appeared more willing to accept a lower risk premium" as the asset class institutionalized. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. **HICL (listed core).** For the year to March 31, 2026:
   - HICL's weighted average portfolio discount rate was 8.5 percent (8.4 percent at March 31, 2025);
   - InfraRed describes this as implying "a weighted average equity risk premium of 3.5%" for a portfolio of core infrastructure assets;
   - HICL's long-term IRR target is 7–8 percent "as set out at IPO." [confidence: high] [source: 5]
6. **UK PFI greenfield equity.** The NAO (February 10, 2012, HC 1792) reported that the expected equity return agreed when PFI contracts are signed "typically ranges between 12 and 15 per cent." Supporting evidence:
   - PwC (2002) found planned pre-tax equity IRRs falling from over 15 percent to about 13.5 percent from the mid-1990s to 2001, with some early deals at up to 20 percent;
   - an NAO sample of 24 PFI projects awarded since 2005 showed projected returns of 12–17 percent;
   - 14 school deals in 2006–08 were at 12–15 percent. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
7. **UK PFI secondary (operating) equity.** The NAO reported that annualized returns required by secondary investors:
   - fell from about 12 percent in 2003 to just above 7 percent in 2005;
   - fell further to just above 5 percent in 2006–07, as low-cost borrowing let buyers pay "particularly generous prices";
   - moved back to around 8 percent after the financial crisis.

   Primary investors selling early typically achieved exit returns of 15–30 percent a year (exceptionally up to 60 percent, or as low as 5 percent). [confidence: high] [source: 6]
8. **PF2 expected equity returns.** The NAO later reported expected equity returns of 10.3–12.4 percent for PF2 schools batches. The equity funding competition for Midland Metropolitan Hospital reduced the price of project equity from 12 percent to 10 percent (see facts/uk-pfi.md for details and sources). [confidence: high] [source: 9]

**Realized returns**
9. **Andonov, Kräussl and Rauh (2021), headline results.** "Institutional Investors and Infrastructure Investing," *Review of Financial Studies* 34(8): 3880–3934, studied closed-end infrastructure funds raised 2002–2018, with cash flows to end-2019.

   | Measure (equal-weighted, Preqin) | Infrastructure | Buyout | VC | Real estate |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | Number of funds (IRR) | 210 | 1,534 | 1,419 | 1,477 |
   | Mean net IRR | 9.92% | 14.52% | 13.33% | 10.58% |
   | Median net IRR | 9.35% | 12.70% | 11.00% | 10.90% |
   | Mean net multiple | 1.34x | 1.56x | 1.74x | 1.36x |
   | Mean PME (S&P 500) | 0.93 | 1.05 | 0.99 | 0.94 |

   Burgiss data on 201 infrastructure funds gave a mean net IRR of 5.60 percent (median 7.94 percent), a multiple of 1.23x and a PME of 0.89. Only 43 funds with performance data were fully realized. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
10. **Andonov et al. (2021), interpretation.**
    - Infrastructure funds' PME standard deviation (0.31) is comparable to buyout's (0.36).
    - Fund cash flows "display similar volatility and cyclicality as other private equity investments" and depend on quick exits. A 10-percentage-point rise in deals exited within five years is associated with a 2.575-percentage-point higher net IRR.
    - Public investors earned a net IRR 1.81 percentage points lower than private investors.
    - ESG preferences and regulation explain 25–40 percent of public investors' higher allocation and 30 percent of their underperformance. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
11. **Listed-trust exit evidence (EDHEC, 2026).** EDHEC Infrastructure & Private Assets Research Institute (October 3, 2026) studied 333 exits by 29 UK-listed investment trusts, 2015–2026. Findings:
    - The median exit price for private infrastructure was about 23 percent above the last carrying value.
    - Uplifts fell with holding period: about 47 percent median for holdings under three years, near zero above seven years.
    - Infrastructure trusts traded at a premium to NAV through 2021, but at discounts of about 10–20 percent after the 2022 rate shock. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

**Secondary-market pricing**
12. **Lazard 2025 secondary market report.**
    - The global secondary market transacted an estimated USD 233 billion in 2025 (GP-led USD 116 billion; LP-led USD 117 billion).
    - About 73 percent of infrastructure and real asset GP-led transactions priced above 90 percent of NAV.
    - The average secondary-buyer return target for infrastructure and real asset GP-leds was a net IRR of 18.7 percent and a net multiple of 1.9x. These two targets were read from a three-panel chart, so this attribution is medium confidence. [confidence: high for volumes and pricing; medium for the return target] [source: 8]

Reading notes:
- Gross-to-net gaps are typically several percentage points for closed-end funds (see t-infra-fund-terms for fee levels).
- Asset-level discount rates and fund-level IRRs differ by leverage and fees.
- Lender-imposed gearing raises equity IRRs on the same asset.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Expected vs realized.** Andonov et al. argue that closed-end infrastructure funds failed to deliver the "long-term stable returns" investors expected. Net returns were below buyout and real estate on a PME basis, with similar dispersion. They attribute part of this to finite fund lives forcing quick exits, and part to non-financial (ESG and regulatory) motives of public investors (source 2).
- **Compression of core returns.** The NAO data show secondary PFI yields halving between 2003 and 2006–07 as competition and cheap debt grew. J.P. Morgan notes a falling risk premium as the asset class institutionalized (sources 4, 6). Rising rates after 2022 pushed listed vehicles to NAV discounts (source 7).
- **Valuation caution.** EDHEC's finding of persistent exit uplifts suggests reported NAVs (and hence interim IRRs) understate exit values. It also notes uplifts have fallen since 2022 and that 2020–22 vintages may face lower exit multiples (source 7). Practitioners and EDHEC disagree on whether conservative NAVs are prudent or inconsistent with fair value. This sheet favors reporting both NAV-based and realized-exit evidence.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 46 (Equity returns and valuation):**
  - Build the hurdle ladder from risk-free plus premium. Use HICL's 8.5 percent discount rate and 3.5 percent premium as the core anchor, NAO's 12–15 percent greenfield PFI bid returns as the construction premium, and the drop to about 7–8 percent at secondary sale as the value created by de-risking.
  - Use this ladder in the Case R acquisition valuation.
  - Show why a sponsor who sells at completion captures most of the "greenfield premium" (NAO exit returns of 15–30 percent).
- **Ch 47 (Bidding, acquisitions and funds):**
  - Use Brookfield's gross targets against Andonov's realized net IRRs (mean 9.9 percent, median 9.4 percent, PME 0.93) to teach the gap between target, gross and net returns.
  - Use the Lazard GP-led pricing (about 73 percent above 90 percent of NAV) to discuss how secondary buyers price infrastructure.
  - Teach the drivers that move each bucket, so readers can update the ranges.

## Do not state

- Do not present "core 5–8 percent, core-plus 8–12 percent, value-add 12–15 percent, opportunistic 15 percent+" as a sourced industry standard. These commonly quoted bands were not verified in any primary source here. Use the dated, attributed figures above instead.
- Do not describe Brookfield's 9–13, 15–16 and 20-plus percent bands as infrastructure-specific or as net returns. They are gross targets across its private funds.
- Do not quote Preqin or Burgiss infrastructure IRRs for vintages after 2018 or for 2025 benchmarks. Not retrieved.
- Do not claim greenfield infrastructure equity has realized higher returns than brownfield across markets. The verified evidence is expected returns at bid (NAO) and early-exit returns in UK PFI, not a cross-market realized comparison.
- Do not equate J.P. Morgan's 6.5 percent with a fund target. It is a long-run beta assumption, leveraged and net of fees.
