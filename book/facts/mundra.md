# Tata Mundra and Adani Mundra (India): imported coal cost shock, force majeure and change in law

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: the Ministry of Power's extension, reported on 25 September 2026, of its Section 11 direction requiring the Tata Power Mundra plant to run until 31 December 2026)

## Summary

Two privately owned coal plants at Mundra in Kutch, Gujarat, were built to burn imported coal, mainly from Indonesia. They sold power under long-term PPAs won through competitive tariff bidding. Tata Power's subsidiary Coastal Gujarat Power Ltd (CGPL) built the 4,000 MW Mundra Ultra Mega Power Project (UMPP), five 800 MW supercritical units. It won the project in December 2006 with a levelized tariff of INR 2.26/kWh. Only part of the fuel charge was escalable, so CGPL kept the coal price risk on 55% of its fuel. Adani Power's 4,620 MW Mundra plant sold 1,000 MW to Gujarat (GUVNL) and 1,424 MW to Haryana's utilities, both at quoted energy charges that did not escalate. In 2010–11 Indonesia required coal exports to be priced at or above government benchmark prices, even under existing long-term contracts. The fuel supply contracts the bids relied on stopped giving cheap coal. Both generators asked the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) for relief, citing force majeure and change in law. CERC said neither clause applied, then granted a "compensatory tariff" anyway in 2014. The Appellate Tribunal for Electricity (APTEL) upheld relief on force majeure grounds in April 2016. On 11 April 2017 the Supreme Court of India (Energy Watchdog v CERC) reversed it. It held that a rise in fuel price is commercial hardship, not force majeure, especially since the PPA excluded changes in fuel cost. It also held that a change in Indonesian law is not a "change in law" under PPAs that cover only Indian law. Years of workouts followed: a Gujarat high-powered committee in 2018, supplementary PPAs, Gujarat's revocation in 2020, and a shutdown of the Tata plant from July 2025. In March 2026 a supplementary PPA with GUVNL allowed coal-cost pass-through, and Ministry of Power directions under Section 11 of the Electricity Act, 2003 ordered the plant to run.

## Verified facts

1. CGPL was the special-purpose vehicle for the Mundra UMPP. It is a wholly owned subsidiary of Tata Power Company Ltd (TPC). The project was a 4,000 MW coal plant using supercritical technology near Mundra Port, Kutch district, Gujarat. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. TPC won the project in competitive bidding run by the Ministry of Power and Power Finance Corporation in December 2006. It quoted a levelized tariff of INR 2.26/kWh, including a fuel charge of INR 0.86/kWh. The PPA ran for 25 years with distribution utilities in Gujarat, Haryana, Maharashtra, Punjab and Rajasthan. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. The bidding rules let each bidder choose how much of its tariff was escalable or non-escalable, and the lowest levelized tariff won. TPC bid 45% of the fuel charge as escalable, linked to CERC's imported-coal escalation index, and 55% as non-escalable. Both parts of the fuel charge were denominated in US dollars. About a third of the overall INR 2.26 levelized tariff did not escalate. ADB's breakdown of the bid, citing the 2013 CERC committee report, in INR/kWh levelized: [confidence: high] [source: 1]
   - capacity charge: 0.05 non-escalable plus 0.83 escalable = 0.88;
   - fuel charge: 0.49 plus 0.37 = 0.86;
   - transport and fuel handling: 0.20 plus 0.32 = 0.52;
   - total: 0.74 non-escalable plus 1.52 escalable = 2.26.
4. ADB concluded that the project carried coal price risk on 55% of its coal requirement and inflation risk on about 40% of transport and handling charges. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. TPC signed long-term fuel supply agreements with Indonesian suppliers. In 2007 it bought 30% stakes in PT Kaltim Prima Coal (KPC) and PT Arutmin Indonesia, expecting a natural hedge at group level. The dividends, later assigned to lenders, were small compared with CGPL's cash shortfall. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4]
6. The power was allocated as Gujarat 1,805 MW, Maharashtra 760 MW, Punjab 475 MW, Haryana 380 MW and Rajasthan 380 MW. That totals 3,800 MW contracted out of 4,000 MW installed. CARE Ratings confirms the 3,800 MW figure. [confidence: medium] [source: 3, 6]
7. ADB approved a loan of up to USD 450 million on 17 April 2008. ADB funded USD 250 million itself and placed USD 200 million with the Export–Import Bank of Korea (KEXIM) through a risk participation. The loan had a 20-year tenor and up to 5 years' grace. Other lenders included the International Finance Corporation (IFC), KEXIM, Korea Export Insurance Corporation, and rupee lenders led by State Bank of India (SBI) and India Infrastructure Finance Company Ltd. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. At appraisal the project cost was INR 169 billion, or USD 4,226.7 million. The planned debt–equity ratio was 75:25, with about USD 3.17 billion of debt and USD 1.06 billion of equity. ADB later put actual cost at USD 4,620.1 million, funded 44.4% by equity and 55.6% by debt. Only USD 351.2 million of the ADB loan was disbursed, because further disbursement was withheld for safeguards non-compliance. The loan was prepaid in full by the end of 2018. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. A July 2008 supplemental PPA moved the scheduled commercial operation dates. All five units were commissioned between March 2012 and March 2013 without penalties. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. Indonesia's Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources Regulation No. 17 of 2010, on setting benchmark prices for minerals and coal, is dated 23 September 2010. ADB says the change took effect in September 2011 and required coal exporters to sell at regulator-set benchmark prices even where existing contract prices were lower. In June 2012 the benchmark price was USD 74/tonne, against an average of USD 43/tonne in TPC's bid. [confidence: medium for the regulation number and date; high for the ADB description and prices] [source: 1, 6]
11. Per-unit operating profit at CGPL averaged about INR 0.20/kWh from FY2013 to FY2019, against INR 0.73/kWh in the base-case model. ADB attributes the gap to higher fuel costs. It says the problem was made worse by under-hedged foreign exchange, ship demurrage charges and blending in lower-calorific coal. There was no payment default, because TPC funded CGPL's cash shortfalls. ADB estimated that an average tariff increase of about INR 0.60/kWh, to about INR 3.04/kWh, would have made the plant viable. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. CGPL raised the problem with the Ministry of Power in August 2011. In September 2011 the Ministry replied that the PPA was a contract between the offtakers and the developer. CGPL asked the Indonesian government in February 2012 to exempt existing contracts, without success. It petitioned CERC in July 2012 (Petition No. 159/MP/2012), invoking force majeure and change in law. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. CERC's 15 April 2013 order on CGPL accepted the offtakers' main points. It found that linking fuel price to market benchmarks was not force majeure, and that change in law did not cover foreign law. CERC nonetheless granted temporary relief and set up a committee of the parties, financial analysts and bankers to design it. Four members supported the order and one dissented. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. Adani Power's Mundra plant has 4,620 MW: four 330 MW units and five 660 MW units. Its two PPAs: [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
   - GUVNL, 1,000 MW: bid in January 2007 at a levelized INR 2.3495/kWh with a non-escalable energy charge of INR 1.3495/kWh. The PPA is dated 2 February 2007.
   - Haryana utilities, 1,424 MW: bid on 24 November 2007 at a levelized INR 2.94/kWh with non-escalable energy charges.
15. CERC's compensatory tariff orders came in February 2014, with 21 February 2014 for Adani. CARE Ratings reported the formula: [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
   - The gross compensatory tariff was the cost of coal at the Indonesian reference index, adjusted for calorific value, minus the fuel charges recoverable under the PPA.
   - It was reduced by the generator's share of profits from its Indonesian mines, by a return-on-equity haircut (100 bps for CGPL), and, for Adani, by part of the profits on sales above normative availability.
   - CARE's estimate for CGPL was a gross INR 0.53/kWh and a net of about INR 0.41/kWh.
16. APTEL's judgment of 7 April 2016 treated the Indonesian measures as force majeure and remanded the cases to CERC to set compensatory tariffs. CERC then set one by an order of 6 December 2016. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
17. Energy Watchdog v CERC, decided by the Supreme Court of India on 11 April 2017 (R.F. Nariman and Pinaki Chandra Ghose JJ.), held: [confidence: high, except the domestic coal holding, which is medium] [source: 2, 5]
   - Clause 12.4 of the PPA expressly excluded from force majeure changes in fuel cost and the agreement becoming onerous to perform. A rise in fuel price was not force majeure and did not frustrate the contract, since coal could be bought elsewhere at a higher price.
   - Change in law under the PPAs means Indian law, so changes in Indonesian law did not qualify.
   - A 2013 change in India's domestic coal distribution policy could be change in law for shortfalls in domestic coal supply, and CERC was to work out the relief case by case.
   - CERC had jurisdiction under Section 79 of the Electricity Act, 2003, because generation and sale of power in more than one state is a "composite scheme".
18. In June 2017 CGPL offered to sell 51% of the project to the offtaker utilities for INR 1. The utilities did not take up the offer. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
19. In January 2018 SBI asked the Government of India for a high-powered committee for CGPL and similar projects. On 3 July 2018 Gujarat set up a three-member committee: former Supreme Court judge R.K. Agrawal, former RBI Deputy Governor S.S. Mundra and former CERC chair Pramod Deo. It covered Adani Power Mundra (4,620 MW), CGPL (4,000 MW) and Essar's 1,200 MW Gujarat plant. It recommended sharing the burden among generators, lenders and consumers, including: [confidence: high] [source: 1, 7]
   - a lender sacrifice of INR 0.20/kWh;
   - a deduction for the group's Indonesian mining profits;
   - a tariff linked to Indonesian coal cost going forward.
20. In October 2018 the Supreme Court allowed CERC to consider PPA amendments and gave it eight weeks to act. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 8]
21. On 1 December 2018 Gujarat issued a government resolution permitting supplementary PPAs. It revoked that resolution on 12 July 2020, citing low international coal prices and consumer interest. CERC approved Essar's supplementary PPA in April 2020, but the CGPL supplementary PPA was not concluded at that time. [confidence: medium] [source: 9]
22. All units of the Tata Power Mundra plant were shut on 2 July 2025. They restarted on 1 April 2026 under a Ministry of Power direction issued on 22 March 2026 under Section 11 of the Electricity Act, 2003. The direction was extended on 19 June 2026 to 30 September and on 25 September 2026 to 31 December 2026. Under the directions, a committee of Ministry of Power, Central Electricity Authority and NTPC officials sets a benchmark rate covering prudent imported-coal costs and reviews it every 15 days. [confidence: medium] [source: 10]
23. In March 2026 Tata Power disclosed that, after Gujarat cabinet approval and a government order, it had signed a supplementary PPA for the Mundra plant with GUVNL that provides for pass-through of coal costs. Similar agreements with Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana awaited approval. [confidence: medium] [source: 11]

## Financing and structure details

- CGPL: TPC owned 100%. Cost at appraisal was USD 4,226.7 million (INR 169 billion at INR 39.47/USD) and actual cost USD 4,620.1 million. Planned gearing was 75:25; actual funding was 55.6% debt and 44.4% equity. [source: 1]
- Lenders: ADB (USD 450 million A/B structure: USD 250 million direct and USD 200 million KEXIM risk participation; 20-year tenor, up to 5 years' grace, semi-annual repayment), IFC, KEXIM, Korea Export Insurance Corporation, and SBI-led rupee banks including India Infrastructure Finance Company Ltd. Amounts for lenders other than ADB are not verified here. [source: 1]
- Credit support in practice: repayment started on the original schedule and was met by accelerated equity and a subordinated sponsor loan. TPC covered CGPL's fuel under-recovery. ADB records no default and a full refinancing and prepayment in FY2019. [source: 1]
- Offtake: 25-year PPA with five state utilities, Gujarat as lead procurer. The levelized tariff was a capacity charge plus a dollar-denominated fuel charge split into escalable and non-escalable parts. [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- ADB's 2020 evaluation said the project carried unmitigated coal price risk that the developer could not manage. It concluded that the only workable mitigant was a pass-through escalation in the tariff, as CERC had noted in 2013. [source: 1]
- ADB also said long-term fixed-price supply contracts "cannot assure supply below market rates". Such contracts are only as good as the damages for breach. The supplier was acting lawfully under Indonesian law, so no remedy was available against it. [source: 1]
- ADB found that changes in foreign law may give no relief under a contract with domestic counterparties governed by domestic law. It recommended due diligence on cross-border links and, where possible, government counterparties or consents. [source: 1]
- ADB said it had not appraised or adequately highlighted the commodity price risk. It recommended that ADB not finance projects carrying such risk, and that bidders' assumptions be separated from parameters fixed by the bidding process. [source: 1]
- CERC's 2013 reasoning, as summarized by ADB, was that making generators carry fuel price risk pushes up bid tariffs and defeats the purpose of competitive bidding. It also recognized that reopening a competitively bid PPA could undermine "the sanctity of competitive bidding". [source: 1]
- The Supreme Court's position was that a bidder that chose a non-escalable fuel quote under a PPA expressly excluding fuel cost changes from force majeure cannot later use that clause to shift the risk. [source: 2, 5]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 18 (PPAs and tolling):** Use the CGPL bid to teach that the escalable/non-escalable split is a risk allocation decision dressed up as a pricing decision. A low levelized tariff won the bid by taking unindexed fuel risk. Have readers rebuild ADB's Table 1 to see that only about a third of the tariff was fixed, yet that third sank the project. Contrast a fuel pass-through PPA with CGPL's structure, and show why the offtaker in a pass-through PPA should insist on heat-rate and procurement-prudence tests.
- **Chapter 25 (inputs and access):** A fuel supply agreement below market price is a hedge only while it can be enforced. When the supplier's own law changes, there may be no breach to sue on. Teach readers to test fuel contracts for (a) the supplier's incentive to perform when the market moves against it, (b) whether the contract's price can be enforced under the supplier's law, and (c) whether an upstream equity stake is a real hedge at project level or only at group level. In CGPL's case the mine profits stayed with TPC, not with CGPL's lenders.
- **Chapter 69 (thermal power):** Coastal plants designed for imported coal are locked into one fuel chain, because the boilers are designed for a specific coal and switching is hard (ADB para. 5). Use the Mundra history as the standard case of fuel-price-driven distress in thermal IPPs. It shows how the losses were absorbed by the sponsor, the courts, a state committee, supplementary PPAs and finally Section 11 directions. It also shows how blending in cheaper coal hurts efficiency.

## Do not state

- That the Supreme Court granted Tata or Adani relief for the Indonesian price rise. It refused. Only the domestic coal policy point went the generators' way, and only for domestic-linkage capacity.
- That CGPL "defaulted" on its loans. ADB records no default; TPC funded the shortfall.
- Per-state allocations of 1,900/800/500/400/400 MW. These appear in secondary sources and conflict with the 3,800 MW contracted total. Use the 1,805/760/475/380/380 split, at medium confidence, or say "five states".
- That Rajasthan terminated its PPA in January 2013. This appears only in an unsourced secondary summary.
- Amounts lent by IFC, KEXIM direct, Korea Export Insurance Corporation and Indian banks (figures such as "IFC USD 450 million" circulate but were not verified here).
- That the Indonesian regulation "banned" exports or "imposed an export tax". It required benchmark pricing.
- Loss totals (for example, "INR 800 crore during the 2025–26 shutdown", or net-worth erosion figures). These are reported but not verified from filings.
- The plant's capacity as 4,150 MW. Some 2026 reports use this figure; it is not verified, so use 4,000 MW.
- The detailed terms of the March 2026 supplementary PPA, beyond coal-cost pass-through. Also whether Tata Power still holds its KPC/Arutmin stakes. Neither was verified.
- That CGPL was merged into Tata Power, or its date. Not verified.
- Earlier Section 11 episodes (for example, 2022). Not verified here.
