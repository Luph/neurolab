# Dabhol Power (India): Enron, MSEB affordability, political backlash

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: the 2005 settlements and Ratnagiri Gas and Power Private Limited (RGPPL) takeover, with brief notes on RGPPL to 2018)

## Summary

In June 1992 Enron, General Electric (GE) and Bechtel signed a memorandum of understanding (MoU) with the Maharashtra State Electricity Board (MSEB). It was for a very large liquefied natural gas (LNG)-fired independent power project at Dabhol, on the Maharashtra coast, to be owned by Dabhol Power Company (DPC). There was no competitive bidding. The World Bank refused to finance it in April 1993, calling it "not economically viable" and too large for base-load operation in the MSEB system. The power purchase agreement (PPA, December 1993) had a two-part tariff denominated in US dollars. MSEB's payments were backed by the Government of Maharashtra and by a Government of India counter-guarantee. Phase I (695 MW) reached financial close with US export credit agency (ECA), US Overseas Private Investment Corporation (OPIC) and Indian bank support. A new state government cancelled the project in August 1995, then revived it in 1996 on renegotiated terms, with a larger combined Phase I and II of about 2,184 MW and MSEB taking equity. Phase I began commercial operation in early 1999. By late 2000 MSEB could not afford the power. Payments stopped, DPC invoked the guarantees, and the state repudiated the PPA. Phase I was shut in June 2001 with Phase II about 95% complete, and Enron went bankrupt in December 2001. About 30 arbitrations and lawsuits followed, including the first OPIC-initiated government-to-government arbitration. In 2005 Indian state-owned interests bought out the foreign lenders and equity, and the plant was revived under RGPPL.

## Verified facts

1. On 20 June 1992 MSEB signed an MoU with Enron and GE for the Dabhol project, a few days after Enron officials surveyed sites on the Maharashtra coast. DPC was a joint venture of Enron, GE and Bechtel. No competitive bidding took place. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. Enron's August 1992 application to the Foreign Investment Promotion Board (FIPB) was for a project of approximately USD 3.1 billion and 2,550 MW, fueled by LNG. On 12 December 1992 the FIPB required it to be cut to 1,920 MW in two phases at USD 2.65 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. On 30 April 1993 the World Bank's country director for India wrote to the Ministry of Finance on the 2,015 MW project. The letter said the project "as presently formulated is not economically viable, and thus could not be financed by the Bank." It gave two reasons: the project was too large for base-load operation in the MSEB system, and it was not part of the least-cost development sequence. The Bank reaffirmed the refusal in July 1993. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
4. The Central Electricity Authority (CEA) gave "in principle" clearance on 20 September 1993 and provisional clearance on 26 November 1993. During that review DPC declined to give the CEA a detailed capital cost breakdown, arguing that the tariff was fixed and DPC bore the capital cost risk. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. The PPA between MSEB and DPC was executed in December 1993. It authorized Phase I (695 MW) and gave an option on Phase II. The tariff was denominated in US dollars. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
6. MSEB's payment obligations were guaranteed by the Government of Maharashtra and, subject to a cap of roughly USD 300 million, by the Government of India. The central government counter-guarantee was signed on 9 September 1994. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
7. Enron held 80% of the equity at the outset, GE 10% and Bechtel 10%. Bechtel built both phases, GE supplied equipment, and GE's investment was held through a GE Structured Finance affiliate. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 5]
8. Human Rights Watch (HRW), citing the Economist Intelligence Unit (April 1995), puts Phase I at approximately USD 920 million. Its financing included approximately USD 150 million from international banks led by Bank of America and ABN Amro, USD 95 million from Indian banks led by the Industrial Development Bank of India (IDBI), approximately USD 100 million of OPIC political risk insurance, and an approximately USD 290 million US Export-Import Bank (US Ex-Im) loan guarantee from late 1994. [confidence: medium, secondary aggregation] [source: 1]
9. Writing about the whole project, Hansen, O'Sullivan and Anderson report that Enron raised USD 1.9 billion of project debt. Lenders included Indian government-owned banks, ECAs, offshore commercial lenders and OPIC. OPIC's USD 160 million was the single largest offshore loan commitment, and it also provided about USD 200 million of political risk insurance for the Enron, GE and Bechtel equity. [confidence: medium] [source: 3]
10. The Shiv Sena-BJP coalition won the Maharashtra elections in March 1995. A cabinet sub-committee chaired by Deputy Chief Minister Gopinath Munde was set up on 3 May 1995. It recommended cancelling Phase II and repudiating Phase I, and on 3 August 1995 the state announced that Phase I would stop and Phase II was cancelled. Enron began arbitration in London in August 1995. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. On 8 January 1996 the state announced acceptance of a renegotiated project. It reported capital cost cut from approximately USD 2.8 billion to approximately USD 2.5 billion and a tariff cut of 22.5%. HRW reports critics' view that the savings arose from committing to Phase II rather than from cheaper Phase I power. [confidence: high for the announced figures; the critique is attributed] [source: 1]
12. Under the renegotiation Phase I grew to 740 MW and the total project to about 2,184 MW. Hansen et al. report that Enron sold a 30% equity interest to an MSEB affiliate for USD 137 million, cutting Enron to 50%. MSEB's interest was later diluted to roughly 15% when it did not make further equity contributions. Enron's stake was 65% by 2001. [confidence: medium, because HRW gives about USD 151 million for the 30% stake at the November 1998 exchange rate] [source: 1, 3]
13. Phase I achieved commercial operation in early 1999. [confidence: high for "early 1999"] [source: 3]
14. The October 2000 payment due from MSEB went unpaid until January 2001, when the state government stepped in. Months of slow payment and non-payment followed. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
15. In early February 2001 DPC invoked the Government of India counter-guarantee after MSEB defaulted. The central government resisted payment, citing alleged supply shortfalls by DPC. [confidence: medium, single thesis source drawing on press reports] [source: 4]
16. The Godbole review panel, chaired by former Union finance secretary Madhav Godbole, reported in April 2001. It recommended separating the LNG terminal from the power project, restructuring loan repayment and removing dollar denomination of the tariff. [confidence: medium] [source: 4]
17. DPC sent MSEB a notice of arbitration in May 2001. MSEB sought injunctions and repudiated the PPA. Phase I was shut in June 2001 and all employees were terminated, with Phase II roughly 5% short of completion. Lenders suspended funding and the contractors left the site. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
18. Enron filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in December 2001. Hansen et al. describe Enron as "radioactive" to the workout by early 2002. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
19. On 9 September 2003 an arbitral tribunal chaired by former US District Judge Charles Renfrew ruled that the Government of India and its agents had violated international law through an unlawful taking of GE's and Bechtel's investments in DPC. It awarded USD 28.57 million each to GE and Bechtel against their political risk insurer, OPIC. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
20. In January 2004 the US Government began arbitration against India over OPIC's Dabhol claims. Hansen et al. call it the first time in OPIC's history that an arbitration had been started against a host government under its bilateral agreements. GE and Bechtel separately brought claims under the India-Mauritius bilateral investment treaty through Mauritian subsidiaries. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 7]
21. Under the "Enron Lite" deal, OPIC funded a transaction in which Enron gave up its DPC interest and its USD 142 million insurance claim in return for roughly the return of premiums it had paid. Control passed to GE and Bechtel. The New York Bankruptcy Court approved the deal on 8 April 2004. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
22. GE announced a comprehensive settlement on 2 July 2005. Hansen et al. date the closings as follows: GE on 8 July, the offshore lenders' position acquired by a special purpose vehicle for the Indian banks on 12 July, and OPIC and Bechtel on 16 July 2005. At that point the project was wholly owned by Government of India interests. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 6]
23. RGPPL was incorporated in July 2005 to take over and revive DPC's assets, with NTPC as the lead promoter. It operates approximately 1,967 MW. The LNG terminal was later demerged, and RGPPL reports a "hibernation period" in 2015-2016 owing to gas shortage. [confidence: high for 1,967 MW and NTPC leadership; medium for other details] [source: 8]

## Financing and structure details

- **Sponsors.** Enron (initially 80%; 50% after the 1996 sale to MSEB; 65% by 2001), GE (10%) and Bechtel (10%). DPC was an unlimited liability company incorporated in India. [source: 1, 3, 5]
- **Offtake.** A 20-year two-part PPA with MSEB as sole buyer: capacity payments plus energy. The tariff was in US dollars, and the fuel was imported LNG, with distillate or naphtha in Phase I. [source: 1, 3, 4]
- **Credit support.** A Government of Maharashtra guarantee, with sovereign immunity waived, and a Government of India counter-guarantee capped at about USD 300 million. MSEB letters of credit included one at Canara Bank that DPC sought to draw in September 2001; this is from a single source. [source: 1, 3, 4]
- **Phase I debt.** Approximately USD 920 million project cost. Lenders were international banks led by Bank of America and ABN Amro, Indian banks led by IDBI, a US Ex-Im guarantee and OPIC. [source: 1, 3]
- **Whole-project debt.** USD 1.9 billion. Hansen et al. give the plan as roughly USD 1 billion of equity, USD 1.2 billion from Indian banks, USD 160 million from OPIC, and the rest from offshore banks and ECAs, for a project of about USD 2.9 billion. [source: 3]
- **Political risk cover.** OPIC insured the GE and Bechtel equity, and about USD 32 million of one commercial bank's loan. [source: 3, 5]
- **Arbitration.** London-seated arbitration under the PPA, blocked by Indian court injunctions. There were also BIT claims via Mauritius and a US-India arbitration under the OPIC investment incentive agreement. [source: 1, 3]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Economics.** The World Bank's 1993 letters said the project was too large for MSEB's base load and not least-cost: inflexible LNG take would displace cheaper coal power. [source: 1] Hansen et al. say the predictions of "too much, too soon" proved prescient. By 2001 MSEB neither needed nor could afford the energy, and the cost of the take-or-pay commitment equalled about half of MSEB's budget. [source: 3]
- **Currency and affordability.** The Munde committee called the dollar tariff "the most amazing aspect of the entire Project." HRW notes that the rupee cost of power rose about 20% between signing and 1999 from depreciation alone. Contract protection against currency risk simply moved that risk to an offtaker that could not bear it. [source: 1]
- **Process and legitimacy.** The 1995 cabinet sub-committee criticized the absence of competitive bidding, the lack of transparency and an inflated capital cost. Those weaknesses fed a political campaign and a change of government. [source: 1]
- **Guarantee chains are not cash.** Hansen et al. conclude that the executed agreements "contained attractive financial, legal, and political protections for all the stakeholders, yet the project failed." [source: 3]
- **What went right, partly.** Political risk insurance and the treaty structuring through Mauritius gave GE, Bechtel and their lenders the leverage that produced the 2005 settlement. OPIC's workout role, including removing Enron, was central (Hansen et al.). The physical asset was eventually revived under state ownership. [source: 3, 8]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 3 (History).** Dabhol is the emblem of the 1990s "fast-track" emerging-market IPP wave: unsolicited, negotiated one-on-one, dollar-denominated and sovereign-backed. The era's lesson is that a contract cannot make an unaffordable project bankable. After Dabhol, competitive procurement, local-currency or partially indexed tariffs, and sizing to demand became mainstream.
- **Ch 59 (Country, currency and payment risk).** Use the chain of a USD tariff, a rupee-earning state utility, a state guarantee, a capped federal counter-guarantee and letters of credit to show that each layer depends on the same fiscal capacity. Have readers trace what happens to MSEB's rupee bill when the rupee falls, and why "invoke the guarantee" escalated the dispute instead of settling it.
- **Ch 60 (Political risk and its protection).** Dabhol is the textbook obsolescing bargain: leverage passed to the host once the plant was built. Cover the use of OPIC insurance, BIT structuring via Mauritius, government-to-government arbitration, and how local court injunctions can block an agreed international arbitration. Contrast what the insured parties recovered with Enron's outcome.
- **Ch 69 (Thermal power).** Teach sizing and dispatch. A large inflexible base-load gas plant with take-or-pay LNG in a coal-dominated system destroys value even if it runs reliably. Contrast DPC's single-buyer PPA with designs that include merit-order dispatch, flexible fuel contracts and phased capacity tied to demand.

## Do not state

- Exact tariffs in rupees per kWh or US cents per kWh, or a single "Dabhol tariff" figure. Sources vary by phase, year and basis.
- That specific bribes were proven. Corruption was alleged (by the Munde committee and the Maharashtra government's pleadings) but not established in any verified judgment. Enron's "USD 20 million education fund" is reported in Hansen et al. only as something critics cited.
- The exact final settlement amounts paid to GE, Bechtel, OPIC or offshore lenders in 2005, such as "USD 160 million to Bechtel" or "USD 228 million to OPIC." These were not verified in primary sources.
- The precise Phase I commercial operation date (often given as May 1999). Use "early 1999."
- The exact Phase I versus Phase II split after renegotiation. Sources give 740 MW and a total of about 2,184 MW, but HRW gives Phase II as 1,320 MW, which does not add up.
- That the World Bank "opposed private power in India." Its letter objected to this project's size and least-cost position.
- Rebecca Mark's or other executives' words, except the exact text of the HRW-published letter, and even that should be paraphrased.
- The RGPPL restart date and its shareholding percentages beyond "NTPC-led with GAIL, MSEB Holding and Indian financial institutions." These were only partly verified.
