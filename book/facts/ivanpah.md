# Ivanpah CSP and the DOE loan guarantee

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: CPUC Resolution E-5429, adopted December 4, 2025 and issued December 10, 2025; a May 2026 press report says all three units were still running)

## Summary

The Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System is a concentrating solar power (CSP) plant on federal land managed by the Bureau of Land Management (BLM) at Ivanpah Dry Lake in San Bernardino County, California, near the Nevada border. It has three power-tower units with a combined nameplate capacity of 392 MW gross. BrightSource Energy developed it with its tower technology, NRG Energy and Google invested equity, and Bechtel built it under an EPC contract. Each unit is owned by its own project company (Solar Partners I, II and VIII), sells to an investor-owned utility under a long-term PPA, and has its own non-recourse loan. Unit 1 (Solar Partners II) and Unit 3 (Solar Partners VIII) sell to PG&E. Unit 2 sells to Southern California Edison (SCE). In April 2011 the Federal Financing Bank (FFB) agreed to lend up to USD 1.6 billion, guaranteed by the US Department of Energy (DOE) under Title XVII of the Energy Policy Act of 2005. It was the largest project in DOE's Loan Programs Office (LPO) portfolio at the time and the first commercial-scale solar tower plant in the US. Total project cost was reported at about USD 2.2 billion. The units entered commercial service in early 2014 and then struggled to meet the guaranteed energy production (GEP) in the PPAs. PG&E signed forbearance agreements in December 2015 and amended the PPAs in 2017. In December 2014, US Treasury Section 1603 cash grants of USD 485 million were used to repay the grant bridge loans under the DOE-guaranteed facility. In January 2025 the owners, with DOE's active support, agreed with PG&E to terminate the Unit 1 and Unit 3 PPAs early, so that DOE could recover more of its loan. In December 2025 the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) rejected those termination agreements without prejudice. It cited grid reliability and the risk of stranding more than USD 333 million of ratepayer-funded transmission. The DOE-guaranteed loan had not been fully repaid.

## Verified facts

1. Ivanpah has three CSP power-tower units with a total nameplate capacity of 392 MW. The plant is on BLM-managed federal land in southeastern California. DOE's 2016 congressional statement calls it the world's largest CSP plant at the time and the first commercial-scale deployment of solar thermal tower technology in the US. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. DOE's April 2011 Record of Decision describes the project as the "370 megawatt (MW)" Ivanpah Solar Electric Generating System (Units 1, 2 and 3) on 3,471.36 acres of BLM-managed land in San Bernardino County. The gap between 370 MW and 392 MW appears to be net versus gross capacity, but the ROD does not say so (see Do not state). [confidence: high] [source: 4]
3. The borrowers were Solar Partners I, LLC, Solar Partners II, LLC and Solar Partners VIII, LLC, one per unit. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
4. In April 2011 DOE issued USD 1.6 billion of loan guarantees under Title XVII of the Energy Policy Act of 2005. The lender was the Federal Financing Bank, part of the US Treasury. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4, 5, 6]
5. NRG's FY2014 10-K states that the Ivanpah Credit Agreement with the FFB was entered into on April 5, 2011, to borrow up to USD 1.6 billion. Each phase was governed by a separate financing agreement, and each was non-recourse to the other projects and to NRG. Borrowings accrue interest at a fixed rate based on US Treasury rates plus a spread of 0.375% and are secured by all of the assets of Ivanpah. IJGlobal dates financial close to April 11, 2011. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
6. As of December 31, 2014, borrowings under the Ivanpah Credit Agreement were USD 380 million for Solar Partners I (due June 27, 2033; weighted average rate 2.808%), USD 377 million for Solar Partners II (due February 27, 2038; 3.132%) and USD 426 million for Solar Partners VIII (due October 27, 2038; 3.097%). That is USD 1,183 million in total against maximum term borrowings of USD 1,219 million. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
7. NRG's FY2014 10-K reports that in December 2014 the company received USD 485 million of US Treasury cash grant proceeds for Ivanpah and used them to repay the cash grant bridge loan outstanding. Solar Partners I, II and VIII applied for USD 196 million, USD 189 million and USD 196 million (USD 581 million in total). Sequestration cut each award by USD 14 million, USD 18 million per unit remained pending Treasury review, and USD 164 million, USD 157 million and USD 164 million were received. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
8. IJGlobal reported at close a total project cost of about USD 2.2 billion and equity of USD 300 million from NRG, USD 168 million from Google and USD 132 million from BrightSource. It said Bechtel lent USD 20 million to a BrightSource subsidiary to help fund BrightSource's equity, and it reported a debt service reserve of USD 57 million and a solar field guarantee escrow of USD 108.6 million. [confidence: medium] [source: 6]
9. Bechtel was the EPC contractor. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7]
10. NRG's FY2014 10-K lists NRG's ownership interest in Ivanpah as 50.1%, with 378 MW attributed to the facility in its asset table. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
11. DOE stated that the plant would generate 940,000 MWh a year. It said construction was expected to create 1,000 construction jobs and support 61 permanent jobs, and that the supply chain drew on more than 18 states. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. Construction began in 2010. DOE says commercial operation was reached in January 2014. NRG announced on February 13, 2014 that the plant was operational, and Energy Secretary Moniz dedicated it the same day. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 8]
13. PG&E's PPAs were first approved by the CPUC on August 20, 2009 (Resolution E-4266) for 110 MW from Unit I and 200 MW from Unit III. An amendment approved on October 28, 2010 (Resolution E-4369) changed the contracted capacity to 118 MW from Unit I and 130 MW from Unit III. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
14. In the CPUC's December 2025 resolution, the PG&E contracts are listed as Unit I (Solar Partners II, 126 MW nameplate, delivery January 21, 2014 to January 20, 2039) and Unit III (Solar Partners VIII, 133 MW, January 27, 2014 to January 26, 2039). Unit II is contracted to SCE for 133 MW. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
15. The units had unexpected difficulty meeting the contractual GEP. PG&E and Solar Partners signed forbearance agreements under which PG&E would take no step toward declaring an event of default from December 18, 2015 through July 31, 2016, in exchange for payments covering any GEP shortfalls. The CPUC approved them on March 17, 2016 (Resolution E-4771). [confidence: high] [source: 2, 9]
16. Both PG&E units met GEP during the forbearance period, but Unit 3 was expected to fall short after it ended. A contract amendment approved on May 11, 2017 (Resolution E-4841) gave PG&E curtailment rights, limited the deliveries PG&E must pay for at the full contract price, and let Solar Partners cure future GEP failures through further performance and financial payments. A failure to cure can still lead to an event of default. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
17. The CPUC notes that the PG&E–Ivanpah PPA requires PG&E to pay for the plant's output but relieves PG&E of payment if the plant does not perform. If certain key obligations are breached, PG&E owes no compensation to terminate. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
18. On January 17, 2025, PG&E filed Advice Letter 7485-E asking approval of contract termination agreements (CTAs) with Solar Partners II and VIII. Under them PG&E would pay to terminate the Unit 1 and Unit 3 PPAs. The CTAs came out of PG&E's 2023 request for information under the CPUC's Voluntary Allocation and Market Offer (VAMO) process for reducing uneconomic legacy contracts. They were contingent on a similar agreement with SCE for Unit 2, and as of November 4, 2025 SCE had filed none. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
19. DOE filed a letter supporting the CTAs on January 17, 2025. In it, DOE said it had worked with Solar Partners to maximize repayment of federal funds and had coordinated the CTA proposals. It also said it was coordinating negotiations with SCE on terminating Unit II's contract. The CPUC records that "The $1.6 billion loan has not yet been fully repaid." [confidence: high] [source: 2]
20. On December 4, 2025 the CPUC adopted Resolution E-5429, which rejected the CTAs in their entirety without prejudice. It was issued December 10, 2025. The CPUC accepted that PG&E's RFI process was reasonable and fair. Its reasons for rejecting were grid reliability uncertainty, including changes in federal renewable policy, and the risk of stranding ratepayer-funded transmission. Ratepayer costs for the Eldorado–Ivanpah Transmission Project exceeded USD 333 million, which the CPUC found greater than the CTAs' estimated savings. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
21. A May 2026 press report states that all three units remained in operation after the CPUC decision. It also says SCE was still negotiating over Unit 2 and that NRG had discussed possible repowering with photovoltaic arrays. [confidence: medium] [source: 10]

## Financing and structure details

- Borrowers: three single-unit project companies (Solar Partners I, II and VIII). Each has its own financing agreement and is non-recourse to the others and to NRG. [source: 5]
- Lender: Federal Financing Bank. Guarantor: DOE under Title XVII of EPAct 2005. The headline amount was USD 1.6 billion. [source: 1, 4, 5]
- Pricing: fixed rate set at Treasury rates plus 0.375%, as stated in NRG's 10-K. IJGlobal says each draw was referenced to the federal government's borrowing cost at the time. [source: 5, 6]
- Term tranches as of December 31, 2014: SP I up to USD 392 million, due June 27, 2033. SP II up to USD 387 million, due February 27, 2038. SP VIII up to USD 440 million, due October 27, 2038. Total USD 1,219 million. [source: 5]
- Grant bridge: part of the facility bridged the expected Section 1603 cash grants. USD 485 million of grant receipts repaid it in December 2014. This explains why the term debt (about USD 1.2 billion) is smaller than the USD 1.6 billion headline. [source: 5]
- Equity at close (IJGlobal): NRG USD 300 million, Google USD 168 million, BrightSource USD 132 million (partly funded by a USD 20 million Bechtel loan). [source: 6]
- Reserves reported at close: USD 57 million debt service reserve and a USD 108.6 million solar field guarantee escrow. [source: 6]
- Offtake: 25-year PPAs with PG&E for Units 1 and 3, running to January 2039. SCE buys Unit 2's output. Payment is for delivered output, with GEP obligations and default-based termination rights. [source: 2]
- EPC: Bechtel. [source: 6, 7]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Performance against contract: the CPUC records "unexpected difficulties" in meeting GEP after commercial operation, which needed forbearance in 2015–16 and PPA amendments in 2017. [source: 2] For the lender, the PPA was only as strong as the plant's ability to deliver contracted energy. Non-performance relieves the offtaker of payment and, after certain defaults, of any termination compensation. [source: 2]
- Technology-cost competition: DOE told the CPUC in 2025 that terminating the PPAs would cut above-market costs for ratepayers and could allow the site to be redeveloped "with a more cost competitive technology." PG&E's case was that the contracts had become excess or uneconomic. [source: 2]
- Lender recovery depends on regulators: the 2025 plan was a negotiated exit. The utility would pay to terminate, the owners would repay federal debt, and the site might be repowered. The plan needed CPUC approval, and the CPUC weighed reliability and stranded transmission over the estimated savings. [source: 2]
- What went right: the plant was built and has operated for more than a decade. The borrowers were current on DOE debt service as of July 2016. The Section 1603 grants were applied to cut DOE-guaranteed debt in 2014. [source: 1, 5]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 3 (How project finance evolved): Ivanpah is the signature project of the 2009–2011 US federal clean-energy credit push, Title XVII loan guarantees plus Section 1603 cash grants. Use it to show how public credit enhancement made first-of-a-kind technology financeable when commercial lenders would not take the technology risk. Then show how fast-falling PV costs left the CSP plant above market within a decade.
- Chapter 70 (Onshore wind and solar): Use the PG&E PPA mechanics as a worked example of output-based solar offtake. Cover guaranteed energy production, look-back testing, forbearance, cure through payments, and the offtaker's right to terminate without compensation after default. Contrast tower CSP's ramp-up and performance risk with mature PV, and show why lenders size debt on demonstrated technology and P90 output.
- Chapter 88 (The frontier: evaluating new structures): Ivanpah is a case study in evaluating a first-of-a-kind structure. Ask who bears technology ramp-up risk, how a public guarantor recovers when the asset becomes uneconomic, and what regulators can block. The 2025 termination attempt shows that a public lender's exit can depend on third parties such as the utility regulator, transmission-cost recovery and federal land policy.

## Do not state

- That Ivanpah reached commercial operation on December 31, 2013. Some secondary sources say so, but DOE and the CPUC say January 2014 and NRG's 10-K says "early 2014." Use "early 2014."
- The specific Title XVII section (1703 or 1705). IJGlobal says section 1703, but this was not confirmed from DOE's own records. Say "Title XVII of the Energy Policy Act of 2005."
- Exact annual generation figures for 2014–2015 (for example, "about 420,000 MWh in 2014"). They are widely repeated from EIA data, but were not checked against EIA here. Say only that output fell short of contractual GEP in early years.
- Google's and BrightSource's exact percentage ownership, or the current ownership split after BrightSource's later restructuring. Only NRG's 50.1% (FY2014) is verified.
- Exact natural gas consumption, bird mortality figures or "solar flux" death counts. These are contested and were not verified. DOE's 2025 letter refers only generally to reduced avian exposure and lower emissions from gas-fired systems.
- That the plant has closed or will close on a particular date. As of the latest source (May 2026), the PG&E terminations had been rejected and the plant was reported operating.
- The outstanding DOE loan balance today, the termination payment amounts (confidential in the CPUC record) and any loss to taxpayers.
- That the 370 MW in the ROD is "net" capacity. This is plausible but not stated in the source.
