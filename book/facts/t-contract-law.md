# Contract law for financiers: the penalty rule (Cavendish v Makdessi), frustration, civil-law hardship (French Civil Code art. 1195), FIDIC 2017 suite and 2022 reprint, NEC4

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: FIDIC's November 2022 reprint of the 2017 Rainbow Suite, with the third set of amendments effective 1 January 2023; no later change to the English penalty or frustration rules has been verified)

## Summary

The doctrines in this sheet decide whether the risk allocation in a project's contracts survives a dispute. Under English law, the penalty rule was restated by the UK Supreme Court in *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Talal El Makdessi; ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67 (4 November 2015). The old question was whether a clause was a "genuine pre-estimate of loss". The new question is whether a secondary obligation triggered by breach imposes a detriment out of all proportion to the innocent party's legitimate interest in performance. In a negotiated contract between properly advised parties of comparable bargaining power, there is a strong initial presumption that the parties are the best judges of what is legitimate. That is why delay and performance liquidated damages (LDs) in EPC contracts are rarely struck down in England. *Triple Point v PTT* [2021] UKSC 29 (see facts/triple-point.md) then settled how delay LDs behave on termination.

Frustration in English law is deliberately narrow. *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) excused performance when the hired music hall burned down. *Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC* [1956] AC 696 refused relief to a builder whose eight-month job took 22 months because of labour shortages: frustration requires performance to become "radically different", not merely more expensive. *Tsakiroglou v Noblee Thorl* [1962] AC 93 (the Suez closure) held that a more expensive but available route does not frustrate a CIF sale. The Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 then governs restitution.

Civil-law systems go further. Since 1 October 2016, article 1195 of the French Civil Code lets a party facing an unforeseeable change of circumstances that makes performance excessively onerous request renegotiation. If no agreement is reached within a reasonable time, a judge may revise or end the contract. French administrative law had recognised *imprévision* for public contracts since *Gaz de Bordeaux* (Conseil d'État, 30 March 1916). The UNIDROIT Principles (2016 edition) contain a similar hardship regime (arts 6.2.1–6.2.3) and allow courts to reduce "grossly excessive" agreed payments (art. 7.4.13).

On the construction side, FIDIC published Second Editions of its Red, Yellow and Silver Books in 2017. It issued errata in December 2018 and a memorandum in June 2019, and reprinted all three "with amendments" in November 2022, effective 1 January 2023. NEC4, published in 2017, offers six main payment options (A–F) ranging from priced to cost-reimbursable and management contracts.

## Verified facts

**A. The penalty rule: Cavendish v Makdessi (England and Wales; as of 2026-10-03)**

1. Citation and court: *Cavendish Square Holding BV v Talal El Makdessi* and *ParkingEye Ltd v Beavis* [2015] UKSC 67. The two appeals were heard together and judgment was given on 4 November 2015. The bench was Lord Neuberger (President), Lord Mance, Lord Clarke, Lord Sumption, Lord Carnwath, Lord Toulson and Lord Hodge. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. Lord Neuberger and Lord Sumption gave the lead joint judgment, with which Lord Clarke and Lord Carnwath agreed. Lord Mance and Lord Hodge gave concurring judgments. Lord Toulson agreed in *Cavendish* but dissented in *ParkingEye* (on the Unfair Terms in Consumer Contracts Regulations 1999). [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. The restated test (joint judgment, para 32): the true test is whether the impugned provision is a secondary obligation that imposes a detriment on the contract-breaker out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in enforcing the primary obligation. The innocent party can have no proper interest in simply punishing the defaulter. The Court called the concepts of "deterrence" and "genuine pre-estimate of loss" unhelpful. [confidence: high, paraphrase of para 32 and the press summary] [source: 1, 2]
4. Lord Mance's two-step formulation, which the press summary records as agreeing with the joint test: first, identify whether any legitimate business interest is served and protected by the clause; second, ask whether the provision made for that interest is extravagant, exorbitant or unconscionable. Lord Hodge's formulation asks whether the stipulated sum or remedy is exorbitant or unconscionable having regard to the innocent party's interest in performance. A damages clause is a penalty if there is an extravagant disproportion between the stipulated sum and the highest level of damages that could possibly arise from the breach. Lord Toulson agreed with Lord Hodge's formulation. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
5. For a straightforward damages clause, the joint judgment said the innocent party's legitimate interest will rarely extend beyond compensation. It expected Lord Dunedin's four tests from *Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre v New Garage* [1915] AC 79 to be usually adequate in such cases. [confidence: high] [source: 1, para 32]
6. The rule applies only to secondary obligations, meaning remedies triggered by breach. It does not regulate the fairness of primary obligations. It can extend beyond money payments to clauses withholding payments, requiring transfer of assets, and forfeiting deposits. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
7. Para 35 (joint judgment): "In a negotiated contract between properly advised parties of comparable bargaining power, the strong initial presumption must be that the parties themselves are the best judges of what is legitimate in a provision dealing with the consequences of breach." [confidence: high, exact words from the judgment] [source: 1]
8. The Court declined to abolish the penalty rule (as some had urged) and declined to extend it. In particular, it declined to follow the High Court of Australia's extension in *Andrews v ANZ Banking Group* (2012) 247 CLR 205 to clauses not triggered by breach. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
9. *Cavendish* facts: by an agreement of 28 February 2008, Mr Makdessi and Mr Ghossoub sold shares in the holding company of the largest advertising and marketing group in the Middle East to a WPP company, later substituted by Cavendish. Cavendish's holding rose to 60%; the sellers kept 40%. The price was a USD 34m completion payment, a USD 31.5m second payment, and interim and final payments based on 2007–2011 operating profit, capped in total at USD 147.5m. The agreement was negotiated over six months, with Allen & Overy acting for Cavendish and Lewis Silkin for the sellers. [confidence: high] [source: 1, paras 46–49]
10. Clause 5.1 removed a defaulting seller's right to the interim and final payments if he breached the restrictive covenants. Clause 5.6 let Cavendish buy his remaining shares at a price that excluded goodwill. For Mr Makdessi, clause 5.1 meant receiving up to USD 44,181,600 less. The Supreme Court held both clauses were primary obligations (price adjustment and option terms) and outside the penalty rule. It added that, even if the rule applied, Cavendish had a legitimate interest in protecting goodwill. The appeal was allowed. [confidence: high] [source: 1, para 67; 2]
11. *ParkingEye* facts and outcome: a GBP 85 charge for overstaying a free two-hour limit at a retail park (Mr Beavis overstayed by almost an hour on 15 April 2013). The penalty rule was engaged but the charge was not a penalty. The landowner and operator had legitimate interests in managing parking and funding the scheme, and the charge was not extravagant or unconscionable. The majority was 6–1. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
12. In *Triple Point Technology v PTT* [2021] UKSC 29, an agreed delay rate of 0.1% of undelivered work per day was treated as liquidated damages despite being labelled "penalty". The Supreme Court held that LDs accrue up to termination and survive it. Details are in facts/triple-point.md. [confidence: high] [source: facts/triple-point.md]

**B. Frustration (England and Wales; as of 2026-10-03)**

13. *Taylor v Caldwell* [1863] EWHC QB J1, (1863) 3 B & S 826, Court of Queen's Bench, judgment of Blackburn J, 6 May 1863. The defendants agreed to let the Surrey Gardens Music Hall for four concert days in summer 1861 at GBP 100 a day. On 11 June 1861 the hall was destroyed by an accidental fire. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
14. Blackburn J held that, where performance depends on the continued existence of a thing, the contract is subject to an implied condition that the parties are excused if performance becomes impossible from the perishing of the thing without default of the contractor. [confidence: high, paraphrase of the judgment] [source: 3]
15. *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696, [1956] UKHL 3, House of Lords, 19 April 1956. By a contract of 9 July 1946 the contractor agreed to build 78 houses at Gudgheath Lane, Fareham, within eight months for GBP 85,836. Mainly because of a lack of skilled labour, the work took 22 months. The contractor was paid the contract price with adjustments, GBP 94,424. It claimed the contract was frustrated and sought more on a quantum meruit. The House of Lords dismissed the appeal. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
16. Lord Radcliffe's statement in *Davis*: "frustration occurs whenever the law recognises that without default of either party a contractual obligation has become incapable of being performed because the circumstances in which performance is called for would render it a thing radically different from that which was undertaken by the contract. Non haec in foedera veni. It was not this that I promised to do." He added that hardship, inconvenience or material loss alone does not engage the doctrine. [confidence: high, exact words from the judgment] [source: 5, 6]
17. *Tsakiroglou & Co Ltd v Noblee Thorl GmbH* [1962] AC 93 (House of Lords) is one of the "Suez cases". Later House of Lords authority describes its principle this way: a seller of goods cannot normally invoke frustration where the contemplated mode of performance becomes impossible but another, more expensive mode that is not fundamentally different remains available. *Tsakiroglou* also treated whether a CIF contract was frustrated as ultimately a question of law. [confidence: high for the principle and citation; the detailed facts were not read in a primary source, see Do not state] [source: 7, 8]
18. Modern approach: in *Edwinton Commercial Corp v Tsavliris Russ (The Sea Angel)* [2007] EWCA Civ 547, Rix LJ described the application of frustration as a "multi-factorial" exercise. The factors include the contract terms, its context, the parties' knowledge, expectations and assumptions about risk at the time of contract, the nature of the supervening event, and the parties' reasonable expectations of performance in the new circumstances. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
19. *National Carriers Ltd v Panalpina (Northern) Ltd* [1981] AC 675 (judgment 11 December 1980) held that frustration can in principle apply to leases. Lord Simon of Glaisdale's formulation requires a supervening event, without default of either party and for which the contract makes no sufficient provision, that significantly changes the nature (not merely the expense or onerousness) of the outstanding contractual rights or obligations. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 8]
20. *Canary Wharf (BP4) T1 Ltd v European Medicines Agency* [2019] EWHC 335 (Ch), 20 February 2019 (Marcus Smith J): the UK's withdrawal from the EU did not frustrate the EMA's 25-year lease of 25 Churchill Place (term 1 July 2014 to 30 June 2039). The judge held, among other findings, that any frustration would have been self-induced. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
21. The consequences of frustration in England are governed by the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943, which allows money paid to be recovered and benefits conferred to be compensated as the court considers just. Section 2(5) excludes certain contracts, including some charterparties and insurance. [confidence: medium, statutory detail via secondary and judicial references] [source: 8, 10]

**C. Civil-law hardship and penalties (France; as of 2026-10-03)**

22. Article 1195 of the French Civil Code was inserted by Ordonnance n° 2016-131 of 10 February 2016 reforming the law of contract. Under article 9 of the ordinance, the reform took effect on 1 October 2016, and contracts concluded before that date remain governed by the prior law. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
23. Article 1195, first paragraph (original French): "Si un changement de circonstances imprévisible lors de la conclusion du contrat rend l'exécution excessivement onéreuse pour une partie qui n'avait pas accepté d'en assumer le risque, celle-ci peut demander une renégociation du contrat à son cocontractant. Elle continue à exécuter ses obligations durant la renégociation." [confidence: high, exact text from the ordinance as published] [source: 11]
24. Article 1195, second paragraph (paraphrase): if renegotiation is refused or fails, the parties may agree to terminate the contract on dates and terms they set, or jointly ask the judge to adapt it. If they do not agree within a reasonable time, the judge may, at one party's request, revise the contract or end it on the date and terms the judge fixes. [confidence: high for the substance; only the final sentence was read verbatim] [source: 11]
25. Before 2016, French private law refused judicial revision for changed circumstances (the *Canal de Craponne* line, Cour de cassation, 1876). French administrative law instead developed *imprévision* in *Compagnie générale d'éclairage de Bordeaux* (Conseil d'État, 30 March 1916). There, a gas-lighting concessionaire hit by a roughly five-fold rise in coal prices during the First World War had to keep supplying the service but was entitled to an indemnity for the extra-contractual burden. The court did not rewrite the tariff. [confidence: high for Gaz de Bordeaux; medium for the Canal de Craponne date, not read in a primary source] [source: 12]
26. Article 1218 of the French Civil Code (same 2016 reform) defines contractual force majeure as an event beyond the debtor's control that could not reasonably have been foreseen at contracting and whose effects cannot be avoided by appropriate measures, which prevents performance. A temporary impediment suspends performance unless the delay justifies termination. A permanent impediment terminates the contract by operation of law. [confidence: high] [source: 13]
27. Article 1231-5 of the French Civil Code allows the judge, even of their own motion, to moderate or increase an agreed penalty (*clause pénale*) that is manifestly excessive or derisory. French law therefore enforces agreed penalties but polices their amount, a different technique from England's binary penalty test. [confidence: high for the judicial power; paraphrased] [source: 11]
28. UNIDROIT Principles of International Commercial Contracts, 2016 edition (the latest edition): art. 6.2.1 says a contract must be performed even if it becomes more onerous, subject to the hardship provisions. Art. 6.2.2 defines hardship as events that fundamentally alter the equilibrium of the contract, by raising the cost of performance or reducing the value received, where the events (a) occur or become known after conclusion, (b) could not reasonably have been taken into account, (c) are beyond the disadvantaged party's control and (d) were not assumed by that party. Art. 6.2.3 entitles the disadvantaged party to request renegotiation without undue delay, and the request alone does not entitle it to withhold performance. Art. 7.4.13 allows an agreed payment for non-performance to be reduced to a reasonable amount where it is grossly excessive. [confidence: high] [source: 14]

**D. FIDIC 2017 suite and 2022 reprint (international standard forms; as of 2026-10-03)**

29. FIDIC published Second Editions in 2017 of the Conditions of Contract for Construction (Red Book), for Plant and Design-Build (Yellow Book) and for EPC/Turnkey Projects (Silver Book), updating the 1999 First Editions. [confidence: high] [source: 15, 16]
30. FIDIC describes the 2017 changes as providing: greater detail and clarity on notices and communications; Employer's and Contractor's claims treated equally and separated from disputes; mechanisms for dispute avoidance; and detailed provisions for quality management and verification of the Contractor's compliance. [confidence: high, paraphrase of FIDIC's product descriptions] [source: 15, 16]
31. The 2017 Silver Book uses a 28-day period for giving a Notice of Claim and an 84-day period, under Sub-Clause 20.2.4, for a fully detailed Claim, measured from when the claiming party became or should have become aware of the event. Disputes go to a Dispute Avoidance/Adjudication Board (DAAB) under Clause 21. [confidence: high for these references as they appear in FIDIC's amendments document; full clause text not read] [source: 17]
32. FIDIC states that the Silver Book is not suitable where: tenderers have insufficient time or information to scrutinise the Employer's Requirements or to carry out design, risk assessment and estimating; construction involves substantial underground work or other areas tenderers cannot inspect, unless special provisions deal with unforeseen conditions; or the Employer intends to supervise closely or control the Contractor's work or review most construction drawings. In those cases FIDIC recommends the Yellow Book for Contractor-designed works. [confidence: high] [source: 16]
33. Amendment history: first set of amendments (errata) December 2018; second set (memorandum of additional errata) June 2019; third set published November 2022, effective 1 January 2023. FIDIC reprinted the Red, Yellow and Silver Books "with amendments" in November 2022. Among other changes, the 2022 amendments rename the "Dispute Avoidance/Adjudication Agreement" as the "DAAB Agreement" throughout. [confidence: high] [source: 15, 17, 18]
34. Other current FIDIC forms on FIDIC's bookshop as of 2026-10-03 include: the Conditions of Contract for Underground Works (Emerald Book, 2019, reprinted 2023 with amendments); the Short Form of Contract (Green Book, 2nd ed. 2021); the Client/Consultant Model Services Agreement (White Book, 5th ed. 2017); and a FIDIC Contracts Guide (2nd ed. 2022) covering the 2017 books as reprinted in 2022. FIDIC also publishes Carbon Emissions Management (CEM) guidance and annexes for use with the Red Book 2017 (2022 reprint). [confidence: high] [source: 19]

**E. NEC4 (UK-origin standard forms; as of 2026-10-03)**

35. NEC4 was published in June 2017 as the successor to NEC3. It added the Design Build and Operate Contract (DBO), the Alliance Contract (ALC) and the Facilities Management Contract (FMC). NEC says all changes came from industry feedback and that NEC3's style, layout and core project-management processes were kept. [confidence: high] [source: 20]
36. The NEC4 Engineering and Construction Contract (ECC) and its Subcontract (ECS) share five main payment options: A (priced contract with activity schedule), B (priced contract with bill of quantities), C (target contract with activity schedule), D (target contract with bill of quantities) and E (cost reimbursable contract). The ECC also has Option F (management contract). NEC describes the range as moving from most risk with the supplier (A, B), through risk sharing (C, D), to most risk with the client (E, F). [confidence: high] [source: 21]
37. NEC4 families cover works (ECC, Engineering and Construction Short Contract, ALC, DBO), services (Professional Service Contract, Term Service Contract, FMC and short forms) and supply (Supply Contract, Supply Short Contract). NEC describes its approach as requiring proactive management of risk and change, an up-to-date programme and a spirit of mutual trust and collaboration. [confidence: high] [source: 20, 22]

## Financing and structure details (parties, tranches, amounts, tenors, guarantees, where verified)

Not a financing case. The contract-law points below map onto project finance documents.

- **LD regimes in EPC contracts.** Delay and performance LDs are secondary obligations, so the penalty rule is engaged. After *Cavendish* the question is proportionality to the employer's legitimate interest. For a project company, that interest plausibly includes debt service, PPA or offtake penalties and lost revenue during delay. The strong presumption for negotiated contracts between advised parties (fact 7) makes English-law LDs in sophisticated EPC contracts hard to attack.
- **Termination and LDs.** *Triple Point* (facts/triple-point.md): delay LDs accrue to termination and survive it; general damages apply afterwards.
- **Change-of-circumstances risk.** Under English law, cost escalation does not frustrate (*Davis*, *Tsakiroglou*). Price and supply risk therefore sits wherever the contract puts it: escalation clauses, change-in-law, force majeure, and risk/reward options such as NEC4 Options C–F. Under French-law contracts, article 1195 offers a default renegotiation and judicial revision route unless the parties accepted the risk. Whether it can be excluded by contract is noted under Do not state.
- **FIDIC Silver Book as the lender-friendly default.** Lenders favour the Silver Book's single-point, lump-sum, design-and-build allocation, but FIDIC itself warns against using it where tenderers cannot assess ground conditions or where the employer will closely control design (fact 32).

## What went wrong or right, and why (well-established analysis only, attributed to named sources)

- The Supreme Court in *Cavendish* (joint judgment, para 3, as quoted in the press summary) described the penalty rule as an "ancient, haphazardly constructed edifice which has not weathered well". It nonetheless kept the rule and reframed it around legitimate interest, rejecting both abolition and the Australian extension in *Andrews*. [source: 1, 2]
- Rix LJ in *The Sea Angel* rejected a rule-based approach to frustration in favour of a multi-factorial assessment, with Lord Radcliffe's "radically different" test as the overall touchstone. [source: 6]
- FIDIC's own guidance on Silver Book suitability (fact 32) reflects long-standing practitioner criticism of using EPC/turnkey forms for tunnelling and other uninspectable ground conditions. The Emerald Book (2019) was created for underground works. [source: 16, 19]

## Teaching angles by chapter (for each listed chapter: the transferable lesson and the angle to take, 2–4 sentences)

- **Chapter 10 (Law for financiers).** Teach the primary/secondary obligation distinction first. It explains why a price-adjustment or option clause can escape the penalty rule entirely (*Cavendish* clauses 5.1 and 5.6) while an LD clause cannot. Then contrast England's narrow frustration doctrine (*Davis*: the cost overrun is the contractor's problem) with France's article 1195 and UNIDROIT hardship. The point to draw is that governing law is itself a risk-allocation choice. Use Case P's draft PPA clause to ask: if gas prices quadruple, what does each legal system do with this contract?
- **Chapter 22 (The EPC contract).** Use *Cavendish* to show how delay LDs should be calibrated: tie the daily rate to identifiable legitimate interests (debt service, offtaker penalties, lost margin) and document that reasoning. *Triple Point* explains what happens to accrued LDs on termination. *Davis* shows why a lump-sum date-certain EPC contractor carries labour and materials shortages unless the contract says otherwise. The FIDIC 2017 claims machinery (28-day Notice of Claim, 84-day fully detailed Claim) is the procedural side of the same allocation.
- **Chapter 23 (Construction structures beyond EPC).** Present the FIDIC suite as a menu matched to who designs and who controls: Red (employer design), Yellow (contractor design, engineer administers), Silver (EPC/turnkey), Emerald (underground), Green (short form). Use FIDIC's own unsuitability criteria for the Silver Book. Contrast NEC4's Options A–F, which separate payment risk from design responsibility and make target-cost and cost-reimbursable risk sharing a standard choice. Note the 2022 reprint as a version-control lesson: name the edition, the reprint and the amendment set in every contract.

## Do not state (claims commonly repeated but unverified or wrong, and uncertain details)

- Do not say *Cavendish* abolished the "genuine pre-estimate of loss" test outright. The Court called it unhelpful as the governing test but said Lord Dunedin's tests will usually suffice for straightforward damages clauses (fact 5).
- Do not say the penalty rule applies to primary obligations or to clauses not triggered by breach in English law. That is the Australian *Andrews* position, which the UK Supreme Court declined to follow.
- Do not give *Tsakiroglou*'s detailed facts as verified (Sudanese groundnuts, Port Sudan to Hamburg, closure of the Suez Canal in November 1956, the length and cost of the route via the Cape of Good Hope). These are widely repeated in textbooks but were not read in a primary source for this sheet. State only the principle and citation (fact 17), or verify against the law report.
- Do not quote the second paragraph of article 1195 verbatim. Only its final sentence was read verbatim.
- Do not state as settled that article 1195 is always excludable by contract, or that it applies to particular financial transactions. French practice commonly treats it as non-mandatory, and a 2018 ratification law carved out certain financial instruments, but neither point was verified in a primary source here.
- Do not give the exact date or citation of *Canal de Craponne* as primary-verified (commonly cited as Cass. civ., 6 March 1876).
- Do not cite FIDIC clause numbers beyond those verified here (Sub-Clause 20.2.4, Clause 21 DAAB, the 28/84-day periods). In particular, do not cite the numbering or content of the liability cap, delay damages or unforeseeable-physical-conditions clauses without checking the 2017 text as reprinted in 2022.
- Do not state the FIDIC Gold Book (DBO) or the MDB Harmonised ("Pink Book") edition status as current. They were not checked here.
- Do not cite NEC4 clause numbers (for example "clause 10.1") or the dates and contents of specific NEC4 amendment issues. They were not verified here.
- Do not state NEC's corporate ownership (commonly described as part of the Institution of Civil Engineers group) without checking; it was not verified here.
- Do not describe the Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943 in detail (section numbers, valuation of benefits) without checking legislation.gov.uk.
