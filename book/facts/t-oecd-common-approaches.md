# OECD Common Approaches for officially supported export credits and environmental and social due diligence (2012, revised 2016 and 2024)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: the March 20, 2024 amendment of OECD/LEGAL/0393; the OECD compendium shows no later amendment as of this date, though a substantive review was under way)

## Summary

The "Common Approaches" set out how export credit agencies (ECAs) in OECD countries screen, classify, review and disclose the environmental and social impacts of the projects they support. The full title is the Recommendation of the Council on Common Approaches for Officially Supported Export Credits and Environmental and Social Due Diligence (OECD/LEGAL/0393).

- **Legal nature.** It is an OECD Council Recommendation: a political commitment, not a legally binding Decision. It is separate from the Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits, which governs financial terms.
- **History.** It was first adopted for the environment only in 2003 and revised in 2007. It was readopted under its current name on June 28, 2012, revised in 2016 (human rights, use of international standards), and amended in March 2024. The 2024 amendment updated the benchmarking standards after the World Bank replaced its Safeguard Policies with the Environmental and Social Framework.
- **How it works.**
  - Every application with a repayment term of two years or more is screened.
  - Projects in or near sensitive areas, or where the ECA's share is SDR 10 million or more, are classified A, B or C.
  - Category A and B projects are benchmarked against international standards. Since 2024 the default benchmark is all eight IFC Performance Standards, plus the World Bank Group EHS Guidelines. For sovereign obligors, ECAs may use the World Bank Environmental and Social Standards instead.
  - For Category A projects, ECAs disclose project information and require the ESIA to be made public at least 30 calendar days before final commitment.

In a project financing with ECA tranches, the Common Approaches are what bring ECA E&S requirements into line with the IFC Performance Standards and Equator Principles that commercial banks and DFIs apply.

## Verified facts

Unless stated otherwise, rules are from the text of OECD/LEGAL/0393 as amended on March 20, 2024 (OECD Legal Instruments PDF, © OECD 2025). The current text is on the OECD Legal Instruments compendium.

**Status, history, scope**
1. **Adoption and revisions.** The OECD Council adopted the Recommendation on June 28, 2012, on the proposal of the Working Party on Export Credits and Credit Guarantees (ECG). It was revised in 2016 and in 2024. The OECD compendium records the last amendment as March 20, 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. **Predecessors.** The first Recommendation (2003) covered environmental impacts only. A 2007 revision added references to social impacts. The 2012 renaming was meant to put social impacts "on an equal footing" with environmental ones. The 2016 revisions aimed to enhance the use of international standards, improve common practices, address project-related human rights impacts, and continue outreach. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. **Legal nature.** OECD Recommendations are "not legally binding" but represent a political commitment, with an expectation that Adherents will do their best to implement them. All OECD Members are ECG members except Chile, Costa Rica and Iceland, which have no medium- and long-term official export credit programs. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. **Scope.** It applies to all types of officially supported export credits for capital goods and/or services with a repayment term of two years or more, except military equipment and agricultural commodities. A footnote recognizes that not all ECA products are in scope, for example products where the risk is on the exporter, such as bonds and working capital, with no linked buyer-risk application. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

**Screening and classification**
5. **Screening criteria.** ECAs should screen all in-scope applications as early as possible. Screening identifies:
   - whether the application relates to a "project" or an "existing operation";
   - whether the location is in or near a sensitive area;
   - whether there is a high likelihood of severe project-related human rights impacts;
   - whether the Adherent's share is SDR 10 million or more. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. **Which applications must be classified or assessed.**
   - ECAs shall classify all projects in or near sensitive areas, whatever their share, and all projects where their share is SDR 10 million or more.
   - Existing operations of SDR 10 million or more must be assessed for E&S risk.
   - So must any application where screening shows a high likelihood of severe human rights impacts.
   - Below those thresholds, and outside sensitive areas, no further action is required beyond screening. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. **Categories.**
   - **A:** potential significant adverse impacts that are diverse, irreversible and/or unprecedented. This in principle includes sensitive sectors and projects in or near sensitive areas.
   - **B:** less adverse, typically site-specific impacts, with mitigation readily available.
   - **C:** minimal or no adverse impacts.

   Annex I gives an illustrative Category A list, originally sourced from EBRD's 2003 policy. Infrastructure entries include:
   - thermal power stations of 300 MW heat output or more;
   - nuclear plants;
   - motorways and express roads, and new or widened roads of 10 km or more;
   - long-distance railways;
   - airports with runways of 2,100 m or more;
   - ports for vessels over 1,350 tonnes;
   - large dams (ICOLD definition);
   - overhead transmission lines of 15 km or more at 110 kV or above;
   - large-scale oil, gas and LNG developments;
   - large mining. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

**Review and standards**
8. **The review.** For Category A projects, ECAs should require an ESIA, provided by the applicant. Annex II lists the typical contents, based on IFC 2007 guidance. An ESIA "should not be carried out and reviewed by the same party." Category B review scope varies by project. For Category C, nothing is required beyond screening and classification. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. **Benchmark standards (2024 text, paragraphs 21–25).**
   - ECAs should benchmark projects against the relevant aspects of all eight IFC Performance Standards.
   - Where projects involve sovereign obligors, they may instead use all ten World Bank Environmental and Social Standards.
   - Where a Major Multilateral Financial Institution supports the project, they may use that institution's standards. The listed institutions are AfDB, AsDB, EBRD, EIB, IDB, IBRD, IFC and MIGA.
   - ECAs should also benchmark against the World Bank Group EHS Guidelines.
   - Where no sector EHS Guideline exists, they should use other internationally recognized standards (for nuclear, IAEA standards and the relevant conventions) and may refer to other guidance (for hydropower, the Hydropower Sustainability Assessment Protocol and the World Commission on Dams).
   - More stringent standards, such as EU standards, may be used. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. **What the 2024 amendment changed.** The OECD background note says the March 2024 revisions were "minor." They updated the benchmarking provisions given (a) changes to the World Bank standards and (b) the increasing use of the IFC Performance Standards beyond limited or non-recourse project finance. The same 2024 report to Council found that substantive elements may need revision, in particular on biodiversity, climate change and human rights. The ECG has begun a substantive review of all elements. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. **Compliance and exceptions.** Projects should comply with host-country standards in all cases, and should meet the international standards they are benchmarked against, recognizing margins of tolerance. In exceptional cases an ECA may support a non-compliant project. It must then report the reasons and justification to the ECG, and aggregated information is made public. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. **Human rights.** Where there is a high likelihood of severe project-related human rights impacts, review "may need to be complemented by specific human rights due diligence." Examples given include threats to life, child or forced labor, large-scale resettlement, and conflict settings. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. **Reliance on other reviews.** An ECA supporting only a minor part of a project, or acting in reinsurance, may rely on the review of another Adherent, a Major Multilateral Financial Institution, or a development agency. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
14. **Associated facilities.** The definition covers facilities that would not be built without the project and on which its viability depends. ECAs should assess their impacts "where appropriate," making reasonable efforts to benchmark them. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

**Conditions, monitoring, disclosure, reporting**
15. **Conditions.** ECAs decide whether to request more information, decline, or support. Support may carry conditions, covenants and action plans. For all limited or non-recourse project finance Category A projects, ECAs should require regular ex post reports during their involvement. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
16. **Ex ante disclosure (Category A).** ECAs should disclose project information (name, location, description, where to obtain the ESIA). They should also require the ESIA or its summary to be made public, as early as possible and at least 30 calendar days before final commitment. Exceptions must be explained and reported. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
17. **Ex post disclosure and reporting.** At least annually, ECAs should publish E&S information on Category A and B projects they have committed to. They shall report Category A and B commitments to the ECG at least semi-annually, using the Annex III template. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
18. **Climate reporting.** ECAs shall report estimated annual GHG emissions for:
    - all fossil-fuel power projects;
    - other projects emitting over 25,000 tonnes CO2-equivalent a year, where data are available.

    For high-carbon fossil power projects above 700 g/kWh, they report specific actions taken to avoid, minimize or offset CO2. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
19. **Next review.** The ECG must report to Council on implementation, and on the need for amendment, no later than three years after a revision. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

## Financing and structure details

How the Common Approaches sit in a typical multi-sourced project financing:
- **ECA tranches.** Each participating ECA screens and classifies the project under the Common Approaches. ECAs exchange information to reach common positions on classification. One ECA, often the lead, may be relied on in reinsurance situations (paragraphs 15, 37).
- **Commercial banks.** Equator Principles banks apply the IFC Performance Standards and EHS Guidelines. Since 2024, ECAs benchmark against the same set by default, which allows a single ESIA, a single environmental and social action plan (ESAP), and a single independent E&S consultant.
- **Sovereign-backed deals.** ECAs may instead use the World Bank ESS, but Annex III asks them to explain the trigger criteria for that choice.
- **Timetable.** For Category A projects, the 30-day ex ante disclosure is a critical-path item before ECA final commitment.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The OECD's 2024 report to Council found implementation "generally uniform and consistent across Adherents." Variations remain, related to product range, export types and ECAs' experience. The report also judged that biodiversity, climate change and human rights provisions may need substantive revision (source 1).
- The Recommendation binds only Adherents. Its own text repeatedly calls for outreach to non-Adherents to promote a "global level playing field." That signals the core weakness, the same one discussed in the t-oecd-arrangement sheet: non-OECD official lenders are not bound (source 1).

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 29 (The lenders):**
  - Present ECAs as lenders with two OECD rulebooks: the Arrangement for money terms, and the Common Approaches for E&S terms (cross-refer t-oecd-arrangement).
  - Show how the SDR 10 million and sensitive-area triggers drive classification.
  - Explain the 30-day disclosure rule, and why the 2024 switch to IFC PS by default makes ECA covenants look like DFI and Equator Principles covenants.
- **Ch 50 (Environmental and social risk):**
  - Map the Common Approaches against the IFC PS, Equator Principles and World Bank ESF (cross-refer t-es-standards).
  - For Case P, walk through screening, Category A (thermal plant of 300 MW thermal or more, or a transmission line of 15 km or more at 110 kV), ESIA, ESAP conditions, ex post reports, and the GHG reporting duty for fossil power.
  - Teach the "exceptional case" reporting route as the safety valve, and why lenders avoid it.

## Do not state

- Do not describe the 2024 changes as a "major" overhaul. The OECD calls them minor revisions, and the substantive review was still under way. Do not claim a revised Recommendation was adopted in 2025 or 2026 unless the compendium shows one.
- Do not give the exact wording of the 2016 version's benchmarking paragraph. It was not read here, because the 2016 text could not be retrieved. Say only that before 2024 the text was built around the World Bank Safeguard Policies, with the IFC Performance Standards used for limited or non-recourse project finance, as the OECD's 2024 background note implies. That phrasing is an inference from the note.
- Do not list Adherent countries individually. The adherence table was not extracted.
- Do not claim the Common Approaches are legally binding, or that they set financial terms.
- Do not state OECD statistics on numbers of Category A projects reported. They were not retrieved.
