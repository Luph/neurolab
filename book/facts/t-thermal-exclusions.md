# Bank and DFI coal and gas financing exclusion policies

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: World Bank Group partnership with the IAEA on nuclear energy, 26 June 2025, and the board-endorsed energy approach referred to in that speech; UNEP FI net-zero banking guidance updated October 2025)

## Summary

Since the mid-2010s, public lenders have stopped financing coal power and upstream fossil fuels, and many have restricted gas. Three verified anchors:

- **World Bank Group.** On 12 December 2017, at the One Planet Summit in Paris, the World Bank Group (WBG) said it would no longer finance upstream oil and gas after 2019. It kept an exception for upstream gas in the poorest countries where energy access benefits are clear and the project fits the country's Paris commitments. The decision is usually called the "2019 upstream exit", but it was announced in 2017 and took effect after 2019. Midstream and downstream gas, including gas-fired power, stayed eligible.
- **European Investment Bank (EIB).** On 14 November 2019 the EIB board adopted an Energy Lending Policy. It ended new financing for unabated fossil-fuel energy projects, including gas, from the end of 2021, and replaced the bank's 550 gCO2/kWh Emissions Performance Standard (EPS) with 250 gCO2/kWh.
- **OECD Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits.** On 22 October 2021 the participants agreed to end export credit and tied-aid support for new unabated coal-fired power plants. Narrow exceptions remain for CCUS and for pollution or CO2 abatement at existing plants.

Commercial banks have published their own thermal coal phase-out policies, often tied to net-zero alliance commitments. These change often and were not verified here (see "Do not state").

From 2025 the direction became less one-way. The WBG reopened nuclear power: its first concrete step was the IAEA partnership in June 2025. Its president also described a board-advanced energy approach that covers gas. The UN-convened Net-Zero Banking Alliance (NZBA) material now appears as UNEP FI "net-zero banking" resources rather than alliance commitments. For project finance, the practical point is that a thermal project's lender universe is set by these policies: DFIs, ECAs and many international banks are excluded or constrained. That pushes sponsors toward domestic banks, non-OECD ECAs, Chinese policy banks or capital markets, and changes pricing, tenor and refinancing risk.

## Verified facts

1. **WBG upstream oil and gas.** At the One Planet Summit in Paris on 12 December 2017 the WBG announced it "will no longer finance upstream oil and gas, after 2019". Exception: in exceptional circumstances it would consider financing upstream gas in the poorest countries where there is a clear benefit for energy access for the poor and the project fits the country's Paris Agreement commitments. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The WBG Q&A of the same date gives the scope:
    - "Upstream" means exploration of oil and gas fields and drilling and operating wells.
    - Existing portfolio projects would continue.
    - The WBG would continue to support midstream and downstream gas, meaning transport and distribution to consumers and gas-fired power generation.

    [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. The same announcement said the WBG would apply a shadow carbon price in the economic analysis of IBRD/IDA projects in key high-emitting sectors where design began from July 2017. It said IFC began carbon pricing in key sectors in January 2017. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. **EIB Energy Lending Policy (EU).** The EIB board adopted the policy on 14 November 2019. Its terms:
    - No new consideration of financing for unabated fossil-fuel energy projects, including gas, from the end of 2021.
    - Gas infrastructure projects already formally under appraisal could still be approved until the end of 2021, as could any gas project on the 4th Projects of Common Interest (PCI) list co-financed with the EU budget.

    [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
5. The EIB policy also:
    - excludes support for upstream oil and natural gas production, coal mining, and infrastructure dedicated to coal, oil and natural gas (networks, LNG terminals, storage);
    - sets an Emissions Performance Standard of 250 gCO2/kWh for power generation, replacing 550 gCO2/kWh. For plants progressively blending low-carbon gases, the standard may be met on average over the plant's economic lifetime.

    The EIB says its 2013 EPS had already made it the first international financial institution to effectively end coal and lignite power lending. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
6. At the same time the EIB Group committed to:
    - align all financing activities with the Paris Agreement from the end of 2020;
    - aim to support EUR 1 trillion of climate action and environmental sustainability investment from 2021 to 2030.

    [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. **OECD Arrangement, coal (export credits).** On 22 October 2021 the Participants to the OECD Arrangement agreed to end export credit support for unabated coal-fired power plants. The participants include the US, the EU, Australia and Canada. Under the ban, exporters cannot get officially supported export credits or tied aid for:
    - new coal-fired power plants without operational CCUS;
    - equipment for existing coal plants, unless it is for pollution or CO2 abatement and does not extend the plant's life or capacity, or it retrofits CCUS.

    [confidence: high] [source: 5] Cross-reference: the facts/t-oecd-arrangement.md sheet records this as part of the Arrangement text (where the current rule is found).
8. **UK.** On 12 December 2020 the UK announced it would end export finance, aid funding and trade promotion for new crude oil, natural gas and thermal coal projects overseas, with very limited exceptions. The UK government states that it ended all financial support for overseas fossil fuel projects in March 2021. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
9. **WBG and nuclear, 2025.** On 26 June 2025 the WBG and the IAEA signed a partnership agreement on nuclear energy for development. The WBG described it as its "first concrete step to reengage with nuclear power in decades". In his remarks President Ajay Banga referred to an energy approach "our board advanced just two weeks ago" that clarifies the WBG's ability to support geothermal, gas, solar, hydro and wind. [confidence: high for the partnership and the remarks; the detailed policy text, including any change to the upstream-gas rule, was not reviewed] [source: 7, 8]
10. **Net-zero banking.** As of October 2026 the UNEP FI net-zero banking page presents guidance as "Net-Zero Banking Resources". These include "Guidance for Climate Target Setting for Banks" (October 2025) and sector briefs from 2025–2026. The page says the resources have supported almost 150 banks and more than 500 sectoral net-zero targets. [confidence: high for the page contents; the alliance's membership changes and institutional status were not verified here] [source: 9]

**Where the current rule is found:** WBG Energy and Extractives policy pages and Board papers; the EIB Energy Lending Policy and the EIB Climate Bank Roadmap; the OECD Arrangement text (TAD/PG series) and the UKEF climate pages; each commercial bank's published sector policy (usually titled "thermal coal", "energy" or "oil and gas" policy) and its annual climate report.

**Indicative market practice (not a rule):** Since about 2020, international project finance banks in Europe and Japan and OECD ECAs have generally not lent to new unabated coal power. Gas-fired power remains financeable from many commercial banks and some DFIs, but often with conditions: emissions intensity, hydrogen or CCS readiness, or a role in coal displacement. What moves this: home-country climate policy and regulation (for example EU taxonomy disclosure), shareholder and NGO pressure, ECA rules, energy security shocks (2022), and changes of government in shareholder countries (2025). [source: synthesis of 1–9; no quantitative range verified]

## Financing and structure details

- These are lender-side eligibility rules, not project terms. Their structuring effects:
    - **Lender pool.** DFI and ECA cover is excluded for coal, and for upstream or EIB-scope gas.
    - **Refinancing.** Exiting banks may not roll over loans, so tenor and refinancing risk rise.
    - **Covenants.** Use-of-proceeds and emissions covenants appear (for example EPS-style thresholds).
    - **Capex.** Sponsors design for compliance, such as hydrogen-ready turbines or CCS space, to keep access to finance.
- The EIB's 250 gCO2/kWh threshold sits well below typical unabated combined-cycle gas emissions. That is why the EIB says the level lets it focus on the most efficient combined heat and power projects. Typical CCGT emissions intensity was not verified from a primary source here. [4]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **EIB's rationale (EIB):** to concentrate limited resources on investments needed for the EU 2030 targets, while accepting that fossil fuels would continue to play a role up to 2030. [4]
- **WBG's rationale (WBG Q&A):** falling solar and wind costs widen low-cost options, and "commercial financing is often readily available" for oil and gas exploration and production. Gas still helps transition and access in some countries. [2]
- The 2025 WBG move back into nuclear shows that DFI exclusion lists can be reopened when shareholder priorities change. That is a reason for lenders and sponsors not to treat any eligibility rule as permanent. [7, 8]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 69 (Thermal power):** Treat "financeability" as a function of the lender universe, not only of the cash flows. Map which lender types (DFIs, OECD ECAs, international banks, domestic banks, non-OECD ECAs) can lend to a coal plant, an unabated CCGT, a hydrogen-ready CCGT and a CHP plant under the EIB 250 g test. Then discuss how a smaller and more domestic lender pool affects margin, tenor, currency and refinancing risk. Use the WBG 2017 wording to show how a policy can keep downstream gas eligible while excluding upstream.

## Do not state

- That the World Bank "announced" its upstream exit in 2019. It was announced on 12 December 2017 and applied after 2019.
- Any specific commercial bank coal exit dates or thresholds (for example for HSBC, BNP Paribas or JPMorgan). The primary policy documents could not be retrieved in this session and these policies are revised often.
- That the WBG has resumed upstream gas financing, or the detail of the June 2025 board energy approach beyond the president's description. The policy text was not reviewed.
- The current NZBA membership, any vote to wind down or restructure the alliance, or which banks left it in 2024–2025. Not verified here.
- The exact OECD effective date and the Participant list for the coal ban. The OECD site blocked automated access, and the UK release gives the agreement date, not the entry-into-force date.
- Any ADB, AIIB, AfDB or IFC coal or gas policy details. Not verified here.
