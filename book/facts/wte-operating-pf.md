# Dublin Waste-to-Energy (Poolbeg): a project-financed, operating EfW plant with public financing data

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered in verified sources: An Bord Pleanála permission to raise annual capacity to 690,000 tonnes, December 2021; Covanta ownership position in its 2020 Form 10-K, filed February 2021)

Cross-references: facts/wte.md (the English waste PFI programme and two EfW projects that failed before or during financing: Norfolk's Willows and Herefordshire and Worcestershire's EnviRecover). Read the two together: wte.md shows why EfW projects fail before financial close, and this sheet shows one that closed, was built and refinanced.

## Summary

The Dublin Waste-to-Energy facility on the Poolbeg peninsula is a two-line energy-from-waste (EfW) plant. It was licensed for 600,000 tonnes a year of residual municipal waste and has about 58 MW net (about 68 to 69 MW gross) electrical output. Covanta developed it under a 45-year project agreement with Dublin City Council, acting for the four Dublin local authorities, signed in September 2014. The plant reverts to the council at the end of the term. The approximately EUR 500 million construction cost was funded at financial close in September 2014 with:

- EUR 300 million of non-recourse project debt: a EUR 250 million senior term loan and a EUR 50 million second-lien term loan (about 60% leverage);
- a EUR 75 million convertible preferred investment from First Reserve's energy infrastructure fund;
- about EUR 125 million of Covanta equity.

Hitachi Zosen Inova supplied the electro-mechanical package, and the plant began commercial operations in October 2017. Two months later, in December 2017, Covanta refinanced the whole construction-era capital stack with EUR 446 million of longer-dated debt, much of it from institutional investors: a EUR 396 million senior loan and a EUR 50 million second-lien loan, both maturing 2032. Repaying the convertible preferred early cost a make-whole payment of USD 41 million. In February 2018 Macquarie's Green Investment Group bought 50% of the equity for EUR 136 million. The council's upside comes through revenue sharing: on waste revenue for the first 15 years and on energy revenue for the full term. Contemporary press reports describe contingent council revenue support if a gate-fee threshold is not reached in the first 15 years. The case is useful because almost every stage (construction financing, operational-phase refinancing, equity sell-down) is documented in SEC filings.

## Verified facts

1. On 19 September 2014 Covanta announced financial close on a project financing package for the Dublin Waste-to-Energy facility and the start of construction. The plant was designed to process 600,000 metric tonnes a year and generate 58 MW net of electricity. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. The total construction investment was approximately EUR 500 million, funded by:
   - EUR 300 million of project debt (about 60% leverage);
   - a EUR 75 million convertible preferred investment by the energy infrastructure arm of First Reserve;
   - approximately EUR 125 million of Covanta equity, of which more than EUR 30 million had already been spent on development.

   Macquarie Capital was exclusive financial adviser. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
3. Covanta's 2017 Form 10-K says the EUR 300 million of project debt was a EUR 250 million senior secured term loan ("Dublin Senior Term Loan due 2021") and a EUR 50 million second-lien term loan ("Dublin Junior Term Loan due 2022"). The short legal maturities (2021 and 2022) show the original debt was mini-perm construction financing, built to be refinanced. [confidence: high for tranches and maturities; the mini-perm label is this sheet's characterisation] [source: 3]
4. Covanta's announcement described a 45-year agreement with Dublin. Ownership of the facility reverts to Dublin at the end of the term. Dublin shares in waste-revenue variance for the first 15 years and in energy revenue for the full 45 years. More than 50% of the electricity was expected to qualify for inflation-escalated pricing under Ireland's renewable feed-in tariff (REFIT) through 2031. Covanta said it expected a mid-teens IRR on its equity and a purchase multiple of less than 9x Adjusted EBITDA. [confidence: high (as Covanta's stated expectations)] [source: 1]
5. Hitachi Zosen Inova (HZI) won the electro-mechanical contract, valued at over EUR 200 million: two lines of 102.5 MW thermal each, 68.8 MW gross electrical output. Covanta subsidiaries held the overall EPC and O&M roles on a fixed-price basis, with Covanta guaranteeing performance. [confidence: high for HZI scope; medium for the fixed-price wrapper wording] [source: 1, 2]
6. Planning approval dated from 2007, the EPA waste licence (W0232-01) from 2008, and the energy regulator's authorisations from 2009. Final approvals came in summer 2014. The concession therefore ran for about seven years from planning to financial close. [confidence: high for dates; medium for the 2009 authorisations] [source: 2, 8]
7. The Irish Times reported in June 2015 that a Luxembourg company, Dublin First WTE, had lent EUR 75 million to Dublin Waste to Energy (Holdings) at 13.5% a year, repayable in 2029. The same report described the council arrangements:
   - for the first 15 years, the councils receive a share of waste intake fees and energy sales above a threshold;
   - for the remaining 30 years, they share energy income;
   - contingent revenue support is available in the first 15 years if a gate-fee threshold is not achieved;
   - a community gain fund of approximately EUR 0.6 million a year.

   The EUR 75 million matches the First Reserve convertible preferred, so this is probably the same instrument as seen in company filings, but no primary source confirms that. [confidence: medium] [source: 4]
8. Commercial operations began in October 2017. Covanta's 2017 10-K lists the plant at 1,800 metric tonnes a day of design capacity and 58 MW. Covanta spent USD 117 million on construction in 2017. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
9. On 14 December 2017 Covanta signed agreements for EUR 446 million (USD 534 million) of project financing. This was a EUR 396 million senior secured loan to Dublin Waste to Energy Limited (the "Dublin Senior Loan") and a EUR 50 million second-lien term loan to Dublin Waste to Energy Group (Holdings) Limited (the "Dublin Junior Loan"). The proceeds repaid the 2014 senior and junior term loans and the convertible preferred. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
10. The 2017 refinancing produced these charges to loss on extinguishment of debt:
    - a USD 41 million "make whole" payment on the convertible preferred;
    - USD 19 million of third-party fees and a partial write-off of deferred financing costs;
    - USD 11 million of unamortised discount and costs.

    A further USD 13 million, relating to the portion of debt treated as a modification, continued to be amortised. Covanta's 10-K gives a total of approximately USD 71 million for the Dublin refinancing loss. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
11. Trade press reports that the senior debt carries a fixed rate of 3.1% and the junior debt 5.2%, both due 2032, with institutional investors taking about 65% of the senior debt. The remaining roughly EUR 139 million was floating-rate bank debt with a 15-year tenor. Eight lenders are named: AIB, Bank of Ireland, Hastings, Helaba, the National Strategic Infrastructure Fund (Ireland), Natixis, Rivage and Samsung Life Insurance. Covanta's 10-K separately confirms swaps converting EUR 135 million of the senior loan from floating to fixed, terminating between 2018 and 2032, which is consistent with the bank-tranche figure. [confidence: medium for pricing, split and lender list (trade press); high for the swap] [source: 3, 5]
12. On 18 December 2017 Covanta and Green Investment Group (GIG, part of Macquarie) announced a 50/50 joint venture to develop EfW projects in the UK and Ireland. As the first step, GIG acquired 50% of the Dublin project for EUR 136 million. The deal closed on 12 February 2018, and Covanta stayed on as O&M provider. In its 2020 10-K Covanta still reported a 50% indirect interest in Dublin through the JV Covanta Europe Assets Ltd, accounted for under the equity method. It listed design capacity at 1,968 tonnes a day and 68.0 MW gross. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 6, 7]
13. The Irish Times reported in July 2018 that the plant was running at full capacity, with 540,000 tonnes contracted. About 67% of its waste came from the Dublin local authorities, 15% from the Eastern-Midlands region and 18% from elsewhere in Ireland. Covanta was seeking a 15% (90,000-tonne) increase in annual capacity. Critics, including a former environment minister, argued the plant was too large for the region's residual waste once recycling targets were met. [confidence: high (as reported)] [source: 9]
14. An application was made to An Bord Pleanála (case 309812) to raise annual capacity from 600,000 to 690,000 tonnes. Secondary reporting states that permission was granted in December 2021. The EPA licence review application was lodged on 27 June 2019. [confidence: medium (dates from secondary summaries of the planning and EPA files)] [source: 8, 10]

## Financing and structure details

- Original (2014): approximately EUR 500 million total project cost.
  - Senior term loan of EUR 250 million (due 2021) and second-lien term loan of EUR 50 million (due 2022), giving about 60% debt.
  - EUR 75 million convertible preferred (First Reserve).
  - Approximately EUR 125 million Covanta common equity.
- Senior bank group at close: Bank of Ireland, AIB, Ulster Bank, Macquarie Bank, the National Pensions Reserve Fund and Barclays, as reported by trade press. [confidence: medium]
- Refinancing (December 2017): EUR 396 million senior (opco) and EUR 50 million second lien (holdco), both 2032. The senior was a mix of about 35% bank floating-rate debt, swapped to fixed, and about 65% institutional fixed-rate debt. The refinancing raised debt from EUR 300 million to EUR 446 million and repaid the expensive preferred, after a USD 41 million make-whole.
- Equity: Covanta 100% until February 2018, then 50% Covanta and 50% GIG through the JV.
- Revenue base: gate fees from councils and commercial collectors; electricity sales with REFIT support for more than half of output until 2031; council revenue sharing (waste for 15 years, energy for 45 years); reported contingent council support against a gate-fee shortfall in years 1 to 15.
- Handback: the plant reverts to Dublin City Council after 45 years.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Right: construction was delivered in about three years, in line with the September 2014 target of late 2017 (sources 1, 3). The construction-era mini-perm structure was refinanced within two months of commercial operations. The refinancing extended tenor to 2032, added EUR 146 million of debt and replaced the preferred equity, which carried a 13.5% coupon on the press account. Trade press describes the refinancing as extending maturities and reducing costs (source 5).
- Lesson in cost: the convertible preferred bridged an equity gap at close but cost a USD 41 million make-whole to retire. Sponsors who use such instruments should price the prepayment terms as part of the all-in cost (source 3).
- Recycling and waste-supply tension: the Irish Times account (source 9) shows the standing risk to EfW revenue. As recycling rises, local residual waste may fall below plant capacity, and the operator turns to regional waste and capacity increases.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 81 (social infrastructure, water and waste-to-energy): use Dublin as the worked example of an EfW revenue stack. It combines gate fees under council and merchant contracts, REFIT-supported power, council revenue sharing and reported contingent gate-fee support. Contrast it with the English PFI unitary-charge model in facts/wte.md, where the authority pays a service charge and takes waste-volume risk directly. Use the 2018 waste-supply debate to show why lenders test tonnage availability against recycling targets, and why put-or-pay or threshold mechanisms matter.
- Ch 81 also, for the financing trace: follow the capital stack from close (60% mini-perm bank debt plus preferred plus sponsor equity), to post-COD refinancing with institutions, to a 50% equity sell-down at a premium. It is a clean three-stage example of how risk falls and the cost of capital re-prices once construction risk is gone.

## Do not state

- Do not state the exact gate fee, the council contingent payment threshold or any payments made under it: no primary figures were found.
- Do not state that the Luxembourg EUR 75 million loan "is" the First Reserve preferred. It is likely but unconfirmed.
- Do not state that the European Investment Bank lent to the project: no evidence was found.
- Do not state current (2026) ownership beyond the 2020 10-K position. Covanta was taken private by EQT Infrastructure in 2021 (letsrecycle, 1 March 2022) and later rebranded, and whether GIG still holds its 50% of Dublin was not verified.
- Do not state the 2017 debt pricing as primary-sourced: the 3.1% and 5.2% figures come from trade press.
- Do not state the precise date of the 690,000-tonne planning permission or the EPA licence amendment outcome without checking the An Bord Pleanála and EPA files.
- Do not repeat the Irish Times "EUR 100 million from four local authorities" figure: its meaning is unclear.
