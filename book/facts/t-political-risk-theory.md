# Political risk theory: Vernon's obsolescing bargain and later literature; country risk ratings (sovereign ceiling, OECD country risk classification, transfer and convertibility assessment)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest data: OECD Country Risk Classifications valid as of 26 June 2026; the literature runs to 2021)

## Summary

The obsolescing bargain model comes from Raymond Vernon's *Sovereignty at Bay: The Multinational Spread of U.S. Enterprises* (1971). Before investing, a foreign investor has the bargaining power: capital, technology and market access, and the option to go elsewhere. Once the capital is sunk in an immobile asset such as a mine, pipeline or power plant, power shifts to the host government. The government can then revisit taxes, tariffs, royalties or ownership. The original bargain "obsolesces". The model was tested and refined later:
- Moran (1974), on copper in Chile;
- Kobrin (1987), on 563 subsidiaries of US manufacturers in 49 developing countries, found that the bargain obsolesces strongly in natural resources but much more slowly, if at all, in manufacturing with changing technology and global integration;
- Ramamurti (2001) and Eden, Lenway and Schuler (2005) widened it into multi-party political bargaining models;
- Collins et al. (2021) argued that short-cycle shale investment weakens the dynamic.

MIGA's 2013 empirical study of private infrastructure contracts supports the model. The risk of breach of contract rises with contract age, levels off between about the eighth and twelfth year, then rises again more slowly.

On the measurement side, lenders and export credit agencies use country risk ratings. The OECD Arrangement's country risk classification puts countries in categories 0 to 7, reviewed at least annually. In the 26 June 2026 list, for example, Peru, Mexico, India and Indonesia are in Category 3, Colombia, Brazil and South Africa in Category 4, Nigeria and Egypt in Category 6, and Argentina, Pakistan and Ukraine in Category 7. Credit rating agencies historically applied a "sovereign ceiling": a borrower's foreign-currency rating could not exceed its sovereign's. The logic was that the state can block conversion and transfer of hard currency. Since about 2001 the agencies have relaxed this into a more nuanced "ceiling lite", assessing transfer and convertibility (T&C) risk separately. That allows some issuers and structures to pierce the sovereign rating, for example through future-flow securitizations, offshore structures or political risk insurance.

## Verified facts

**The obsolescing bargain and the bargaining literature**

1. Raymond Vernon's book *Sovereignty at Bay: The Multinational Spread of U.S. Enterprises* was published in 1971. Crossref indexes reviews of it from 1971 and 1973. [confidence: high for title and date; publisher (Basic Books, New York) is widely cited but was not verified here] [source: 1]
2. Kobrin (1987) summarises the bargaining power model of host country and multinational interaction. It treats economic nationalism as rational self-interest, with both conflict and convergent objectives. In extractive industries, Kobrin reports strong evidence that outcomes depend on relative bargaining power and that "as power shifts to developing HCs [host countries] over time, the bargain obsolesces". [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. Kobrin's 1987 cross-national test used data on 563 subsidiaries of US manufacturing firms in 49 developing countries. It found that manufacturing is not characterised by the "inherent, structurally based, and secular obsolescence" found in natural resources. Bargaining power may shift to hosts when technology is mature and global integration limited. Where technologies change and global integration spreads, "the bargain will obsolesce very slowly and the relative power of MNCs may even increase over time". [confidence: high] [source: 2]
4. Theodore H. Moran's *Multinational Corporations and the Politics of Dependence: Copper in Chile* (1974) is a founding natural-resource case study in this literature. [confidence: high for bibliographic details; its findings were not separately verified here] [source: 3]
5. Later reformulations:
   - Stephen J. Kobrin, "Political Risk: A Review and Reconsideration", *Journal of International Business Studies* 10(1): 67–80 (1979);
   - Ravi Ramamurti, "The Obsolescing 'Bargaining Model'? MNC–Host Developing Country Relations Revisited", *JIBS* 32(1): 23–39 (2001);
   - Lorraine Eden, Stefanie Lenway and Douglas A. Schuler, "From the obsolescing bargain to the political bargaining model", in *International Business and Government Relations in the 21st Century* (Cambridge University Press, 2005), pp. 251–272;
   - Collins, Jones, Krane, Medlock and Monaldi, "Shale renders the 'obsolescing bargain' obsolete: Political risk and foreign investment in Argentina's Vaca Muerta", *Resources Policy* 74 (December 2021), article 102269. [confidence: high for bibliographic details; the arguments are summarised only as their titles indicate] [source: 4]

**Empirical evidence from MIGA (World Investment and Political Risk reports)**

6. MIGA's 2013 analysis of private projects with a contractual relationship with a developing-country public counterparty found several things:
   - breach risk is higher in middle-income than in low-income countries;
   - sector, private ownership share and the presence of international financial institutions matter at the project level;
   - economic downturns, dependence on primary commodities and the quality of political institutions matter at the macro level;
   - in MIGA's words, the results "support the idea of the 'obsolescing bargain'": breach risk rises with years of contract life, levels off between the eighth and twelfth year, then rises again, more slowly. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
7. MIGA's 2013 review of its own pre-claim events picked out tariff disputes in power projects as "a useful illustration of the obsolescing bargain principle". Volatile fuel prices and inflation push tariffs up, and the host comes under pressure to resist. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
8. MIGA's 2011 analysis found expropriation more likely after major political or economic shocks. Regime type mattered. In democracies, adverse measures tend to come from legislatures or sub-national bodies and are often settled once the executive engages. In non-democratic regimes, measures tend to come from the executive and are less often resolved. MIGA notes that most PRI expropriation contracts embed long waiting periods that give settlements time to mature. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

**OECD country risk classification (OECD Arrangement Participants; current list)**

9. The OECD publishes "Country Risk Classifications of the Participants to the Arrangement on Officially Supported Export Credits". The current list is "Valid as of: 26 June 2026". Categories run from 0 to 7. High-income OECD and high-income euro-area countries are "not reviewed or classified". The methodology (quantitative model plus qualitative assessment, at least annual review, use in minimum premium rates) is covered in t-oecd-arrangement.md items 20–21. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
10. Selected classifications as of 26 June 2026 (current; the previous classification differs only where noted):
    - **Category 2:** China, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, United Arab Emirates.
    - **Category 3:** India, Indonesia, Mexico, Oman, Peru, Philippines, Viet Nam (previously 4).
    - **Category 4:** Brazil, Colombia, Panama, South Africa.
    - **Category 5:** Türkiye.
    - **Category 6:** Egypt, Mongolia, Nigeria, Uganda.
    - **Category 7:** Argentina, Ghana, Kenya, Lao PDR, Mozambique, Pakistan, Russia, Ukraine, Venezuela.
    - **Not classified (high-income OECD):** Chile and Australia. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
11. Note (9) to the list says that, under Article 23 of the 2023 Arrangement, the risk of the sovereign is also assessed for every country classified under the methodology. The purpose is to identify, exceptionally, sovereigns that are not the lowest-risk obligor in the country and whose credit risk is significantly higher than country risk. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

**Sovereign ceiling and transfer and convertibility (T&C) risk**

12. World Bank researchers (Mohapatra, Nose and Ratha, Policy Research Working Paper 7618, March 2016) describe the sovereign rating as often acting as a "ceiling" for sub-sovereign ratings. They make four further points:
    - the historically high correlation between sovereign defaults and corporate default premia in emerging markets justified rating agencies' "sovereign ceiling" practice;
    - since 2001 the agencies have relaxed the policy (Moody's 2005; S&P 2013);
    - after the 2008–09 crisis, S&P redefined the maximum notch difference between a non-sovereign's rating and the sovereign's according to sector and region;
    - "corporate ratings that 'pierce' the sovereign ceiling" have become more common. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
13. The same paper finds that "three-quarters or more" of sub-sovereign foreign-currency bond ratings in 47 emerging and developing economies (1990–2013) were constrained by the sovereign ceiling. It treats local-currency debt as typically not subject to convertibility and transfer risks, so "the sovereign ceiling does not appear to apply to local currency bonds". [confidence: high] [source: 8]
14. Borensztein, Cowan and Valenzuela, "Sovereign ceilings 'lite'? The impact of sovereign ratings on corporate ratings", *Journal of Banking & Finance* 37(11): 4014–4024 (November 2013), with an earlier IMF Working Paper 07/75 (2007), frame the "sovereign ceiling lite" hypothesis. Under it, corporates can be rated above the sovereign, but sovereign ratings still weigh heavily on them. [confidence: high for citation; summary per source 8] [source: 4, 8]
15. MIGA's 2006 Capital Markets Brief states that a company's rating "may be constrained by the risk perception of the country", which caps the rating of its bond issue. MIGA can help local issuers "pierce the country's sovereign ceiling" by providing political risk insurance (transfer restriction and inconvertibility, expropriation, war and civil disturbance, breach of contract) to bondholders. It cites a USD 75 million guarantee issued in February 2006 for a Kazakh mortgage-backed securitization (First Kazakh Securitization Company B.V.). The brief adds that governments imposing transfer moratoria may agree to exclude MIGA-insured projects, "as has occurred in the past". [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

How the concepts show up in project finance terms:
- **Country risk category drives ECA pricing.** The OECD category feeds directly into the Minimum Premium Rate (t-oecd-arrangement.md).
- **The sovereign ceiling limits foreign-currency debt ratings.** Tools used to pierce it include:
  - offshore revenue accounts and future-flow structures, which the OECD Arrangement recognises as country-risk mitigation techniques (t-oecd-arrangement.md item 20);
  - political risk insurance (source 9; t-pri-market.md);
  - multilateral participation (t-dfis.md).
- **Countering the obsolescing bargain** with stabilization clauses, investment treaties and arbitration (t-arbitration-treaties.md), multilateral lenders and insurers, and local stakeholders.

## What went wrong or right, and why

The literature is analytical, not a single case. Well-established points, with attribution:
- The obsolescing bargain is strongest for large, immobile, single-purpose assets in natural resources and infrastructure. It is weaker where the investor keeps controlling technology, market access or integration (Kobrin 1987). [source: 2]
- Empirical breach risk rises with contract age (MIGA 2013), which matches the theory and argues for front-loaded debt amortisation and early-life protections. The amortisation inference is this sheet's own, not MIGA's. [source: 5]
- Sovereign ceilings are not absolute: agencies relaxed them after 2001. Local-currency debt largely escapes T&C risk but still carries sovereign economic risk. [source: 8]

Case cross-references in this fact base: chad-cameroon.md, dabhol.md, oyu-tolgoi.md and cobre-panama.md each discuss the obsolescing bargain; argentina-2002.md covers convertibility and pesification.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 59 (country, currency and payment risk).** Separate three things students blur: sovereign credit risk (will the state pay its own debt?), T&C risk (will the state let a solvent private borrower convert and transfer?) and country risk (the OECD's broader category). Use the 26 June 2026 OECD list to give students real categories. Use the sovereign ceiling and "ceiling lite" to explain why a strong offshore-revenue project in Category 6 or 7 may still be capped near the sovereign rating unless it uses offshore accounts or political risk insurance.
- **Chapter 60 (political risk and its protection).** Teach Vernon's model as the dynamic behind most host-government disputes in project finance: leverage shifts once capital is sunk. Then qualify it with Kobrin's finding on technology and integration, and with MIGA's evidence that breach risk rises with contract age. Ask students which mitigants restore investor leverage after financial close: multilateral participation, treaty protection, PRI, local partners, and continuing technology or market dependence. Link each to the cases listed above.

## Do not state

- Do not quote Vernon's text or claim that he coined the exact phrase "obsolescing bargain" in the 1971 book. The phrase is standard in the literature, but its first exact appearance (sometimes attributed to Vernon's later writing, around 1980) was not verified.
- Do not state the OECD Arrangement's legal definition of "country risk" (for example, that it covers transfer and conversion risk plus force majeure) as a quotation. The Arrangement text could not be retrieved; use t-oecd-arrangement.md.
- Do not state the current S&P, Moody's or Fitch criteria for ratings above the sovereign: maximum uplift notches, T&C assessment methods or country ceiling levels. Their criteria pages could not be read here, and the criteria change; cite the agencies' current published criteria.
- Do not give any country's current T&C assessment or country ceiling from memory.
- Do not summarise the findings of Moran (1974), Ramamurti (2001), Eden et al. (2005) or Collins et al. (2021) beyond what their titles indicate. Their texts were not read.
- OECD classifications change several times a year. Always cite the "valid as of" date.
