# Local-currency solutions: TCX, local-currency bonds and DFI local-currency lending

As of: 2026-10-03. Latest data points: TCX monthly report for August 2026; Moody's TCX credit opinion of 2 September 2026; S&P TCX report of 7 July 2026; IFC–Citi South African rand facility, 14 April 2026.

## Summary

Most cross-border lending to developing countries is in hard currency: TCX says more than 80% is in US dollars or euros. A project earning local-currency revenue therefore carries a currency mismatch that can make a sound project insolvent after a devaluation. The Currency Exchange Fund N.V. (TCX) was founded in 2007 by DFIs, microfinance investment vehicles and donors to take that risk in frontier currencies where no commercial hedge market exists. Its products are non-deliverable cross-currency swaps and FX forwards, settled in USD or EUR under ISDA/CSA. It usually faces the lender, so the lender can offer a local-currency loan while receiving hard currency. TCX cannot hedge most of what it takes on. It relies on diversification across more than 70 traded currencies (over 110 covered), a large capital base and selling risk on to private investors.

In 2025 TCX hedged a record USD 2.84 billion across 54 currencies and 572 transactions. It on-sold USD 1.76 billion (62%), earned USD 305 million and ended at a 27.6% capital ratio. It is rated Aa3 (Moody's) and A (S&P), both stable. Its capital totals about USD 2.06 billion at current value. The layers are:
- A-shares held by 19 DFIs and MIVs (EBRD, KfW and FMO the largest);
- subordinated convertible debt from European governments;
- first-loss capacity facilities from the European Commission, whose total support reaches EUR 561 million.

TCX's own report names infrastructure as a sector where it has struggled to grow: deals need large amounts and long tenors, and energy contracts are usually dollar-denominated. DFIs offer local-currency solutions directly too. IFC lends in more currencies than any other international financial institution. It syndicates local-currency loans through parallel loans and structured solutions such as overlay swaps and hedged A-loan participations, and it builds local funding lines, such as an ISDA with the State Bank of Pakistan (October 2025) and a ZAR 1.6 billion borrowing facility with Citi (April 2026). Local-currency subsidy is IFC's most concession-intensive blended product, at 10.9% of total project cost on average.

## Verified facts

### A. TCX: purpose, structure and products (Netherlands-domiciled AIF; current data at tcxfund.com, Reports page)

1. TCX was founded in **2007** by a group of DFIs, specialised microfinance investment vehicles (MIVs) and donors. It began operating in January 2008 (S&P). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
2. **Investors.** TCX's current investors are 19 multilateral and bilateral DFIs and MIVs, plus the Dutch, German, Swiss, British and French governments and the European Commission. Its own investors have accounted for over 90% of volumes to date; the remainder goes mainly through commercial banks. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. **Operating principles:**
   - additionality: provide solutions where markets are thin or non-existent;
   - risk-reflective pricing at prevailing market rates;
   - non-speculation: hedge only exposures from actual underlying obligations.
   
   TCX "acts as a price-maker in currencies and maturities not covered by commercial banks". It "cannot hedge the currency risk that it assumes and must bear and manage the open positions", relying on diversification backed by investor capital. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. **Products.** TCX offers non-deliverable cross-currency swaps (typical use: long-term and project finance) and FX forwards (short-term, trade and portfolio). All are settled in USD or EUR, available at floating rates and traded under ISDA/CSA. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
5. **Lender-hedge versus borrower-hedge.** TCX's main product is a non-deliverable cross-currency swap matched to the cash flows of a local-currency loan from one of its shareholders. The lender receives USD or EUR while the borrower owes local currency. TCX can also hedge the borrower directly, which allows flexible terms. That route requires TCX to onboard the local client, manage counterparty risk (through guarantees or other means) and clear local legal, regulatory and tax hurdles. "For these reasons, TCX generally prefers dealing directly with lenders." [confidence: high] [source: 3]
6. **Scale at a glance** (TCX two-pager, "figures reflective through 2025"):
   - total volume hedged: USD 19 billion or more;
   - capital base: USD 2 billion or more;
   - risk on-sold: USD 8.8 billion;
   - more than 110 currencies covered and more than 70 traded, historically across 79 countries.
   
   [confidence: high] [source: 2]

It was spread across 54 currencies in 572 transactions. 51% went to least-developed and lower-middle-income countries. [confidence: high] [source: 3]

The offsets included USD 434 million of local-currency bond issues by highly rated investors and others, across 12 currencies in 37 transactions (2024: USD 539 million). [confidence: high] [source: 3]
9. **Results.** 2025 profit was **USD 305 million**; the capital ratio was 27.6% of risk-weighted assets. Moody's (Aa3) and S&P (single-A) reconfirmed with stable outlooks in July 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
10. **Capital structure at 31 December 2025** (current value). Total capital was USD 2,060 million, made up of [confidence: high] [source: 3]:
    - **A-shares paid in: USD 1,431 million (69.5%).** The largest holders are EBRD, KfW and FMO (12.2% each), EIB (7.8%), IFC (7.7%) and AFD (5.2%).
    - **Subordinated convertible debt: USD 272 million (13.2%).** Lenders are the Netherlands, Germany (BMZ and BMUKN via KfW), UK FCDO, Swiss SECO and Proparco.
    - **First-loss loan: USD 10.9 million from the Netherlands.**
    - **EC capacity facilities (committed, undisbursed, tier 1): USD 170 million under EFSD (EUR 145 million, 100% activated) and USD 176 million under EFSD+ (EUR 245.75 million, 61% activated).**
11. **European Commission support** totals **EUR 561 million**: EFSD Capacity EUR 145 million, EFSD Pricing EUR 20 million, EFSD+ Capacity EUR 246 million and EFSD+ Pricing EUR 150 million. The EFSD+ Pricing Component Plus was executed in July 2025 (EUR 150 million of pricing discount for eligible transactions), and EUR 150 million of EFSD+ unfunded capital was activated in December 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
12. **AfDB.** In September 2025 the African Development Bank's board approved a USD 25 million equity investment in TCX. TCX notes it has a track record in 31 African countries. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
13. **2026 target and position.** TCX targets USD 3.0 billion of primary production in 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 3] At 31 August 2026, the local-currency portfolio was [confidence: high] [source: 4]:
    - long-LCY primary positions: USD 7,012 million;
    - LCY:USD hedging (short) positions: USD 3,477 million;
    - total LCY gross outstanding: USD 10,557 million;
    - net exposure: USD 3,466 million.
    
    Net realised profit for 2026 to date was USD 224.4 million.
14. **Ratings, 2026.** S&P (7 July 2026) cites a high likelihood of extraordinary support from 'AAA'-rated main shareholders, strong funding and liquidity and robust capitalisation, against very high frontier-currency market risk and volatile earnings. It estimated a buffer of USD 1.1 billion in May 2026 before TCX's 14% minimum capital ratio "before liquidation" would be triggered. Moody's affirmed **Aa3 stable** in a credit opinion of 2 September 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7]
15. **Infrastructure is hard for TCX.** TCX lists structural impediments. In energy, most commercial contracts are USD-denominated and FX risk is passed to end clients. In infrastructure, the amounts are large and the tenors long. As a market-risk vehicle, TCX "is not in a position to absorb a significant amount of the credit and country risks", so it depends on others to bear those risks as counterparties or guarantors. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
16. **Example (sovereign).** Jamaica's Ministry of Finance used a USD 71 million, 10-year hedge in Jamaican dollars via the IDB. TCX links it to a debt strategy that cut debt-to-GDP from 144% to 68% (2012–2024). That attribution is TCX's own claim. [confidence: medium] [source: 2]

### C. DFI local-currency lending and funding (current information at ifc.org)

17. **IFC local-currency syndication.** IFC states it now lends long-term "in more currencies than any other international financial institution". It mobilises partners into local-currency loans through [confidence: high] [source: 8]:
    - **parallel loans** from domestic banks or international financial institutions with local-currency capability;
    - **overlay swaps**, where a market counterparty swaps hard-currency proceeds into local currency for the borrower;
    - **hedged A-loan participations**, where IFC provides the whole local-currency loan while participants fund in hard currency.
    
    IFC is also exploring credit insurance on local-currency exposure to expand own-account lending.
18. **IFC blended local-currency subsidy.** The average concessional level for IFC blended local-currency support is 10.9% of total project cost, against 4.7% for all blended products (April 2026). [confidence: high] [source: 9]
19. **IFC–State Bank of Pakistan.** On 20 October 2025 IFC and the State Bank of Pakistan signed an ISDA agreement. The aim is to let IFC manage currency risk more effectively and increase its investments in Pakistani rupees. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
20. **IFC–Citi local funding facilities.** On 14 April 2026 IFC and Citi signed a **ZAR 1.6 billion borrowing facility** to expand IFC's rand lending in South Africa. It supported IFC's anchor investment in the Cape Water outcome-based bond issued by FirstRand Bank. It builds on a similar Kenyan shilling facility that IFC and Citi signed in 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
21. **Local-currency securitised bond (WAEMU).** In July 2025, NSIA Banque Bénin and BOAD's SPV Keur Samba issued a **XOF 52 billion (about USD 90 million) securitised local-currency bond** benefiting SMEs in Benin, Senegal and Togo. IFC, BOAD and British International Investment anchored 87% of the XOF 34 billion main tranche. IFC invested USD 25 million. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
22. **Local-currency project bond example.** Colombia's 4G Puerta de Hierro toll-road concession issued COP 760 billion of inflation-indexed (UVR) 144A/Reg S notes in May 2021. See colombia-4g.md. [source: cross-reference]

## Financing and structure details

How a TCX-hedged project loan works (from facts 4–5):
- The DFI lends local currency (LCY) to the project at a rate linked to local rates.
- The DFI enters a non-deliverable cross-currency swap with TCX. It pays TCX the LCY-indexed amounts, settled in USD, and receives USD-based amounts.
- The project's debt service is fixed in LCY. TCX holds the FX risk, which it diversifies or on-sells.
- The DFI keeps the credit risk on the borrower. TCX keeps only the counterparty risk on the DFI.
- The cost is the TCX swap price (local rate differential plus risk premium), which EU pricing facilities can subsidise.

The TCX example from the two-pager: an EU Pricing Facility cut hedging cost by 5%, making Acumen's USD 2 million investment in Yellow Malawi viable. [2]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Original sin.** TCX cites Eichengreen and Hausmann (1999): most countries cannot borrow abroad in their own currency. TCX exists because frontier currencies have no long-dated offshore hedge market. [source: 1]
- **Demand is pro-cyclical and short-termist.** TCX reports that borrowers often choose cheap hard-currency loans ("how much will it cost me now?") over the protection of local currency ("how much more could I lose later?"). That dampens its growth. [source: 3]
- **Capital and on-selling are the binding constraints.** As TCX increasingly warehouses and on-sells risk, periods of investor aversion to frontier currencies reduce its ability to offset positions and consume capital. This is why it retains earnings instead of paying dividends, and why EC first-loss facilities matter. [source: 3]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 34 (Blended, concessional and local-currency finance):**
  - Present TCX as a blended structure in its own right: DFI equity, donor subordinated debt and EC first-loss capital support a fund that prices frontier-currency risk at market rates, then on-sells about 60% of it to private investors.
  - Walk students through the lender-hedge swap. Then show the cost lever: pricing facilities, and IFC's 10.9% average concessionality for local-currency support.
  - Use the IFC–Citi rand line and the WAEMU securitisation to show local funding, not just hedging.
- **Ch 59 (Country, currency and payment risk):**
  - Teach currency mismatch with a devaluation example. Then show the solution menu: TCX swaps, DFI local-currency loans (parallel loans, overlay swaps, hedged A-loans), local-currency and indexed bonds such as the Colombian UVR notes, and tariff indexation in the PPA.
  - Stress TCX's own admission that infrastructure and energy are hard to serve: large tenors, and dollar PPAs that push FX risk to offtakers.
  - That is why Case P's currency shock lands on the offtaker's ability to pay, linking currency risk to payment risk.
