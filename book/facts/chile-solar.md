# Chile northern solar: curtailment, transmission, cannibalization

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Chilean curtailment data for January to July 2026, published September 2026, and Kimal–Lo Aguirre construction progress reported 9 September 2026)

## Summary

Northern Chile, around the Atacama Desert (the Antofagasta, Atacama and Tarapacá regions), has some of the best solar resources in the world. The main demand centre, Santiago, is more than 1,000 km to the south. From about 2014, solar capacity grew faster than the grid could carry it. Until November 2017 Chile ran two separate grids, the northern SING and the central SIC. Spot prices fell to zero at some nodes on many days: 192 days in 2015 and 113 days in January to April 2016, according to Bloomberg reporting of grid-operator data. The 2017 Mejillones–Cardones 500 kV line joined the two systems, but solar and wind kept outgrowing transmission. Curtailment of solar and wind reached about 5.6–5.9 TWh in 2024, roughly a fifth of their output, and 6,084 GWh in 2025. Solar-hour marginal costs across large parts of the system sit at or near zero for much of the year. The worst damage came from the gap between prices at the node where a generator injects power and the node where its customers withdraw it (called "desacople", or decoupling, in Chile). Generators that had won regulated supply contracts in the 2015–2016 auctions were paid nothing for power injected in the north. At the same time they had to buy power at high marginal costs to supply their contracts in the centre, and costs rose further when fossil fuel prices spiked in 2022. María Elena Solar (Solarpack) became insolvent in September 2022 and was put into liquidation in April 2023. The wind project Cabo Leones II also became insolvent, in October 2022. Policy responses include a storage law (Law 21.505, 2022), an energy transition law aimed at transmission (Law 21.721, 2024) and the 3,000 MW Kimal–Lo Aguirre HVDC line, now under construction and due in 2029. Batteries are now absorbing much of the daytime surplus.

## Verified facts

1. Until November 2017 Chile's two main grids, the northern SING (Sistema Interconectado del Norte Grande) and the central SIC (Sistema Interconectado Central), were not connected. Surplus power in one could not reach the other. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
2. In June 2016 Bloomberg reported grid-operator data showing that spot prices reached zero in parts of Chile on 113 days through April 2016, against a total of 192 days in 2015. The same report said solar capacity on the central grid had quadrupled since 2013. [confidence: medium (secondary report of operator data; the nodes are not specified)] [source: 1, 2]
3. On 22 November 2017, Transmisora Eléctrica del Norte (TEN) commissioned the 500 kV Mejillones–Cardones line, which connected the SING and SIC for the first time. TEN is owned 50/50 by Red Eléctrica Internacional and Engie. The line is about 600 km long, has 1,350 towers and four substations, and cost approximately USD 800 million (EUR 679 million). [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. In the August 2016 regulated supply auction for distribution companies, Solarpack bid USD 29.10/MWh for a solar project of about 120 MW. This was then reported as a world-record low. The contract was for 280 GWh a year, delivered in daytime hours. The auction's average price was USD 47.6/MWh. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
5. In March 2019 KfW IPEX-Bank provided USD 91 million of debt for that Solarpack project. pv magazine described it as 123 MW, costing USD 104 million, selling to the distribution companies from 2021 to 2040. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
6. The project company, María Elena Solar S.A., a Solarpack subsidiary, operates a plant of about 104–105 MW (ac) at Pozo Almonte in the Tarapacá Region. Its regulated supply contract was valid until 2041. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
7. On 29 September 2022 María Elena Solar told the Coordinador Eléctrico Nacional (CEN), Chile's system operator, that it could not pay its balances in the short-term market and would stop supplying its regulated contract. It blamed decoupling: power it injected in the north was valued at zero marginal cost, while the withdrawals needed to serve its contract were priced at high marginal costs. Its letter said that, according to the Coordinador's own information, 25 generating companies had pending debts from balances. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
8. On 5 April 2023 the 23rd Civil Court of Santiago declared María Elena Solar in liquidation. The main creditor was KfW IPEX-Bank, holding promissory notes totalling USD 91 million. The court ordered the plant to keep operating. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
9. On 6 October 2022 Ibereólica Cabo Leones II, owner of a wind farm of 49 turbines of 5 MW each at Freirina in the Atacama Region, told the Coordinador it was insolvent. It became the second renewable generator to do so. It had been financed with a syndicated loan of USD 217 million. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
10. An Energy Policy paper of 2024 (Turmoil in the Chilean electricity sector) treats these regulated supply contracts as "advanced contracts for differences" in which the generator carries the locational basis risk between injection and withdrawal nodes. It says the decoupling was made worse by the jump in fossil fuel prices after Russia invaded Ukraine. [confidence: medium (read via abstract and summaries; full text not accessed)] [source: 10]
11. ACERA (the Chilean renewable energy and storage association), citing CEN data, reported 5,909 GWh of solar and wind curtailment in 2024, up 121% on 2023. Ember gives 5,642 GWh, equal to 19% of solar and wind supply and 6.6% of all electricity generated in Chile. The difference reflects method. Both show 2024 at roughly 5.6–5.9 TWh. [confidence: high] [source: 11, 12]
12. ACERA, as reported by PV Tech, said Chile added 2.4 GW of solar PV in 2024, for a total of 11.7 GW. It also said the extra solar PV curtailment in 2024 (about 2.7 TWh) exceeded the solar generation added. [confidence: medium (association data via trade press)] [source: 11]
13. Ember estimates that about 11,900 GWh of renewable electricity was curtailed between January 2022 and May 2025, at a cost of about USD 562 million to generators since 2022. Ember also estimates that 1 GW of new battery storage would cut curtailment by almost a quarter. [confidence: medium (analyst estimate)] [source: 12]
14. Curtailment in 2025 was 6,084 GWh, up 7.8% on 2024, according to ACERA. For January to July 2026 it was 2,680 GWh, the highest January–July total on record and 1.6% above the same period of 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 13]
15. At the end of July 2026 Chile had 2,655 MW / 11,250 MWh of battery storage in operation, according to ACERA. [confidence: medium] [source: 13]
16. Law 21.505, which promotes electricity storage and electromobility, was published on 21 November 2022. It lets stand-alone storage take part in the system's energy balances and be paid for energy and capacity. [confidence: high] [source: 14]
17. Law 21.721, the Energy Transition Law, was published in the Diario Oficial on 27 December 2024. It amends the General Electricity Services Law on transmission. Among other things it adds a mechanism for urgent works and lets generators propose and finance transmission expansion works. [confidence: high] [source: 15]
18. On 6 December 2021 a consortium of Transelec, ISA Inversiones Chile and China Southern Power Grid International won the Kimal–Lo Aguirre project. It is a bipolar ±600 kV HVDC line of 3,000 MW from Kimal (Antofagasta Region) to Lo Aguirre (Metropolitan Region), Chile's first direct-current line. The annual remuneration bid was USD 116.3 million (2021 dollars), and commissioning was then expected by end-2028. [confidence: high] [source: 16]
19. As of 9 September 2026, the project company, Conexión Kimal Lo Aguirre, reports:
    - a line of 1,346 km and 2,694 towers;
    - capex of approximately USD 1.5 billion;
    - construction 20.35% complete;
    - USD 422 million disbursed (28.51% of capex);
    - commissioning expected in 2029;
    - sectoral permits named as a main remaining challenge.

    [confidence: high] [source: 17]

## Timeline

- 2013–2016: rapid solar build-out, first in the north. Zero spot prices recorded on 192 days in 2015 and 113 days in January–April 2016 (fact 2).
- August 2016: distribution-company auction; Solarpack bids USD 29.10/MWh; average USD 47.6/MWh.
- 22 November 2017: Mejillones–Cardones 500 kV line joins SING and SIC into the National Electric System (SEN).
- 6 December 2021: Kimal–Lo Aguirre HVDC awarded.
- 2021–2022: decoupling between northern injection prices and central withdrawal prices; fossil price spike in 2022.
- 29–30 September 2022: María Elena Solar declares it cannot pay its balances.
- 6 October 2022: Cabo Leones II declares insolvency.
- 21 November 2022: Law 21.505 (storage) published.
- 5 April 2023: María Elena Solar declared in liquidation.
- 2024: curtailment about 5.6–5.9 TWh. 27 December 2024: Law 21.721 published.
- 2025: curtailment 6,084 GWh.
- August 2026: Kimal–Lo Aguirre construction milestone. September 2026: 20% complete.

## Financing and structure details

- María Elena Solar: KfW IPEX-Bank senior debt of USD 91 million, documented as nine promissory notes repayable in 77 instalments. Project cost was USD 104 million (pv magazine). Revenue came from a regulated distribution-company supply contract running to 2040/2041, a contract for a fixed volume during set daytime hours. [source: 6, 8]
- Cabo Leones II: syndicated loan of USD 217 million. Owners were Ibereólica Renovables, with Grupo Naturgy reported as a partner. [source: 9]
- Kimal–Lo Aguirre: regulated transmission concession paid through an annual value set by the bid (USD 116.3 million a year, 2021 dollars). Shareholders are Transelec, ISA Inversiones Chile and CSG. The equity split is reported as equal thirds but was not confirmed in a primary source. Capex is about USD 1.5 billion. [source: 16, 17]
- Mejillones–Cardones (TEN): Engie and Red Eléctrica Internacional 50/50; about USD 800 million. [source: 3]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Generation outran transmission. ACERA and Ember both attribute curtailment mainly to transmission congestion between northern resources and central demand, plus too little flexibility. Ember adds that daytime supply exceeds demand. [source: 11, 12, 13]
- Contract design put basis risk on the generator. Regulated supply contracts are settled at the withdrawal side while the generator is paid at its injection node. When the two decouple, the generator pays the spread. María Elena Solar said this was the cause of its default, and the Energy Policy paper treats it as the structural flaw. [source: 7, 10]
- The fixed daytime-block contract helped and then hurt. It matched solar output, but in a market where daytime prices collapse it protects nothing against nodal separation. It also leaves the generator exposed when its own output is curtailed but contract volumes must still be served.
- Policy has partly responded: the storage law, the transmission law and Kimal–Lo Aguirre. ACERA reports that batteries absorbed a significant part of the 2025 surplus. Even so, curtailment set a new January–July record in 2026. [source: 13, 14, 15]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 11 (power assets and markets): Use Chile as the cleanest real example of merit-order collapse in solar hours, nodal versus system prices, curtailment and capture-price cannibalization. Show how a second grid connection (2017) moved prices without ending congestion. Have readers reason from published curtailment shares (about 19% in 2024) to the effect on a solar plant's realized revenue.
- Ch 20 (merchant revenue and hedges): The María Elena Solar default shows that a "fixed-price" contract is only as good as the node it settles at. Teach shape risk (a daytime block versus as-produced output), basis risk (injection versus withdrawal node) and volume risk (curtailment) as separate exposures. Show how a record-low bid with locational basis left unhedged became a liquidation within about three years of operation.
- Ch 70 (onshore wind and solar): Lessons for diligence: model nodal prices and curtailment rather than system averages; stress basis spreads; check transmission plans and how firm their commissioning dates are (Kimal–Lo Aguirre slipped from end-2028 to 2029). Treat co-located batteries as a revenue-protection tool, not an add-on. The Chilean pipeline shift toward hybrid solar-plus-storage, and lenders' growing focus on curtailment, follow directly.

## Do not state

- The specific nodes (for example Crucero) and the hourly counts behind the 2015–2016 "zero-price days". Bloomberg's original article could not be accessed directly.
- That the Coordinador "projected USD 0/MWh in solar hours for 8 of 12 months in 2024–2025". This appears in trade-press summaries that could not be checked against the CEN report.
- Exact peso amounts of María Elena Solar's arrears or guarantees. The figures reported ("$2,788 million", "$2,507 million") are Chilean pesos and easy to misread as US dollars.
- Any equity percentages for Kimal–Lo Aguirre, or the precise terms of the 2023 USD 480 million financing (reported as HSBC plus USD 160 million each from Transelec and ISA). Not verified in a primary document.
- Regional shares of solar capacity by region (for example "Antofagasta 35%, Atacama 26%"). The context of these trade-press figures is ambiguous.
- That Solarpack's bid price was the global record "ever". It was a record when made in August 2016 and was later beaten.
- That Cabo Leones II is a solar or northern project. It is a wind farm in the Atacama Region, hit by the same decoupling.
