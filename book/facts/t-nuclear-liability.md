# Nuclear third-party liability regimes (Paris/Brussels, Vienna, CSC, US Price-Anderson)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest rule change covered: 2004 Paris and Brussels Protocols in force 1 January 2022; NRC nuclear insurance backgrounder reviewed September 2026; Price-Anderson extended to 31 December 2065)

## Summary

Nuclear third-party liability (NTPL) law departs from ordinary tort rules in a way that matters directly to project finance. Under the international conventions and most national laws:

- The **operator** of the nuclear installation is **exclusively and strictly liable** for nuclear damage to third parties. Suppliers, EPC contractors and lenders are channelled out.
- Liability is **limited in amount and time**, and the operator must hold **insurance or other financial security** up to the limit.
- **State funds** sit above the operator tier.
- **Jurisdiction** is concentrated in one country's courts.

There are three families:

- **Paris Convention (OECD, 1960) with the Brussels Supplementary Convention (1963).** Both were amended by 2004 Protocols in force from 1 January 2022. The operator is liable for at least EUR 700 million, the installation state tops up to EUR 1.2 billion, and Brussels parties collectively add EUR 300 million, for EUR 1.5 billion in total.
- **Vienna Convention (IAEA, 1963).** Its 1997 Protocol entered into force on 4 October 2003, with an operator minimum of not less than 300 million SDR (special drawing rights, the IMF unit of account).
- **Convention on Supplementary Compensation (CSC, 1997).** It entered into force on 15 April 2015 and adds an international fund on top of a national first tier.

The United States sits outside Paris and Vienna. Its own **Price-Anderson Act** (1957) channels liability, though the statutory mechanism differs from the conventions. It has a two-tier pool:

- USD 500 million of private primary insurance per site;
- a retrospective assessment of up to approximately USD 158 million per reactor.

Together these give more than USD 16 billion. The Act has been extended to 31 December 2065. The US is also a CSC party. For lenders and EPC contractors, the key questions on any new-build are:

- whether the host country's regime channels liability to the operator, so that suppliers and lenders are protected;
- whether it is linked by treaty to neighbouring states, so that cross-border claims are channelled;
- whether the operator's limit can be insured in the nuclear pools.

## Verified facts

**Paris/Brussels (OECD; current rule as amended by the 2004 Protocols)**

1. The Paris Convention imposes on the operator:
    - exclusive liability, limited in amount, time and types of compensable damage;
    - a duty to cover that liability by insurance or other financial security;
    - a specified geographical scope;
    - jurisdiction rules ensuring the courts of one Paris country handle all claims from an incident, with recognition of judgments in other Paris countries.

    The Brussels Supplementary Convention adds public funds where Paris compensation is insufficient. Its geographical scope is narrower: it does not extend to damage in non-party countries. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The 2004 Protocols:
    - added new heads of damage: economic loss from personal injury or property damage, the cost of reinstating an impaired environment, loss of income from a direct economic interest in the environment, and the cost of preventive measures;
    - raised the minimum operator liability and the public funds;
    - extended geographical scope;
    - extended the limitation period for personal injury from 10 to 30 years, while other claims stay at 10 years;
    - brought disposal installations into scope;
    - allowed states to impose higher or unlimited liability.

    [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Under the amended regime, total funds rise from approximately EUR 300 million to EUR 1,500 million. The Brussels three-tier allocation is:
    - **Tier 1:** the operator, at a minimum of EUR 700 million;
    - **Tier 2:** the operator or installation-state public funds, from the operator's limit up to EUR 1,200 million;
    - **Tier 3:** a collective contribution from all Brussels parties of EUR 300 million.

    [confidence: high] [source: 1; consistent with source 5]
4. The 2004 Protocols entered into force on 1 January 2022, when the UK's changes to the Nuclear Installations Act 1965 also took effect. The UK implemented them through the Nuclear Installations (Liability for Damage) Order 2016. The UK put the first two tiers on operators of standard sites:
    - **Power plants and other standard sites:** EUR 700 million, rising by EUR 100 million a year to EUR 1.2 billion. Before ratification the limit was GBP 140 million.
    - **Intermediate sites:** EUR 160 million.
    - **Low-risk sites:** EUR 70 million.
    - **Low-risk transport:** EUR 80 million.

    [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
5. The UK Government Actuary's Department (GAD) reported in 2022 that the commercial insurance market could cover most of the extended cover. It could not cover all of the increased personal-injury liability for the 10-to-30-year period, so the UK set up temporary government risk-sharing measures. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
6. Per the World Nuclear Association (WNA), the 2004 limits of EUR 700 million operator, EUR 500 million installation state and EUR 300 million collective now apply across all 16 Paris Convention states. Some states set operator liability higher or unlimited. Examples:
    - **Germany:** unlimited liability, with EUR 2.5 billion of security per plant.
    - **Finland:** unlimited liability beyond the Brussels EUR 1.5 billion.
    - **Sweden:** operator insurance of at least SDR 1,000 million.

    [confidence: medium (industry association secondary source)] [source: 5]

**Vienna and CSC (IAEA)**

7. The Vienna Convention on Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage was concluded on 21 May 1963 and entered into force on 12 November 1977. Its 1997 amending Protocol entered into force on 4 October 2003. Per WNA:
    - The 1963 Convention set an operator minimum of not less than USD 5 million.
    - The 1997 Protocol requires operator liability of not less than 300 million SDR.
    - The Protocol broadens "nuclear damage" to include environmental damage and preventive measures, extends geographical scope and the period for personal-injury claims, and gives coastal states jurisdiction over transport incidents.

    [confidence: medium for the amounts (secondary); high for dates] [source: 5, 6]
8. The CSC was adopted on 12 September 1997. The US signed it then and deposited its ratification in May 2008. It entered into force on 15 April 2015. [confidence: high] [source: 4] The CSC can be joined by Paris parties, Vienna parties, or states whose national law conforms to the CSC Annex. Its structure:
    - a first tier, supplied by the installation state, of at least 300 million SDR;
    - a second tier of international contributions based largely on installed nuclear capacity.

    Per WNA, the CSC needed five states with at least 400,000 MW(t) of installed capacity to enter into force. Japan's ratification triggered entry into force, and India joined in 2016. [confidence: medium] [source: 5]
9. Per the NRC, the CSC provides additional coverage for nuclear incidents in the US, currently up to approximately USD 57 million, for damages above the CSC first-tier amount. [confidence: high] [source: 4]

**United States (Price-Anderson)**

10. The Price-Anderson Act became law on 2 September 1957. It caps the total liability each licensee faces for a commercial power plant accident and creates a pooled insurance system. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
11. NRC figures (backgrounder updated July 2025, reviewed September 2026):
    - **Primary tier:** each reactor site carries USD 500 million of private offsite liability insurance (per site, not per reactor).
    - **Secondary tier:** for damage above that, each licensee is assessed a prorated share of up to approximately USD 158 million per reactor. With 95 reactors in the pool, this tier is about USD 15 billion.
    - **Total pool:** more than USD 16 billion.
    - **Court supervision:** payouts above 15 percent of the funds need a court-approved prioritisation plan.
    - **Surcharge:** if damage may exceed both tiers, a further assessment of up to 5 percent of the maximum deferred premium (approximately USD 7.9 million per reactor) may apply.
    - **Beyond that:** Congress is committed to decide whether further disaster relief is needed.

    [confidence: high] [source: 4]
12. NRC on the insurance market:
    - American Nuclear Insurers is the only pool writing US nuclear liability insurance.
    - The average 2025 premium is approximately USD 1.1 million for a single-unit site and approximately USD 1.5 million on average per site.
    - Ordinary US property and liability policies exclude nuclear accidents.

    [confidence: high] [source: 4]
13. The Further Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2024 extended the Price-Anderson Act to 31 December 2065. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
14. Separately from Price-Anderson, 10 CFR 50.54(w) requires at least USD 1.06 billion of onsite property insurance per reactor site, to stabilise and decontaminate after an accident. NRC says only Nuclear Electric Insurance Limited (NEIL) currently provides it. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
15. NRC records that at Three Mile Island (1979) private insurers had USD 140 million of first-tier cover. The pools paid approximately USD 71 million in claims and litigation costs, and the last litigation ended in 2003. [confidence: high] [source: 4]

**Other national examples (WNA; secondary)**

16. **Japan.** Operators must hold JPY 120 billion of financial security. Liability is unlimited, and the state may relieve the operator in a grave natural disaster of an exceptional character. [confidence: medium] [source: 5]
17. **India.** The 2010 Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act gave operators a statutory right of recourse against suppliers, which was contrary to the conventions. WNA reports that in December 2025 the Act was repealed and replaced by the SHANTI Act, which removes statutory supplier recourse. [confidence: medium (single secondary source; verify against the Indian Gazette before relying on it)] [source: 5]
18. **China** is not party to any international liability convention (WNA). [confidence: medium] [source: 5]

**Where the current rule is found:**
- OECD Nuclear Energy Agency, Paris and Brussels Convention texts and status tables;
- IAEA treaty status pages for Vienna, the 1997 Protocol, the CSC and the 1988 Joint Protocol;
- national statutes, for example the UK Nuclear Installations Act 1965 as amended and the US Atomic Energy Act section 170 (Price-Anderson);
- the NRC "Backgrounder on Nuclear Insurance and Disaster Relief" for current US pool figures, which are inflation-adjusted every five years.

## Financing and structure details

- **Channelling** is what makes nuclear EPC and supply contracts insurable and financeable. Suppliers and lenders are not liable to third parties, so their exposure is contractual, not tortious. Where a host-state regime allows recourse against suppliers, as under India's 2010 Act, supplier participation and ECA support become harder.
- **The operator's limit sets the insurance requirement in loan documents.** Lenders require evidence of NTPL cover from a nuclear pool (or state security) up to the statutory amount. They also require separate onsite property and decontamination cover, which in the US means NEIL cover under 10 CFR 50.54(w).
- **Timing gaps.** A step-up in limits (UK: EUR 100 million a year) and capacity gaps (UK: 10–30-year personal-injury tail) create interim periods where the state backs part of the cover. Diligence should identify these.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **UK (GAD):** the 2004 Protocols enlarged heads of damage and limitation periods faster than commercial capacity could absorb. The state therefore took a temporary risk-sharing role, with GAD supporting a long-term market solution. [3]
- **Fragmentation (WNA analysis):** Fukushima (2011) highlighted that many reactors sit outside Paris and Vienna. A 2013 US–France joint statement sought to bridge their preferred regimes (CSC and revised Paris/Vienna) and encourage wider adherence. [5]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 74 (Nuclear power):** Teach liability channelling as a precondition for financing, then walk through the tiers: Paris/Brussels EUR 700 million, then EUR 1.2 billion, then EUR 1.5 billion; Price-Anderson USD 500 million plus approximately USD 158 million per reactor. Have the reader check, for a hypothetical new-build in a non-party state, who would be sued, where, and up to what amount. Then show what an EPC contractor, an ECA and a lender would require: convention accession or conforming law, operator insurance, and a state indemnity above the cap.

## Do not state

- The exact current SDR amounts or entry-into-force dates for individual states' Vienna, CSC or Joint Protocol accessions. The IAEA and NEA status pages blocked automated access, and the WNA figures are secondary.
- India's SHANTI Act details (date, caps, tiering) as settled law without checking the official Gazette text. These come from WNA only.
- Germany's EUR 2.5 billion security, Japan's JPY 120 billion figure, and the WNA statement that the 2004 limits apply across "all 16 Paris states" as primary-verified. These are secondary.
- That the US is a party to the Paris or Vienna conventions. It is not; it is a CSC party.
- The exact current Price-Anderson secondary-tier amount after the next five-yearly inflation adjustment. Use the NRC backgrounder figure with its date.
