# Project finance markets in the 2008–2009 global financial crisis: bank retrenchment, monoline collapse, volumes, recovery and the Basel III response

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: the EIB Project Bond Credit Enhancement guide of 21 December 2012, plus the Basel III standards of December 2010/June 2011 and their current consolidated text)

## Summary

The crisis hit project finance through two channels.

**Bonds went first.** Before 2008, many UK PFI deals and US public-private partnerships (PPPs) were funded with long-dated bonds wrapped by AAA-rated monoline insurers such as Ambac and MBIA. These carried investment-grade ratings from financial close. Mortgage-related losses destroyed the monolines' ratings during 2008:
- S&P cut both Ambac and MBIA from AAA to AA on 5 June 2008;
- on 19 June 2008 Moody's cut Ambac to Aa3 and MBIA to A2;
- by November 2008 Ambac was Baa1 (Moody's) and A (S&P);
- Ambac said it could write only a "de minimis" amount of new business.

The National Audit Office (NAO) records the last UK PFI bond as the GBP 146 million Northern Ireland Roads II issue in December 2007, after which the collapse of confidence in credit insurers left UK PFI projects without access to capital markets. EIB economists found that bond finance for new European projects "dried up almost completely". Engel, Fischer and Galetovic, writing in EIB Papers, also judged the monoline wrap model "unlikely to return in the foreseeable future".

**Bank lending then contracted.** After Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, loan syndication stopped through 2009. In the UK, banks would lend only about GBP 25 million each per PFI deal, so sponsors had to assemble "clubs" of five or six banks. Margins on standard PFI deals rose from about 0.79% (2007) to 2.51% (2009). The NAO estimated that the higher financing costs added 6–7% to typical PFI unitary charges. Between October 2008 and March 2009 only four smaller PFI projects reached close. Governments stepped in:
- HM Treasury created The Infrastructure Finance Unit (TIFU), whose only loan was GBP 120 million to the Greater Manchester Waste PFI (financial close 8 April 2009);
- the European Investment Bank (EIB) was asked to lend more;
- France guaranteed 80% of PPP debt once projects were operating.

The NAO's view was that only TIFU's one loan was needed to restart the UK market, but that higher costs had been locked in.

**Volumes.** European PPP value fell by almost 40% in 2008 from the 2007 peak and by about a further 20% in 2009, to EUR 15.8 billion (almost 50% below 2007), before recovering in 2010. In developing countries, PPI investment commitments fell 45% in the second half of 2008 compared with a year earlier, then rose 15% in 2009 to USD 85.4 billion. That recovery was concentrated in energy projects in Brazil, China, India and Turkey.

**Regulation.** The Basel Committee's response (Basel III, December 2010, revised June 2011, with liquidity standards following) raised the quantity and quality of bank capital. It added a leverage ratio and introduced the liquidity coverage ratio (LCR) and net stable funding ratio (NSFR). The NSFR assigns an 85% required-stable-funding factor to most performing loans with a residual maturity of a year or more. By 2012 the EIB was citing higher regulatory capital requirements as a constraint on banks' long-tenor lending. It was building a capped, subordinated credit enhancement (PBCE) to bring institutional bond investors back without a monoline wrap.

## Verified facts

**Monolines and the end of wrapped project bonds**

1. **How the wrap worked (Engel, Fischer and Galetovic, EIB Papers 2010).** Before the 2008–09 crisis, US PPP projects were often financed with bonds issued at contract close. Sponsors bought cash-flow insurance from a monoline so that rating agencies gave an investment-grade rating "from the start". The monolines thereby "replaced the monitoring role of banks during the construction phase". Because monolines "defaulted on their obligations during the 2008-09 crisis", the authors judged this model "unlikely to return in the foreseeable future". [confidence: high, as to the authors' analysis] [source: 1]
2. **Ambac ratings history:**
   - Fitch cut Ambac Assurance to AA on 18 January 2008;
   - S&P downgraded it from AAA to AA on 5 June 2008;
   - Moody's downgraded it from Aaa to Aa3 on 19 June 2008, and to Baa1 on 5 November 2008;
   - S&P lowered it to A on 19 November 2008.

   As a result, Ambac said it had been able to originate "only a de minimis amount of new financial guarantee business". Ambac Assurance reported a 2008 statutory net loss of approximately USD 4,034.7 million. Its 10-K cites credit risk related to residential mortgage-backed securities and CDOs. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. **MBIA ratings history.** On 5 June 2008 S&P downgraded MBIA Corp.'s insurance financial strength from AAA to AA. On 19 June 2008 Moody's downgraded it from Aaa to A2. MBIA had held AAA ratings from S&P since 1974 and Aaa from Moody's since 1984. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. **UK bond market closes.** The NAO states that before the crisis credit insurance could lift a bond's rating enough for non-specialised lenders such as pension funds. "In late 2007, market confidence in the providers of this credit insurance collapsed, leaving PFI projects in the United Kingdom without access to capital markets." The last PFI bond issue was Northern Ireland Roads II, GBP 146 million, in December 2007. The MoD's Future Strategic Tanker Aircraft project then switched from bond to bank finance. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. **Europe: bonds disappear from new projects.** EIB economists Wagenvoort, de Nicola and Kappeler (Projectware data) found that bond finance in new European projects "dried up almost completely in the crisis", even though infrastructure corporates issued record bond volumes in 2009. They suggest the "disappearance of monoline insurance early on in the crisis" as one explanation. They also found that the loan share rose slightly and that equity shares were broadly stable. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

**Bank retrenchment and pricing**

6. **UK timeline and margins (NAO):**
   - **March 2008:** the Future Strategic Tanker Aircraft (FSTA) financing raised GBP 2,200 million from 8 arranging banks and 15 participants, at about 6% interest and up to 1.15% over bank cost of funds.
   - **After Lehman (September 2008):** some banks withdrew, and others charged more than 1.5% over cost of funds (some as high as 2.2%), against 1% previously.
   - **Syndication:** the collapse of Lehman Brothers "led to a halt in loan syndication, continuing throughout 2009".

   [confidence: high] [source: 4]
7. **Club deals and tighter terms (NAO).** Most banks would not lend more than about GBP 25 million per PFI project, so borrowers had to assemble clubs of five or six banks rather than rely on one lead bank underwriting and syndicating. Banks also "de-risked":
   - lower debt proportions;
   - higher cover ratios;
   - earlier equity injection;
   - tighter distribution conditions.

   [confidence: high] [source: 4]
8. **Interest margins (NAO/KPMG):**

   | Deal | Date | Interest margin | Total interest cost |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Standard PFI deals | 2007 | 0.79% | 5.9% |
   | Standard PFI deals | 2009 | 2.51% | 6.9% |
   | FSTA | March 2008 | 1–1.15% | — |
   | Greater Manchester Waste | April 2009 | 3.25–4.50% | — |
   | M25 | May 2009 | 2.5–3.5% | — |

   UK PFI margins had averaged around 1% or less in 1999–2007, against an international project loan average of 1.7% over 1994–2008. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
9. **Cost to the public sector (NAO).** Higher financing costs raised the annual charge of typical PFI projects by 6–7%, and Greater Manchester Waste's by about 12%. The NAO estimated GBP 500 million to GBP 1 billion of higher cost "locked in". In October 2008 HM Treasury raised the public sector's share of future refinancing gains from 50% to 70%. [confidence: high] [source: 4]

**Government responses**

10. **Stalled market and the EIB (NAO).** In September 2008 HM Treasury asked the EIB to step up infrastructure lending. Between October 2008 and March 2009 only four smaller PFI projects were financed. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
11. **TIFU (NAO).** The Infrastructure Finance Unit was approved at the end of January 2009 and implemented in March 2009. Its purpose was to fund shortfalls in bank finance, on commercial terms, for some 110 projects worth more than GBP 13 billion. After ten months of bank negotiations, Greater Manchester Waste could not raise its last GBP 120 million. The Treasury lent that sum, and the project reached financial close on 8 April 2009 as "the first contract to be let following the credit crisis". The M25 widening PFI closed on 20 May 2009 without a Treasury loan. The Department for Transport had prepared to co-lend on it. No further TIFU loans were required up to the NAO report (July 2010). [confidence: high] [source: 4]
12. **Other models noted by the NAO.** The French government guarantees 80% of PPP debt once a project is operating successfully. The EIB, as a not-for-profit lender, can offer more favourable margins and fees, and some European countries used publicly owned banks similarly. [confidence: high] [source: 4]

**Volumes and recovery**

13. **European PPP volumes (EIB EFR 2010/04):**
    - EU PPP transactions totalled EUR 15.8 billion in 2009, almost 50% below 2007;
    - value fell more than deal numbers as projects got smaller;
    - the lowest quarter was Q4 2009, at about EUR 2 billion;
    - the authors note that the 2008 total includes the FSTA deal (GBP 2.6 billion by their measure), which alone was over 15% of that year's figure.

    They interpret much of the decline as a reversal of an "extraordinary spike" before the crisis. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
14. **Fall and rebound (Wagenvoort, de Nicola and Kappeler, EIB Papers 2010).** The capital value of European PPP projects reaching financial close fell by almost 40% in 2008 from the 2007 peak and by a further 20% in 2009, then bounced back sharply in 2010. The first eight months of 2010 were more than 30% above the same period of 2009, but PPP and non-PPP project values remained about 35% below peak. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
15. **Developing countries (World Bank PPI, May 2010):**
    - investment commitments to new private infrastructure projects fell 45% in the second half of 2008 compared with the second half of 2007;
    - in 2009, 253 projects with USD 85.4 billion of investment reached closure in 50 developing countries, up 15% on 2008;
    - the recovery was concentrated in large energy projects in Brazil, China, India and Turkey; excluding those countries and Russia, 2009 investment would have fallen 32%;
    - for projects that did raise money, the World Bank reported "a higher cost of debt, lower debt/equity ratios, shorter tenors, and more conservative structures";
    - local public banks and bilateral and multilateral agencies were "key sources of finance".

    [confidence: high] [source: 6]

**Basel III and the post-crisis bond response**

16. **Basel III capital (BCBS, December 2010, revised June 2011):**
    - minimum Common Equity Tier 1 of 4.5% of risk-weighted assets (RWA), Tier 1 of 6.0% and total capital of 8.0%;
    - a 2.5% CET1 capital conservation buffer, phased in from 1 January 2016 and fully effective 1 January 2019;
    - a minimum Tier 1 leverage ratio of 3% to be tested during a parallel run;
    - an LCR to be introduced from 1 January 2015, and an NSFR to become a minimum standard by 1 January 2018.

    [confidence: high] [source: 7]
17. **NSFR funding charge (current Basel Framework, NSF30.31).** Other unencumbered performing loans that do not qualify for a 35% or lower standardised risk weight, and that have residual maturities of one year or more (excluding loans to financial institutions), carry an 85% required stable funding (RSF) factor. Typical long-dated project loans fall into this category. [confidence: high for the rule; the "typical project loans" mapping is an inference from standard project risk weights above 35%] [source: 8]
18. **EIB on the post-crisis gap (December 2012):**
    - "pressure on banks' balance sheets from higher regulatory capital requirements has constrained bank long-tenor lending";
    - bond issues had become difficult "since 2007/8 as the monoline insurers … have become significantly less active";
    - bond investors were hesitant to buy the "low BBB range" that a typical project finance deal would achieve without credit enhancement, partly because of their own regulation (Solvency II).

    [confidence: high] [source: 9]
19. **PBCE design.** The EIB's Project Bond Credit Enhancement is a subordinated tranche capped at the lower of EUR 200 million or 20% of the credit-enhanced senior bonds. It is designed to raise the senior bonds' rating "not to extend the EIB's AAA credit rating to the project", which is how it differs from a full monoline wrap. The pilot phase (to 2016) used EUR 230 million of EU budget resources as a first-loss piece. The EIB estimated this could support about EUR 750 million of PBCE and more than EUR 4 billion of project financing. [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

- **Pre-crisis UK PFI structure.** About 90% debt and 10% equity. Debt came from bank loans swapped to fixed rates, or from bonds, often wrapped. [source: 4]
- **Crisis-era structure.** Bank clubs replaced underwrite-and-syndicate. Banks accepted smaller tickets (about GBP 25 million each) and demanded higher margins, lower gearing, higher cover ratios, earlier equity and tighter lock-ups. Public lenders filled gaps: TIFU, the EIB, and a departmental co-lending option on the M25. [source: 4]
- **Post-crisis bond solution.** Instead of a full wrap, a capped subordinated tranche (PBCE) lifts the senior bond's rating, and investors do their own diligence. [source: 9]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **NAO (2010).** Bond finance went first and then bank finance became severely restricted, mostly for reasons outside the Treasury's control. The Treasury's intervention was "timely and helped to reactivate the market" with a single loan. However, prioritising economic stimulus meant closing deals at prevailing market rates. Extra costs of GBP 500 million to GBP 1 billion were locked in, and the NAO doubted more than half could be recovered by refinancing. [source: 4]
- **EIB Papers (2010).** Wagenvoort, de Nicola and Kappeler argue that banks were less sensitive to the monoline collapse than bond investors because they "traditionally do much of the project appraisal and monitoring themselves". Bond investors relying on third-party guarantors and ratings left. Engel, Fischer and Galetovic note that the crisis exposed "the dependency on rating agencies and monolines". [source: 1]
- **World Bank (2010).** "Flight to quality". Development banks and local public banks were critical but cannot fully replace commercial lending, so project selectivity was expected to persist. [source: 6]
- **EIB (2012).** Post-crisis capital rules constrained long-tenor bank lending. Restoring a bond market without monolines needed a new, capped form of credit enhancement. [source: 9]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 3 (How project finance evolved).** The 2008–09 era teaches three lessons:
  - **Concentration of credit enhancement is a systemic risk.** When a handful of AAA monolines guaranteed a large share of project bonds, their mortgage losses shut an entire funding channel overnight, though the projects themselves were largely performing.
  - **Banks that do their own monitoring are more resilient lenders to construction risk than bond investors who outsource diligence.** Bank lending therefore bent (clubs, smaller tickets, wider margins) rather than broke.
  - **Regulation responded.** Basel III's capital, leverage and liquidity rules (especially the NSFR on loans over a year) changed the economics of long-dated bank lending, which opened the way for institutional investors and private credit in the next era.

  Use NAO margin data and the TIFU/Greater Manchester Waste episode as the concrete illustration, and forward-reference t-basel.md content in the Basel chapter.

## Do not state

- Do not state global project finance loan volumes for 2007–2010 (for example, totals from Dealogic, Thomson Reuters/PFI or IJGlobal league tables). No public primary figure was verified here. Use the EU PPP (EIB) and developing-country PPI (World Bank) figures instead.
- Do not say that monolines "defaulted" on project bonds as a matter of fact without qualification. The Engel, Fischer and Galetovic article uses that language, but the verified rating facts are downgrades. Specific project bond claims paid or missed were not checked.
- Do not mix up the FSTA figures. The NAO gives GBP 2,200 million raised in March 2008 in one place and GBP 2.5 billion of funding in another, and the EIB EFR uses GBP 2.6 billion (apparently capital value). Cite the source with any figure.
- Do not claim that Japanese or other specific bank groups "replaced" European lenders after 2008. Not verified here.
- Do not state US- or EU-specific dates for implementing the LCR, NSFR or leverage ratio. See t-basel.md and the national rulebooks; only the BCBS timetable is verified here.
- Do not state that FSA, FDIC or other government guarantee schemes were used for project loans. Not verified.
