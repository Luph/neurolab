# Frontier data: AI and data-centre power demand (IEA), SMR project status, long-duration storage, hydrogen FIDs, private credit growth

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: US NRC construction permit for TVA's Clinch River BWRX-300, 29 September 2026; Stegra additional-capital announcement, 1 October 2026; Ofgem LDES cap-and-floor "minded-to" decision, 26 June 2026; FSB private credit report, 6 May 2026; IEA "Key Questions on Energy and AI", April 2026)

Cross-references: NEOM green hydrogen financing is covered in facts/neom-hydrogen.md, battery safety and insurance in facts/moss-landing.md, and UK nuclear financing (HPC CfD, Sizewell C RAB) in facts/hinkley-sizewell.md and facts/t-uk-cfd-rab.md.

## Summary

The frontier of project finance in 2025-26 is being shaped by electricity demand from AI data centres. The IEA estimates that data centres used about 485 TWh in 2025, up 17% on the year. Consumption by AI-focused data centres rose 50%. The IEA projects data-centre demand roughly doubling to about 950 TWh by 2030, around 3% of global electricity demand. The constraints are physical: grid connections that can take five to ten years, two- to three-year waits for transformers, and gas turbine deliveries of around five years. Developers are turning to onsite gas, batteries, nuclear offtake and capital markets. The IEA estimates USD 3.9 trillion of cumulative data-centre investment in 2026-30, and the FSB reports that private credit has become a key funder. The flagship structure is Meta's Hyperion joint venture with Blue Owl (October 2025), which raised about USD 27 billion of debt through an off-balance-sheet vehicle. New nuclear is moving from paper to concrete:
- Ontario took the G7's first SMR final investment decision (four BWRX-300s, C$20.9 billion, May 2025).
- The US NRC issued construction permits for TerraPower's Natrium (March 2026) and TVA's Clinch River BWRX-300 (29 September 2026).
- The UK chose Wylfa for three Rolls-Royce SMRs (November 2025) and signed the contract (April 2026).
The IEA notes that the pipeline of SMR offtake deals with data centres grew from about 25 GW to 45 GW during 2025, but that the first units will not run before about 2030. Long-duration storage gained a GB revenue framework: Ofgem provisionally selected 16 projects (about 7.6 GW and 137 GWh) for cap-and-floor support in June 2026. Hydrogen is going the other way. The IEA cut announced 2030 low-emissions supply from 49 to 37 Mtpa; only about 4.2 Mtpa is operational or past FID; and flagship projects such as Stegra's green steel plant have needed repeated rescue financing. Private credit, estimated by the FSB at USD 1.5 trillion to USD 2 trillion of lending at end-2024, is growing fast, and in 2025 AI-related sectors accounted for 34% of private credit deals.

## Verified facts

### A. AI and data-centre electricity demand (global; IEA "Energy and AI" (April 2025) and "Key Questions on Energy and AI" (April 2026); IEA updates these regularly, so check iea.org for the latest)

1. IEA (April 2025): data centres consumed about 415 TWh in 2024, about 1.5% of global electricity. In the Base Case, consumption roughly doubles to about 945 TWh by 2030, just under 3% of global demand, growing about 15% a year from 2024 to 2030. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. IEA (April 2025): in the United States, data centres account for nearly half of electricity demand growth to 2030. US data-centre consumption rises by about 240 TWh (up 130%) from 2024. The IEA estimated that about 20% of planned data-centre projects could face grid connection delays. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
3. IEA (April 2026), on 2025 outturn: data-centre electricity demand grew 17% in 2025 and consumption by AI-focused data centres grew 50%. The updated outlook has consumption roughly doubling from 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030 (about 3% of global demand), with AI-focused data-centre consumption tripling. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
4. IEA (April 2026), on physical bottlenecks: transformer lead times average two to three years; gas turbine deliveries could take about five years (citing Wood Mackenzie); grid connection waits can be five to ten years in many jurisdictions; and gas turbine orders rose 70% in 2025. ERCOT (Texas) has received 160 GW of data-centre interconnection requests, twice existing global hyperscale and colocation capacity. Not all of these requests will be built. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. IEA (April 2026), on investment and finance:
   - cutting-edge AI data centres can cost USD 40,000 to 50,000 per kW including IT equipment;
   - the IEA's projections imply USD 3.9 trillion of cumulative data-centre investment in 2026-30, which "is too large to be funded solely from the balance sheets" of AI companies;
   - leading hyperscalers and neo-clouds have announced a 75% increase in 2026 capital expenditure, to USD 715 billion;
   - AI companies issued about USD 200 billion of debt in 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
6. IEA (April 2026), on procurement and onsite power:
   - the tech sector signed about 40% of global corporate renewable PPAs in 2025;
   - the pipeline of data-centre SMR offtake agreements grew from about 25 GW (end-2024) to 45 GW (end-2025), but the first projects are not expected online until around 2030;
   - 15 to 27 GW of onsite gas may power data centres by 2030, mostly in the US, and reliable onsite supply needs 30% to 70% overbuild relative to demand;
   - 20 to 25 GW of batteries could be installed in data centres by 2030. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
7. The US accounts for about half of global installed data-centre capacity and of announced capacity additions. [confidence: high] [source: 4]

### B. Data-centre financing structures and private credit

8. Meta and funds managed by Blue Owl Capital (announced 21 October 2025) formed a joint venture to develop the Hyperion data-centre campus in Louisiana. The main terms were:
   - Blue Owl funds own 80% and Meta 20%;
   - Blue Owl contributed USD 7 billion of cash; Meta contributed land and construction-in-progress and received a one-time distribution of about USD 3 billion;
   - the partners fund their shares of about USD 27 billion of development costs;
   - Meta provides construction and property management;
   - Meta leases the facilities under operating leases with an initial four-year term plus extension options, and gives a capped residual value guarantee covering the first 16 years of operations;
   - debt was raised through private securities offerings, with PIMCO and other bond investors participating. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
9. The Hyperion debt was issued by Beignet Investor LLC: about USD 27.3 billion of bonds, priced at par with a 6.58% coupon. S&P assigned a preliminary A+ rating (stable) on 16 October 2025. [confidence: medium] [source: 6, 7]
10. FSB "Report on Vulnerabilities in Private Credit" (6 May 2026):
    - private credit, defined as nonbank direct lending to medium-sized companies negotiated bilaterally, totalled an estimated USD 1.5 trillion to USD 2 trillion at end-2024;
    - the US market is the largest, followed by the euro area and the UK;
    - growth drivers are post-crisis bank regulation, borrower demand for tailored terms and fast execution, and investor demand for yield and diversification;
    - private credit "remains untested to a prolonged economic downturn". [confidence: high] [source: 8]
11. In the same FSB report, Box 4 (citing Morgan Stanley and the Bank of England) projects AI infrastructure capex of USD 2.9 trillion in 2025-28. About USD 1.5 trillion of that would come from external capital, including about USD 800 billion from private credit. AI-related sectors' share of private credit deals reached 34% in 2025, against an average of 17% over the previous five years. The FSB describes asset-based finance secured on long leases to creditworthy tenants as well suited to data centres. It names two vulnerabilities: valuation corrections, triggered for example by power shortfalls delaying projects, and overcapacity. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
12. The IMF (Global Financial Stability Report, April 2024) put private credit at about USD 2.1 trillion of assets and undeployed commitments in 2023, about three-quarters of it in the US. Note that the IMF and FSB use different definitions. [confidence: high] [source: 9, 10]

### C. SMR and advanced reactor project status

13. Canada (Darlington New Nuclear, Ontario): the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission issued Ontario Power Generation (OPG) a licence to construct one GE Vernova Hitachi BWRX-300 on 4 April 2025. Ontario announced the final investment decision on 8 May 2025. The total budget for four units (up to about 1,200 MW) is C$20.9 billion. The first unit is C$7.7 billion: C$6.1 billion for the reactor plus C$1.6 billion of shared infrastructure. The three later units are estimated at C$13.2 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 11, 12]
14. On 24 October 2025 the Canada Growth Fund (C$2 billion for 15%) and Ontario's Building Ontario Fund (C$1 billion for 7.5%) agreed to take equity stakes in the Darlington SMR project. Funding comes in two tranches, the second subject to milestones. OPG remains majority owner and operator and targets SMR 1 completion and grid connection by end-2030. [confidence: high] [source: 13]
15. United States: the NRC approved a construction permit for TerraPower's Natrium reactor at Kemmerer, Wyoming, in March 2026. It is a 345 MWe sodium-cooled fast reactor with molten-salt storage that can boost output to 500 MWe, supported by DOE's Advanced Reactor Demonstration Program. It is the first NRC construction permit for a commercial non-light-water power reactor, and completion is expected in 2030. [confidence: high] [source: 14, 15]
16. On 29 September 2026 the NRC issued TVA a construction permit for a BWRX-300 (about 300 MW) at Clinch River, Oak Ridge, Tennessee. It is the first US construction permit for a BWRX-300. The application was submitted in 2025. The announcement disclosed no construction cost or schedule. [confidence: high] [source: 16]
17. United States, cautionary case: on 8 November 2023 Utah Associated Municipal Power Systems (UAMPS) and NuScale agreed to terminate the Carbon Free Power Project because it was unlikely to gain enough subscription. Its target power price had risen from USD 58/MWh to USD 89/MWh. [confidence: high] [source: 17, 18]
18. United Kingdom: on 13 November 2025 the government named Wylfa (Anglesey) as the site for the UK's first SMRs, with up to three Rolls-Royce SMR units. Great British Energy - Nuclear (GBE-N) assessed that the site could host up to eight. On 13 April 2026 GBE-N awarded Rolls-Royce SMR the contract for three units of about 470 MW each, covering site-specific design, regulatory engagement and planning ahead of a future FID. The National Wealth Fund lent Rolls-Royce SMR £599 million at market rates. GBE-N received £2.6 billion in the 2025 Spending Review. [confidence: high] [source: 19, 20]
19. China: construction of CNNC's Linglong One (ACP100, 125 MWe) at Changjiang, Hainan, began in July 2021. Trade sources expected commercial operation in 2026. [confidence: medium] [source: 21]

### D. Long-duration energy storage (GB; Ofgem LDES cap and floor scheme pages)

20. On 26 June 2026 Ofgem published its Window 1 "minded-to" decision under the LDES cap-and-floor scheme. It provisionally selected 16 projects out of the 73 assessed (77 had been found eligible in September 2025 and four withdrew), totalling about 7,645 MW and 136.9 GWh, with durations of 8 to 32 hours (Coire Glas, 32 hours, is the longest). The technologies are pumped storage hydro, compressed air, lithium-ion and vanadium redox flow. Consultation closed on 14 August 2026, with final awards expected in autumn 2026 and a decision on a second window by 2027. [confidence: high] [source: 22, 23; aligned with facts/t-cap-and-floor.md items 18, 20 and 21, which cite Ofgem's consultation document]
   - Alignment note (October 3, 2026, consolidation editor): an earlier version of this item said "16 of 73 eligible projects", "durations of 8 to 22 hours" and "consultation ran to 7 August 2026". Those came from secondary summaries. `t-cap-and-floor.md` checked Ofgem's own documents: 77 eligible, 73 assessed after four withdrawals, a project list running to 32 hours, and a consultation closing on 14 August 2026 (one Ofgem page says 15 August). Where the two sheets differ, `t-cap-and-floor.md` governs. Writers say "73 projects assessed" and "8 to 32 hours".
21. Under a cap and floor, consumers top up revenue to a floor if market revenues fall short, and revenue above the cap is shared with consumers. The model is adapted from Ofgem's interconnector regime. [confidence: high] [source: 23, 24]

### E. Hydrogen FIDs (global; IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2025, September 2025)

22. Global hydrogen demand reached almost 100 Mt in 2024 and was still met overwhelmingly by fossil fuels. Low-emissions production grew 10% in 2024 and was on track for about 1 Mt in 2025, still under 1% of the total. [confidence: high] [source: 25]
23. Potential 2030 low-emissions hydrogen production from announced projects fell for the first time, from 49 Mtpa (2024 Review) to 37 Mtpa, mainly because of cancellations and delays. Electrolysis projects caused more than 80% of the drop. Projects that are operational or past FID would deliver about 4.2 Mtpa by 2030, a fivefold increase on 2024. More than 200 low-emissions hydrogen production projects have taken FID since 2020, and FID projects are 9% of the 2030 pipeline. [confidence: high] [source: 25]
24. China held about 65% of global installed electrolyser capacity, and of capacity at FID, and nearly 60% of electrolyser manufacturing capacity. Global installed water electrolysis capacity was 2 GW at end-2024. [confidence: high] [source: 25]
25. Stegra (formerly H2 Green Steel), Boden, Sweden: Stegra had secured about €6.5 billion of funding by early 2024 for its hydrogen-based green steel plant. It raised a further €1.4 billion round in 2026 led by Wallenberg Investments. On 1 October 2026 it said a 100-day review showed completion costs "significantly higher than assumed" and that more capital was needed; it said its production timeline was unchanged. [confidence: medium] [source: 26, 27]

## Financing and structure details

- **Hyperion (Meta/Blue Owl)**: an 80/20 JV owns the campus; debt of about USD 27 billion sits in an SPV (Beignet Investor LLC) and was placed privately with institutional investors. The credit rests on short initial leases (four years) plus Meta's capped residual value guarantee covering 16 years. The off-balance-sheet treatment for Meta depends on that lease and guarantee design. [source: 5, 6]
- **Darlington SMR**: state-owned utility sponsor (OPG); federal and provincial equity in milestone-gated tranches; first-unit budget C$7.7 billion. [source: 12, 13]
- **Wylfa SMR**: state development company (GBE-N) as client; state lender (NWF) funding the technology vendor's generic design at market rates; FID still to come. [source: 20]
- **LDES cap and floor**: a regulated revenue band over the asset's life; the floor supports debt sizing and the cap limits windfalls. [source: 22, 23]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Power as the binding constraint on AI build-out**: the IEA attributes near-term limits to grid connection queues and equipment lead times rather than demand. That is why developers are pursuing onsite gas, needing 30% to 70% overbuild for reliability, despite turbine shortages. [source: 4]
- **Financing has moved from balance sheets to capital markets**: the IEA notes that 2026 capex will absorb almost all hyperscaler operating cash. The FSB records private credit filling the gap, and warns of losses if valuations correct or capacity overshoots. [source: 4, 8]
- **SMRs: commitment versus cost**: NuScale/UAMPS failed when its price rose from USD 58 to USD 89/MWh and subscribers withdrew. Darlington succeeded with a state-owned sponsor, a public budget and government equity. [source: 12, 13, 17]
- **Hydrogen: offtake and cost**: the IEA attributes delays to high costs, uncertain demand and regulation, slow infrastructure and, in particular, difficulty securing reliable offtake. Stegra's repeated capital calls show construction and ramp-up cost risk in a first-of-a-kind integrated project. [source: 25, 27]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 82 (Telecoms, fiber, towers and data centers)**: Use the IEA numbers to explain why power, not land or fibre, now governs data-centre siting and timing: 5-to-10-year grid waits and 2-to-3-year transformer lead times. Dissect Hyperion as a lease-backed SPV. The credit rests on tenant strength, lease tenor versus debt tenor (a four-year initial lease against long bonds) and a residual value guarantee. Ask students what happens to bondholders if Meta does not renew. Contrast this with conventional stabilised data-centre debt secured on multi-tenant leases.
- **Chapter 88 (The frontier)**: Build a framework that asks of any new structure who bears demand risk, construction risk and technology risk, and how each is priced. Apply it to four cases:
  - SMRs: Darlington, with state sponsor and equity, against NuScale/UAMPS, with municipal subscribers who walked away;
  - LDES: a regulated cap and floor;
  - hydrogen: FIDs at only 9% of the pipeline, and Stegra's overruns;
  - private credit: rapid growth, untested in a downturn, with AI concentration rising to 34% of deals.
  Stress that every number here dates quickly. Tell readers to check the IEA's annual updates, Ofgem decisions and FSB/IMF reports.
