# Toll road traffic forecast accuracy: the S&P/Bain studies (2002–2005), Flyvbjerg et al., optimism bias and ramp-up

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest item covered: Bain, Transportation 36(5), 2009; the GHD report for the Australian Department of Infrastructure and Transport, December 8, 2011, is the latest primary source read)

## Summary

Two bodies of evidence define what the profession knows about traffic forecast accuracy.

The first is the Standard & Poor's (S&P) traffic risk program, run by Robert Bain between 2002 and 2005. It compared the traffic forecasts used at financial close with outturn traffic for privately financed toll roads, bridges, and tunnels. The sample grew from 32 projects (2002) to 68 (2003), 87 (2004), and 104 (2005). In every edition, actual year-1 traffic averaged about 0.73–0.77 of forecast, with a standard deviation of about 0.26. That means forecasts overstated year-1 traffic by roughly 20–30 percent on average, and the error range was wide: from 0.14–0.15 to about 1.5 times forecast. The 2005 update ("Through Ramp-Up and Beyond") tested the common defense that forecasts are only wrong in the opening year. It found that the mean ratio stayed at 0.77–0.80 through years 2 to 5. In other words, the shortfall did not close as ramp-up ended.

The second is the academic work of Bent Flyvbjerg and colleagues (Aalborg, later Oxford). Their 2005 study covered 210 transport projects, mostly un-tolled public roads plus rail. Road forecasts were not biased on average: actual traffic was on average 9.5 percent above forecast. But they were very inaccurate: half were off by more than ±20 percent. Rail forecasts were systematically overstated, by an average of about 106 percent.

Bain combined the two data sets. He concluded that toll road forecasts carry about the same random error as toll-free forecasts plus a systematic optimism bias of about 20 percent. He attributes the bias largely to competitive bidding in which the highest traffic forecast tends to win.

The lessons for lenders, as S&P put it, are to adjust base cases for bias before running sensitivities, and to size liquidity for large and persistent shortfalls rather than a brief ramp-up.

## Verified facts

**S&P traffic risk program (Bain and co-authors, 2002–2005)**

1. S&P published four studies:
   - "Credit Implications of Traffic Risk in Start-Up Toll Facilities" (Bain and Wilkins), dated August 15, 2002, in an S&P Infrastructure Finance publication of September 2002;
   - "Traffic Forecasting Risk: Study Update 2003" (Bain and Plantagie), November 6, 2003;
   - "Traffic Forecasting Risk: Study Update 2004" (Bain and Plantagie), October 19, 2004;
   - "Traffic Forecasting Risk Study Update 2005: Through Ramp-Up and Beyond" (Bain and Polakovic), August 25, 2005.

   [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3, 4]
2. **Method.** Performance is measured as the ratio of actual to forecast traffic, mainly at the end of year 1. The forecast used is the one embedded in the financial model at financial close, because that was the basis for lending. The sample grew by adding:
   - projects with public S&P ratings (about 30 rated toll roads);
   - bank-financed roads in collateralized debt and loan obligation (CDO/CLO) portfolios that S&P rated. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
3. **2002 study (32 case studies).** Twenty-eight of 32 forecasts over-predicted year-1 traffic. The fitted distribution had a mean of 0.73, so traffic averaged about 70 percent of forecast. The study split forecasts by who commissioned them:
   - forecasts commissioned by banks had a mean ratio of 0.82 (range 0.45–1.08);
   - forecasts commissioned by "Others" (sponsors and bidders) had a mean of 0.66 (range 0.31–1.19).

   The banks' average error (18 percent) was nearly half that of the Others (34 percent). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. **Causes of error reported in 2002.** Almost without exception, the reasons lay outside the traffic model itself, in its input assumptions:
   - high tolls and a misjudged willingness to pay;
   - recession;
   - land-use scenarios that never happened;
   - lower time savings than expected;
   - improvements to competing free routes;
   - much lower truck use;
   - lower off-peak and weekend traffic. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. **2002 suggested stress profiles.** S&P proposed revenue adjustments for stress tests.
   - Bank-commissioned forecasts: year-1 haircuts of 10 / 20 / 30 percent (low / average / high risk), ramp-up of 2 / 5 / 8 years, and catch-up of full / −5 percent / −10 percent.
   - Forecasts commissioned by Others: year-1 haircuts of 20 / 35 / 55 percent, with the same ramp-up durations and catch-up of full / −10 percent / −20 percent.

   S&P described these as starting points, not definitive rules. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. **2003 update.** The sample was enlarged from 32 to 68 projects, and the mean year-1 ratio was 0.74. Subsample analysis found smaller errors and less bias in countries where toll roads were well established. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
7. **2004 update (87 projects, 19 of them new).** The mean year-1 ratio was 0.76 and the standard deviation 0.26. The range ran from 15 percent of forecast to more than 50 percent above forecast. S&P concluded that toll road forecasts "on average" overestimate year-1 traffic by 20–30 percent. New projects were admitted only if their data could be second-sourced and compared on a like-for-like basis. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
8. **2004 comparison with toll-free roads.** S&P compared its toll road data with Flyvbjerg's data on 183 roads, more than 90 percent of them toll-free (unpublished at the time; used with permission).
   - The toll-free distribution centered near 1.0, with a long right-hand tail (less than 7 percent of projects exceeded forecast by more than 100 percent).
   - The toll road distribution sat about 0.2 to the left.
   - The spreads were similar.

   S&P concluded that optimism bias is the distinguishing feature of toll forecasts. Shadow toll roads cannot be presumed to have lower traffic risk just because drivers do not pay at the point of use. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 5]
9. **2005 update (104 case studies; more than 90 percent project-financed concessions).** The year-1 mean was 0.77 and the standard deviation 0.26. For the subset with multi-year data, the means and standard deviations by year from opening were:

   | Year | Mean | Std dev |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 | 0.77 | 0.26 |
   | 2 | 0.78 | 0.23 |
   | 3 | 0.79 | 0.22 |
   | 4 | 0.80 | 0.24 |
   | 5 | 0.79 | 0.25 |

   Only seven case studies covered years 1–6. S&P concluded there was no automatic improvement in accuracy after year 1. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
10. **Ramp-up (2005).** Most case-study forecasts already included some ramp-up profile. Actual ramp-up was "often far less aggressive than is assumed" and could take many years, and a number of roads still fell short after ramp-up ended. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
11. **Trucks (2005).** The standard deviation of truck forecasts was 0.33, against 0.26 for all vehicles. S&P noted three features of the revenue mix:
    - trucks commonly pay 2–5 times the car toll (sometimes up to 10 times);
    - on one reviewed road, trucks were under 10 percent of traffic but over 25 percent of revenue;
    - on some French networks trucks contribute one third of toll income. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
12. **Conflicting base cases (2005).** S&P compared four base-case forecasts for the same road, made by different firms at about the same time. The gap between the highest and lowest grew with the horizon: 26 percent at 5 years, 66 percent at 10 years, 106 percent at 15 years, 204 percent at 30 years, and 255 percent at 35 years. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
13. **Sample bias (S&P's own caveat).** The sample was not random. It over-represented higher-credit-quality facilities presented for rating or selected into CLO portfolios. Results were therefore "likely to be flattered" relative to global toll road performance. [confidence: high] [source: 4]

**Bain's synthesis (Transportation, 2009)**

14. **Published synthesis.** Bain, "Error and optimism bias in toll road traffic forecasts," *Transportation* 36(5): 469–482 (2009), consolidates the S&P program. Its findings for the 104 projects:
    - the fitted normal distribution had a mean of 0.77 and a standard deviation of 0.26;
    - the range was 0.14 to 1.51;
    - a t-test rejected a mean of 1.0 at the 1 percent level.

    Bain also cites an earlier J.P. Morgan study (1997) of 14 US toll roads. Only one of those beat its revenue forecast, and the actual/forecast ratios averaged 0.58 (range 0.18–1.17). [confidence: high] [source: 5]
15. **Tolling culture.** Bain (2009) reports that forecasts in countries new to tolling had a mean ratio of 0.58 (sd 0.26). In countries with a strong history of tolling the mean was 0.81 (sd 0.24). [confidence: high] [source: 5]
16. **Attribution.** Bain attributes the random error to the intrinsic difficulty of forecasting, and the bias to "strategic game-playing." Concessions are commonly awarded to the bidder with the highest traffic and revenue projections. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

**Flyvbjerg et al.**

17. **Sample.** Flyvbjerg, Skamris Holm and Buhl, "How (In)accurate Are Demand Forecasts in Public Works Projects? The Case of Transportation," *Journal of the American Planning Association* 71(2): 131–146 (2005), studied 210 projects in 14 countries worth approximately USD 59 billion (2004 prices): 27 rail and 183 road projects completed 1969–1998. Inaccuracy is defined as actual minus forecast traffic, as a percentage of forecast, for the first year of operation. The forecast is the one available at the decision to build. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
18. **Road results.**
    - Forecasts underestimated traffic by an average of 8.7 percent, so actual traffic was on average 9.5 percent higher than forecast (sd 44.3).
    - 50 percent of road projects missed by more than ±20 percent, and 25 percent by more than ±40 percent.
    - 21.3 percent were below −20 percent and 28.4 percent above +20 percent.

    The data did not allow toll and non-toll roads to be compared. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
19. **Rail results.** Passenger forecasts were overestimated by an average of 105.6 percent, and actual traffic averaged 51.4 percent below forecast. 84 percent of rail projects fell more than 20 percent short. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
20. **Trend and stated causes.** Forecasts did not become more accurate over the 30-year period studied. The most frequently stated causes differed by mode:
    - for roads: uncertainty in trip generation and land-use development;
    - for rail: uncertainty about trip distribution, and "deliberately slanted forecasts." [confidence: high] [source: 6]
21. **Ramp-up (Flyvbjerg, 2005).** In "Measuring Inaccuracy in Travel Demand Forecasting: Methodological Considerations Regarding Ramp Up and Sampling," *Transportation Research Part A* 39(6): 522–530 (2005), Flyvbjerg argues against the claim that first-year comparisons overstate error. Projects with low first-year traffic also tend to stay low later. His examples:
    - Channel Tunnel: more than five years after opening, Eurostar passengers were 45 percent of the opening-year forecast, and rail freight was 40 percent.
    - Humber Bridge: traffic was still about half of forecast 16 years after opening.
    - New Little Belt Bridge (Denmark): it took more than 20 years to reach forecast.

    He also notes that some forecasts assume a ramp-up over the first 3–5 years, for example 50 percent of modeled demand in year 1 and 80 percent in year 2. In that case an opening-year comparison measures both model error and ramp-up error. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

**Official inquiry (Australia)**

22. In December 2011, GHD reported to the Australian Department of Infrastructure and Transport on over-optimistic patronage forecasts. It studied two major cases, CityLink (Melbourne) and Lane Cove Tunnel (Sydney), and two supporting cases, Westlink M7 and Go Between Bridge. Its findings:
    - PPP bidding processes lead to selection of "the most optimistic of optimistic forecasts."
    - Bid-team modelers produce a "low" forecast for lenders and a "high" one for equity, and the highest equity forecast almost always wins.
    - Ramp-up estimates are especially prone to optimism.
    - Pressures are greater where construction costs are high (tunnels), competition is intense, and governments accept up-front concession payments. [confidence: high] [source: 8]

## Financing and structure details

How the evidence feeds into lending, as stated by the sources themselves:
- S&P (2004): adjust base-case forecasts for suspected optimism bias first, then run sensitivity tests (source 2).
- S&P (2005): an investment-grade toll road "is the one that performs robustly against a number of likely future-year scenarios." Such projects need liquidity throughout the concession sized from those stress tests (source 4).
- Bain (2009): "transaction structures need to retain sufficient flexibility, liquidity and liquidity support" to absorb large departures from forecast (source 5).
- The S&P Traffic Risk Index scores projects from 0 (good) to 10 (bad) on a set of attributes. The attributes include tolling culture, tariff escalation freedom, forecast horizon, and others listed in Bain (2009), Table 4. It is a checklist and does not produce a mechanical haircut (sources 1, 5).

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Bias vs error.** S&P and Bain (sources 2, 5) decompose inaccuracy into two parts:
  - random error, which is similar for toll and toll-free roads;
  - systematic optimism of about 20 percent, which is specific to toll roads.

  Bain attributes the bias to bidding incentives. GHD (source 8) reached the same conclusion for Australia from interviews.
- **Who commissions the forecast matters.** Bank-commissioned forecasts were less biased (S&P 2002 means: 0.82 for banks vs 0.66 for Others). That supports lenders commissioning their own traffic advisers rather than relying on sponsors' forecasts (source 1).
- **Ramp-up is not a cure.** Both S&P (2005) and Flyvbjerg (2005) reject the view that early shortfalls reliably catch up (sources 4, 7). Practitioners such as Vassallo (2007, cited by Bain) had argued that opening-year forecasts are the hardest to make. The S&P multi-year data did not support systematic improvement, but the multi-year sample was small (source 5).
- **Where experts differ.** Flyvbjerg's road sample shows no average bias. The S&P toll sample shows a strong one. Both can be right: the samples differ (public un-tolled roads vs privately financed toll concessions), and so do the incentives. This sheet favors the reading that bias is driven by incentives, because the S&P split by who commissioned the forecast and the GHD interviews point the same way.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 48 (Technical, resource and market diligence):**
  - Teach the reader to read a traffic study by first asking who commissioned it and when.
  - Use the S&P 2002 split (banks 0.82 vs Others 0.66), the 2005 conflicting-base-case table (106 percent gap at 15 years), and the truck volatility finding as a checklist for challenging the Case T traffic study.
  - Show the difference between error, which is handled by sensitivities, and bias, which is handled by adjusting the base case before running sensitivities.
- **Ch 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels; Case T ramp-up):**
  - Model Case T's ramp-up against the empirical evidence: mean 0.77 in year 1 and still about 0.79–0.80 by year 5.
  - Use S&P's 2002 stress grid (year-1 haircut, ramp-up duration, catch-up) to build downside cases.
  - Cross-refer to the facts sheets on SH 130, Dulles Greenway, Indiana Toll Road and the Sydney tunnels for named failures. Use the tolling-culture split (0.58 vs 0.81) to discuss first toll roads in a country.

## Do not state

- Do not attribute the S&P findings to "S&P/Bain studies 2002–2005" as if they were peer-reviewed. They were rating-agency research reports. The peer-reviewed synthesis is Bain (2009).
- Do not say the S&P sample covered named roads. The data set was anonymized.
- Do not claim the S&P mean improves to 1.0 after ramp-up, or that ramp-up typically lasts a fixed number of years. Durations varied by project.
- Do not state the 2003 update's publication date as November 6, 2004. The 2004 report contains that apparent typo; the 2005 report gives November 6, 2003.
- Do not use Flyvbjerg's 2005 road results as evidence about toll roads specifically. The authors state their data could not separate toll from non-toll roads.
- Do not quote figures from the Flyvbjerg arXiv draft as verbatim journal text without checking against the published JAPA version. The arXiv copy is marked as a referee draft.
- Do not cite later updates (for example post-2009 studies by others, or Bain's later consultancy reports) without reading them. They were not reviewed here.
