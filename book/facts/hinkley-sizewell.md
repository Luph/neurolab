# Hinkley Point C (CfD) and Sizewell C (RAB, FID 2025)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: EDF's Hinkley Point C cost and schedule update of 20 February 2026; National Audit Office report on Sizewell C, 20 May 2026)

## Summary

Hinkley Point C (HPC) in Somerset and Sizewell C (SZC) in Suffolk are near-identical two-reactor EPR stations of about 3.2 GW each. They were financed under two different British models, and comparing them is one of the clearest lessons in how risk allocation sets the cost of capital. HPC was financed entirely by its sponsors, EDF (66.5%) and China General Nuclear (CGN, 33.5%). Its revenue support is a 35-year, CPI-indexed contract for difference (CfD) at a strike price of £92.50/MWh in 2012 prices, cut to £89.50/MWh once SZC reached its final investment decision. Nothing is paid until the station generates, so the sponsors carry all construction risk. The government approved the deal on 29 September 2016 at an expected construction cost of £18 billion (2016 prices). By February 2026 EDF's estimate had risen to about £35 billion (2015 prices), with Unit 1 now expected in 2030 rather than 2025. The overruns fell on EDF, which impaired its investment heavily, not on consumers. After HPC, no other developer would sign a CfD-only deal for nuclear. Government then legislated the nuclear regulated asset base (RAB) in the Nuclear Energy (Financing) Act 2022, modelled on the Thames Tideway Tunnel. Under that model SZC reached FID on 22 July 2025 and financial close in November 2025, at a project baseline cost of £38.2 billion (2024-25 prices). The equity is held by the UK government (44.9%), La Caisse (20%), Centrica (15%), EDF (12.5%) and Amber Infrastructure (7.6%). Debt is up to £36.6 billion from the state-owned National Wealth Fund plus £5 billion of commercial bank debt, 94% covered by France's export credit agency. Consumers began paying through electricity bills during construction after November 2025. Cost overruns are shared 50:50 between investors and consumers within regulatory thresholds, and a government support package covers the tail. The NAO judged that SZC's cost of finance is about 5 percentage points lower than HPC's after adjusting for interest rates, but that consumers may still pay more per MWh for SZC than for HPC.

## Verified facts

### Hinkley Point C

1. The government (Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy) announced on 29 September 2016 that it had reached a deal with EDF and CGN to support construction of HPC. The EDF Board took its final investment decision in July 2016, and the government then paused for two months before approving. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The project company is NNB Generation Company (HPC) Limited (NNBG). At the deal it was owned 66.5% by EDF and 33.5% by CGN, and HPC was to be financed in full by these two investors. CGN agreed in October 2015 to invest £6 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. HPC comprises two EPR units with a combined output of 3.2 GW (1.6 GW each). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The CfD strike price is £92.50/MWh in 2012 prices. It is indexed to CPI and lasts 35 years from the start of generation. The government and EDF agreed the strike price in October 2013, and the European Commission gave state aid approval in October 2014. The strike price was to fall to £89.50/MWh (2012 prices) if a final investment decision was taken on Sizewell C. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. When the market reference price is below the strike price, NNBG receives top-up payments from the Low Carbon Contracts Company, funded by electricity consumers. When the market price is above the strike price, NNBG pays the difference back. The CfD also includes a mechanism for consumers to share in construction cost savings, and the Secretary of State Investor Agreement (SoSIA) includes an equity gain-share above a return threshold. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. The SoSIA lets NNBG be compensated if a change in government policy leads to HPC being shut down. In 2017 the government estimated this could cost up to £22 billion (2012 prices). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. HM Treasury agreed an initial guarantee of up to £2 billion of NNBG bonds, subject to conditions to be met by December 2018. A further guarantee of up to £13.1 billion could be considered later, subject to conditions and ministerial approval. EDF said it did not expect NNBG to draw on the initial guarantee. [confidence: high for the terms; whether any guarantee was ever used was not verified] [source: 1]
8. At the 2016 deal, NNBG expected construction to cost £18 billion (2016 prices), with generation from 2025 to 2085. The NAO said the strike price gave NNBG a return of about 9% over the project's life. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. Because forecast wholesale prices fell, the NAO estimated in 2017 that the present value of expected CfD top-up payments had risen from £6 billion (2013) to £30 billion (March 2016). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. In 2017 the NAO concluded that the deal committed the developers to bear construction risk and protected taxpayers and consumers from overruns. It also noted that consumers could end up paying more than if government had shared risks, and it urged alternative financing approaches. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
11. Cost and schedule revisions:
    - May 2022: EDF estimated £25 to 26 billion (2015 prices), with Unit 1 in June 2027.
    - 23 January 2024: EDF estimated £31 to 34 billion (2015 prices), with Unit 1 in a 2029 to 2031 range (base case 2030). EDF's UK managing director stated that consumers and taxpayers would not bear the cost.
    - 20 February 2026: EDF put the cost at about £35 billion (2015 prices) and confirmed Unit 1 for 2030. It cited lower-than-expected productivity on the electromechanical installation programme. A further 12-month delay would add about £1 billion (2015 prices).

    [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4, 5]
12. EDF impaired its HPC investment for cost or schedule reasons by about £8.8 billion in its 2023 accounts and about £1.5 billion (NAO conversion of EUR 1,769 million) in its 2025 accounts. In February 2024 EDF reported that HPC's rate of return was below its cost of capital. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
13. The HPC strike price was reduced by £3/MWh, from £92.50 to £89.50/MWh (2012 prices), because SZC reached FID. The SZC company paid HPC £1.6 billion in November 2025 for access to HPC information. The NAO states that this payment was calculated to exactly offset the impact of the £3/MWh reduction. Restated by the NAO, the HPC price is £128.94/MWh in 2025 prices. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

### Sizewell C

14. From 2008 to 2022 government policy was to support privately developed and funded nuclear. Four other projects in development in early 2017 had fallen away by the end of 2022, leaving SZC. The NAO says DESNZ concluded that taxpayers and consumers would have to accept more construction risk to attract investors. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
15. The Nuclear Energy (Financing) Act 2022 created the nuclear RAB model. Under it the company receives a set return on accepted construction spend and a regulated return during operation, which reduces its exposure to construction risk. Ofgem is SZC's economic regulator. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
16. In November 2022 the government announced a £679 million investment to take a 50% stake in the SZC company, allowing CGN to exit. By November 2025 it had injected £5 billion and owned 90% of the equity before the new investors came in. Construction started in April 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
17. The government announced SZC's final investment decision on 22 July 2025. That was 4.5 years after negotiations began and at least 28 months later than first planned. Financial close followed in November 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 6, 7]
18. Equity at close: DESNZ 44.9%, La Caisse 20%, Centrica 15%, EDF 12.5% and Amber Infrastructure 7.6%, of up to £8.5 billion of equity in nominal terms. Amber has an option to rise to 10%, which would dilute the government to 42.5%. DESNZ accepted 12.5% from EDF rather than its earlier assumption of up to 19.9%. DESNZ has committed up to £3.8 billion of equity. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 6]
19. Debt: the National Wealth Fund provides up to £36.6 billion (nominal), which is 85.5% to 88.0% of construction debt depending on cost. Commercial lenders provide £5 billion, 94% of it guaranteed by Bpifrance Assurance Export. Debt is capped at 65% of the RAB, a level DESNZ said rating agencies required for an investment-grade rating. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 8]
20. DESNZ agreed with investors a construction-period WACC of 6.73% (real, CPIH), with Ofgem to set it periodically once the plant operates. Expected post-tax nominal equity returns are 10.8% to 13.0% between the lower and higher regulatory thresholds after successful construction. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
21. Cost metrics (2024-25 prices):
    - the company's project baseline is £38.2 billion (fully operational by July 2039);
    - DESNZ's lower regulatory threshold is £40.5 billion (October 2039);
    - DESNZ's higher regulatory threshold is £47.7 billion (August 2043). Government and investors committed funding up to this level.

    Within the thresholds, the RAB is set at the lower threshold plus half the difference between allowed capex and that threshold, so investors and consumers share overruns and savings equally. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
22. The SZC government support package comprises:
    - a Contingent Financing Agreement, which allows Exchequer funding of overruns above the higher threshold;
    - a Supplemental Compensation Agreement for insurance shortfalls, which is uncapped;
    - a Government Liquidity Facility Agreement, a revolving loan facility of up to £8 billion nominal;
    - a Discontinuation and Compensation Agreement, capped at the RAB value, expected to peak at about £70 billion nominal at the higher threshold;
    - a Nuclear Administration and Statutory Transfers Agreement, which is a special administration regime.

    [confidence: high] [source: 2]
23. Above the higher threshold, the Secretary of State can approve further spending (with limits on investor returns), provide further government funding that dilutes private equity, or discontinue the project. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
24. Consumers began paying for SZC through electricity bills after November 2025. DESNZ expects about £4 on a typical household bill in 2025-26, rising to a peak of about £19 to £21 a year in the first decade of operation (2024-25 prices). The government's FID announcement described the cost as about £1 a month on average over construction. HM Treasury plans to "recycle" government profits to cut the bill impact by up to £19.2 to £20.9 billion over SZC's life, but this is a policy choice that could change. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 6]
25. The NAO estimated SZC's equivalent strike price after recycling at £130.67 to £155.41/MWh (2024-25 prices), between the project baseline and the higher threshold. HPC's price is £128.94/MWh (2025 prices). SZC would therefore need to come in below its project baseline to beat HPC's price, even though its baseline cost is 22% below the lowest current HPC estimate. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
26. The NAO calculated that using public finance instead of 100% private finance raises SZC's net benefits by about £21 billion. DESNZ justifies private investors' involvement by assuming they will save at least £3.9 billion of cost and 24 months of delay, which would offset the at least £4.0 billion cost of private finance. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
27. About 85% of SZC's above-ground design replicates HPC. The company says it has at least a high level of certainty on about 60% of its cost estimate, including some inflation-linked fixed-price contracts. Generation is expected from 2038, for at least 60 years. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

## Financing and structure details

**HPC (CfD, sponsor-financed)**
- Sponsors: EDF 66.5%, CGN 33.5% at the 2016 deal. A December 2025 dilution of CGN to about 23% has been reported in the press (see Do not state). [source: 1, 5]
- Revenue: 35-year CPI-indexed CfD from the start of generation, with the counterparty being the Low Carbon Contracts Company. No revenue during construction. [source: 1]
- Government support: SoSIA, which compensates political shutdown (up to £22 billion, 2012 prices) and includes an equity gain-share; HM Treasury bond guarantee of up to £2 billion initially, with up to £13.1 billion possible later; and a Funded Decommissioning Programme. [source: 1]
- Construction financing: from sponsor equity. EDF bears the overruns. [source: 1, 4]

**SZC (nuclear RAB, mixed public-private)**
- Company: Sizewell C Ltd, regulated by Ofgem under a RAB licence. [source: 2]
- Equity: DESNZ 44.9%, La Caisse 20%, Centrica 15%, EDF 12.5%, Amber 7.6% (option to 10%); up to £8.5 billion nominal in total. [source: 2]
- Debt: NWF up to £36.6 billion; £5 billion commercial, 94% Bpifrance-guaranteed; gearing cap 65% of RAB. [source: 2, 8]
- Returns: construction WACC 6.73% (CPIH-real); equity IRR 10.8% to 13.0% nominal post-tax. [source: 2]
- Cost sharing: 50:50 pain/gain between the lower threshold (£40.5 billion) and the higher threshold (£47.7 billion). Above that, the government decides. [source: 2]
- Government support package: see fact 22. [source: 2]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **HPC, risk transfer worked but priced high**: The NAO (2017) found that the CfD protected consumers and taxpayers from construction overruns, which later proved to be roughly a doubling of real cost. But the price embedded a return (about 9%) for bearing that risk, and top-up payments rose sharply as wholesale price forecasts fell. [source: 1]
- **HPC, the model could not be repeated**: The NAO (2026) records that EDF ruled out a similar deal, and that no other developer agreed CfD-only nuclear terms. This is what led to the RAB model. [source: 2]
- **SZC, lower cost of capital through state finance and consumer risk-sharing**: The NAO (2026) estimates SZC's cost of finance at about 5 percentage points below HPC's after adjusting for the risk-free rate, and finds that public finance adds about £21 billion of net benefit. Consumers and taxpayers, however, now bear most construction risk. [source: 2]
- **SZC, the case for private capital is contested**: The NAO notes a departure from the usual private-finance rationale (risk transfer) towards a rationale based on better management. It warned that investor incentives may be insufficient to deliver the £3.9 billion savings needed to justify private finance, and that the same benefits might be achieved more cheaply. [source: 2]
- **SZC, replication and delay**: The NAO credits the 28-plus months of delay before FID with improving cost and schedule estimates and supply chain readiness. It notes that EPRs elsewhere (France, Finland) ran more than a decade late and cost four times their original estimates. [source: 2]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 19 (FiTs, CfDs and RAB models)**: Put HPC and SZC side by side as the same asset under two support schemes. With a two-sided CfD, the sponsor takes construction risk and is paid only on output. With a RAB, the company is paid on spend from day one, overruns are shared through thresholds, and the state backstops the tail. Have students trace who bears a £5 billion overrun under each, and why the required return falls from about 9% to a 6.73% real WACC. Note the trade-off the NAO flags: lower financing cost, but consumers paying before any power is delivered.
- **Chapter 74 (nuclear)**: Use HPC to show why nuclear construction risk is so hard to finance privately: FOAK and near-FOAK overruns, the "first concrete to first power" gap, and impairments landing on the sponsor. Use SZC to show the modern toolkit: design replication, state equity and debt (NWF), ECA-covered bank debt, RAB thresholds, and a five-part government support package covering uninsurable and political risks. The £3/MWh HPC strike reduction offset by a £1.6 billion payment is a neat illustration of how sequenced projects interlock commercially.

## Do not state

- Do not state that HPC's CfD is still £92.50/MWh. Following SZC's FID it is £89.50/MWh (2012 prices).
- Do not state construction cost figures without the price base. HPC's estimates are in 2015 prices (except the £18 billion from 2016, which is in 2016 prices), and SZC's are in 2024-25 prices. Press figures of about £46 to 49 billion for HPC in money-of-the-day terms were not verified from an EDF primary source.
- Do not state that CGN's HPC stake is now 23.31%, or that it has stopped funding. This was reported by New Civil Engineer (February 2026) citing EDF but not verified from EDF's own documents. Treat it as medium confidence at best.
- Do not state that the £2 billion HPC bond guarantee was drawn.
- Do not state that EDF raised a specific amount of HPC-related debt from Apollo. This was reported in the press but not verified.
- Do not state SZC's Day-1 RAB value or first-year allowed revenue without checking Ofgem's published letters. They were seen only in search summaries.
- Do not describe SZC's equity as majority government-owned. The government holds 44.9%, the largest single stake but a minority.
- Do not quote the "around £1 a month" bill figure without context. It is the government's average over construction, while the NAO gives £4 in 2025-26 rising to £19 to £21 a year at peak (2024-25 prices).
