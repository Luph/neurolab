# Indiana Toll Road

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: 27 May 2015, IFM Investors' financial close on its acquisition of the concession company; later IFM refinancings noted briefly)

## Summary

The Indiana Toll Road (ITR) is a 157-mile tolled interstate across northern Indiana linking the Chicago Skyway to the Ohio Turnpike. Under Governor Mitch Daniels' 2005 "Major Moves" legislation, the Indiana Finance Authority (IFA) leased the road for 75 years to ITR Concession Company LLC (ITRCC), owned equally by Cintra (Ferrovial) and Macquarie entities, which paid an upfront concession fee of about USD 3.8 billion at closing on 29 June 2006. The state used the money to fund a fully funded 10-year statewide highway program and paid off the road's existing debt. The concessionaire financed its bid aggressively: a nine-year, interest-only bullet bank loan hedged by a 20-year accreting interest rate swap with pre-set rates rising from 3 percent to 11.3 percent by 2023, on about USD 3.4 billion of initial debt. When traffic fell short of forecast in the Great Recession and interest rates fell, the swap's mark-to-market losses ballooned: by 2014 the company had about USD 4.4 billion of debt (USD 3.9 billion of it maturing by June 2015) plus a swap liability of about USD 2.15 billion, taking secured claims to around USD 6 billion. ITRCC filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 in September 2014 in Chicago, with most of its debt by then held by hedge funds and distressed investors. The plan allowed either a sale or a restructuring; a sale process produced a USD 5.725 billion purchase by IFM Investors, which closed on 27 May 2015 with 66 years left on the lease. The road kept operating throughout, the state kept its USD 3.8 billion, and no public money was used. The case is the standard illustration that a concession can be a sound public deal and still be destroyed at the concessionaire level by leverage and a hedging structure that does not fit the asset.

## Verified facts

1. The Indiana General Assembly enacted the "Major Moves" legislation in 2005 under Governor Mitch Daniels, authorizing the lease of the Indiana Toll Road. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 6]
2. The Cintra–Macquarie consortium won the concession with a bid of USD 3.8 billion in January 2006. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 9]
3. On 29 June 2006 the Indiana Finance Authority received USD 3.8 billion from ITR Concession Company for 75 years of operation and management rights; ITRCC assumed operations on 30 June 2006. Some sources give the figure as USD 3.85 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 4]
4. At closing, ITRCC was co-owned equally by Cintra Concesiones de Infraestructuras de Transporte S.A. and Macquarie Infrastructure Group. By 2014 the Macquarie half was held 25 percent by Macquarie Infrastructure Partners and 25 percent by Macquarie Atlas Roads. [confidence: high for 2006; medium for 2014 split] [source: 1, 7]
5. The governor's office said the proceeds would fund the next 10 years of statewide road and bridge construction (including projects such as the US 31 upgrade, the Hoosier Heartland Corridor and I-69), plus USD 240 million to the seven Toll Road counties, USD 120 million to the Northwest Indiana Regional Development Authority and USD 150 million shared among all 92 counties. The operator committed about USD 400 million of improvements over the first four years. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. The Tax Foundation summarized the allocation as about USD 2.8 billion to Major Moves infrastructure, about USD 200 million to pay off existing Toll Road debt, USD 150 million to counties, USD 240 million to local infrastructure and USD 500 million to a trust account. [confidence: medium] [source: 5]
7. The original debt package was a nine-year, interest-only bullet loan hedged with a 20-year accreting interest rate swap whose rates were pre-set to rise from 3 percent to 11.3 percent by 2023. Initial debt was approximately USD 3.4 billion. [confidence: medium (single industry-association case study, consistent with press)] [source: 8]
8. Total project debt rose from about USD 3.4 billion toward about USD 6 billion, which GIIA attributes to declining interest rates creating mark-to-market losses on the swap; these losses prevented early refinancing. [confidence: medium] [source: 8]
9. In June 2014 the Indianapolis Business Journal reported ITRCC's debt at USD 4.4 billion, of which USD 3.9 billion matured within 12 months and had to be refinanced by June 2015, while the road was earning an operating profit from tolls. Tolls had risen about 2.7 percent on 1 July 2014 (full-length car toll to USD 10.00). [confidence: high] [source: 9]
10. Most original bank lenders had sold their positions on the secondary market; by August 2014 about 80 percent of the debt was reported as held by hedge funds and distressed-debt investors, including Strategic Value Partners and King Street Capital Management. [confidence: medium] [source: 7]
11. ITRCC announced on 22 September 2014 that it had filed for Chapter 11 under a prepackaged plan agreed with its principal creditors, and that a sale process would begin. The case was heard in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Northern District of Illinois (Chicago) by Judge Pamela S. Hollis. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4, 10]
12. In the bankruptcy, ITRCC's debt was reported as more than USD 6 billion, including about USD 2.15 billion of liability tied to interest rate swaps. The Bond Buyer attributed the failure to overly optimistic traffic projections, a debt-heavy capital structure and the swap liability. [confidence: high] [source: 10, 11]
13. The plan provided for either a sale of the lease or a restructuring of the debt (a proposed new debt of about USD 2.75 billion under the restructuring alternative), with general unsecured creditors paid in full. The IBJ reported secured obligations of about USD 6.3 billion including projected interest, support from 87 percent of senior secured debtholders and unanimous equity support, and court approval of the plan in October 2014. [confidence: medium] [source: 10, 11]
14. On 11 March 2015 IFM Investors announced an agreement to buy ITRCC for USD 5.725 billion; it reached financial close on 27 May 2015, inheriting the remaining 66 years of the 75-year lease. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 12]
15. Since acquisition IFM has refinanced its acquisition financing and launched a USD 200 million "80/90 PUSH" rehabilitation of a 73-mile segment. [confidence: medium] [source: 12]
16. The road continued operating throughout, the IFA kept the upfront payment, and no taxpayer money was used to resolve the bankruptcy. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7]

## Financing and structure details

- Grantor: Indiana Finance Authority (lessor); oversight by INDOT. [source: 1, 4]
- Concession: 75-year lease of operation, maintenance, tolling and capital improvement rights; tolls rise under a schedule in the lease. [source: 1, 9]
- Upfront payment: about USD 3.8 billion (USD 3.85 billion in some sources), all-cash at close. [source: 1, 5]
- Sponsors: Cintra 50 percent, Macquarie 50 percent. [source: 1]
- Debt: private equity plus senior bank debt; about USD 3.4 billion initial debt; nine-year interest-only bullet loan; 20-year accreting swap, pre-set rates 3 percent rising to 11.3 percent by 2023. [source: 8]
- At filing: senior debt about USD 4 billion-plus, swap liability about USD 2.15 billion, total claims about USD 6 billion or more. [source: 9, 10, 11]
- Exit: sale to IFM Global Infrastructure Fund for USD 5.725 billion (2015). [source: 4, 12]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Leverage and back-ended debt service. Robert Poole of the Reason Foundation (22 September 2014) attributed the failure to an overly optimistic traffic and revenue projection combined with a very aggressive financing structure requiring large debt service toward the end of the first decade, both undermined by the Great Recession. [source: 6]
- Hedging that did not match the asset. GIIA's case study attributes the near-doubling of debt to mark-to-market losses on the accreting swap as rates fell, which blocked refinancing. The bullet maturity in 2015 forced a refinancing exactly when the swap was deeply out of the money. [source: 8]
- The public side did well. Poole and the Tax Foundation both argue the state benefited: it received USD 3.8 billion that funded a 10-year program, bore no bailout cost, and kept the road operating. [source: 5, 6]
- Asset value survived the capital structure. The road was resold for USD 5.725 billion, more than the 2006 price, showing that the failure was of the financing, not the asset. (Inference from verified prices; attribute to the book.)

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 64 (Distress, restructuring and enforcement): Show a prepackaged Chapter 11 used as a sale tool: debt traded to distressed investors, the plan offered sale or restructuring, unsecured creditors were paid in full, and a sale closed about eight months after filing. Use the swap liability (about USD 2.15 billion) to show how hedge close-out amounts rank and how they can dwarf expectations, and contrast with SH 130, where lenders took the keys instead of selling.
- Ch 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels): A brownfield toll road with a long traffic history still failed, because the concessionaire bid high and financed the bid with short bullet debt and an accreting swap. Teach the difference between asset risk and structure risk, how concession bids are capitalized into upfront fees, and why refinancing risk plus traffic ramp-down in a recession is lethal. Pair with the Chicago Skyway (same sponsors, same era) if covered elsewhere.

## Do not state

- That the bankruptcy cost Indiana taxpayers money or returned the road to the state; neither happened.
- A precise original debt and equity split at the 2006 close (for example "USD 3.0 billion of debt and USD 770 million of equity"); only "about USD 3.4 billion" of initial debt is sourced, from one case study.
- Specific traffic shortfall percentages against the original forecast; not verified.
- The exact filing date as 21 or 22 September without qualification: the filing was announced on 22 September 2014 (one IBJ article mistakenly gives 2013).
- Creditor recovery percentages from the IFM sale; not verified.
- The 87 percent versus "nearly 99 percent" creditor support figures conflict between the IBJ and Bond Buyer; cite with attribution or avoid.
- Names of the original bank syndicate members; not verified here.
- Any claim that the lease prevented toll increases; the lease allows scheduled increases.
