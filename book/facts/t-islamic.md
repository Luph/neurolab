# Islamic finance standards and the sukuk market: AAOIFI Shari'ah standards (including draft Shari'ah Standard No. 62 "Sukuk") and global sukuk issuance

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: IIFM Sukuk Report 2026, 15th edition, launched 15 September 2026, data to 31 December 2025; latest verified AAOIFI statement on draft SS 62 dated 28 April 2025)

## Summary

AAOIFI (the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions, based in Bahrain) issues Shari'ah, accounting, auditing, governance and ethics standards for Islamic finance. A group of jurisdictions makes its Shari'ah standards mandatory, including Bahrain, Jordan, Oman, Pakistan, Qatar (and the QIFC), Sudan, Syria, the UAE and Yemen. Others use them as guidance. Malaysia and Saudi Arabia, the two largest sukuk markets, are not on AAOIFI's list. The standards most used in project finance are SS 9 (Ijarah), SS 11 (Istisna' and Parallel Istisna'), SS 12 (Sharikah/Musharaka), SS 13 (Mudaraba), SS 23 (Agency), SS 8 (Murabaha) and SS 17 (Investment Sukuk). SS 17 is listed by AAOIFI as under redrafting.

On 28–30 September 2023, AAOIFI's Shari'ah Board approved for consultation an exposure draft of Shari'ah Standard No. 62, "Sukuk". The draft was released on 6 November 2023, with comments due by 31 December 2023; the deadline was extended to 31 March 2024 and then to 31 July 2024. The draft insists that sukuk holders acquire real, enforceable ownership of the underlying assets, with the right to dispose of them and bear their risk, whatever the legal form of title. It also restricts originator purchase undertakings that fix the exercise price in advance in partnership, mudaraba and investment-agency sukuk. Because these points go to the heart of the asset-based structures that dominate the market, the draft drew heavy industry comment. On 28 April 2025 AAOIFI publicly confirmed that SS 62 "is still in the draft stage", had not been finalised, and was being amended in light of industry comments. No final SS 62 has been verified as of 2026-10-03.

The market is large and growing. The IIFM Sukuk Report 2026 records global sukuk issuance of USD 262.9 billion in 2025 (up 28.2% from USD 205.1 billion in 2024) and global sukuk outstanding of about USD 1.02 trillion at 31 December 2025. Cumulative issuance since 2001 is USD 2.47 trillion, and Malaysia accounts for 43.69% of it.

## Verified facts

**A. AAOIFI and adoption of its standards (as of 2026-10-03; current list at aaoifi.com "Adoption of AAOIFI Standards")**

**B. Draft Shari'ah Standard No. 62 "Sukuk" (status as of 2026-10-03: exposure draft, not final)**

6. AAOIFI's Shari'ah Board approved release of the draft sukuk standard at its 87th meeting, 28–30 September 2023. AAOIFI said the Board consists of twenty scholars from fourteen countries. It said development began in 2019 and involved more than 200 hours of Board work over more than twenty sessions, plus about 250 working hours across 25 meetings and three workshops by the Sukuk Subcommittee and Advisory Committee. AAOIFI planned hearings in Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Malaysia and Pakistan, with comments to be reviewed by the Board in 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
7. The exposure draft bears the release date 6 November 2023. It is issued in Arabic, and the Arabic text prevails over the English translation. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
8. Comment deadlines: originally 31 December 2023, extended to 31 March 2024, then to 31 July 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6, 7]
9. On 28 April 2025, responding to market queries, AAOIFI said the Shari'ah Board was amending the first published draft in light of industry comments. The Chairman of the Board of Trustees, Shaikh Ebrahim bin Khalifa Al Khalifa, said SS 62 was still in the draft stage, had not been finalised, was subject to extensive consultation, and in its final form would be compatible with best practices to meet market needs. [confidence: high, paraphrase] [source: 8]
10. On 2026-10-03, AAOIFI's "Exposure Drafts" page for Shari'ah standards still lists only the "Standard Draft 'Sukuk'" (the November 2023 PDF). No final SS 62 was found on AAOIFI's site. [confidence: medium; absence of evidence, check before stating it is still in draft] [source: 9]
11. Definition in the draft (para 2-1, paraphrased): sukuk are investment securities of equal value representing common shares in ownership of assets (tangible assets, usufructs, rights, debts, cash, or a mix), existing or to be acquired or created. They give holders rights from their share of ownership and obligations to the extent of that share. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
12. The draft (para 2-2) treats an issuance as "securitization" where existing assets move off the originator's balance sheet, the originator no longer controls them in any capacity and has no obligation to repurchase even on dissolution, and holders can dissolve by selling to a third party. [confidence: high, paraphrase of the English translation] [source: 2]
13. The draft lists characteristics distinguishing sukuk from conventional bonds (para 3), including: they are not based on lending against interest; the principal and profit are not automatically an obligation of the originator on subscription; holders share the reward and liability of the underlying assets; and sukuk risk is not confined to the originator's credit risk. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
14. Ownership requirements (para 5-2): transfer of ownership of the underlying assets to holders must be acceptable under Shari'ah and law, or the sale to holders is not permitted. Holders' ownership must give them the ability to dispose of the assets, bear their risk and be entitled to their returns, "whatever is the legal nature of this ownership". It is not permitted to bar holders absolutely, as a body, from recourse to the assets and selling them to a third party. [confidence: high, quoted phrase from the English translation] [source: 2]
15. Where holders buy assets from the originator, the draft requires a price at or close to market value to prevent a sham (*ṣūrī*) purchase. Underlying assets may not be pledged for an interest-bearing loan before or after issuance. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
16. Documentation must not negate holders' ownership, for example by stating in the base prospectus that ownership is not established if local law does not recognise it. The originator's financial statement notes must state that the assets are owned by the holders. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
17. Purchase undertakings (para 6-3-2): an originator's undertaking to buy the underlying assets may not be embedded in the sale contract or the periodic-distribution contract; it may be given in a separate deed. In mudaraba, partnership (musharaka) or investment-agency sukuk, the originator may not undertake to buy at a price fixed at issuance, because that would be a prohibited guarantee of capital. The undertaking may instead be at net asset value, market value, fair value, or a price agreed at the time of purchase. A narrow exception applies to such sukuk whose assets are limited to assets leased to a third party under ijarah muntahia bittamleek. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
18. The draft also says the undertaking may not oblige the originator to pay the exercise price without an actual purchase contract being concluded, including where the purchase cannot be completed for legal or regulatory reasons. A limited exception allows deemed acceptance after a specified period where the originator keeps possession of the assets. The draft requires stipulating that the originator guarantees neither the face value (except for misconduct, negligence or breach) nor a specific return, other than amounts that are a recognised debt. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
19. Istisna' sukuk (para 8-1-1-1-d-3): the draft permits istisna' sukuk to finance an originator's project needing construction, with holders as manufacturer. Proceeds need not be delivered on the day of contract. Delivery to the originator can be in instalments aligned with periodic distributions. The originator may give a separate binding promise to buy the completed item if no buyer is found, provided the price is paid on the spot to avoid *'inah*. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
20. The draft's typology covers commutative-contract sukuk (murabaha, salam, istisna', several forms of ijarah), partnership sukuk (sharikat al-'aqd, sharikat al-milk, mudaraba), wakala sukuk (investment agency and service agency), agricultural sukuk, composite sukuk, convertible sukuk and Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital sukuk. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

**C. The sukuk market (global; data to 31 December 2025; latest source IIFM Sukuk Report 2026, 15th ed.)**

21. Global sukuk issuance (all tenors, all currencies) was USD 262.9 billion in 2025, up 28.2% from USD 205.1 billion in 2024. Long-term issuance was USD 191.4 billion (+31.1%) and short-term USD 71.4 billion (+20.8%). [confidence: high] [source: 10, 11]
22. International sukuk issuance was USD 98.9 billion in 2025 (+50.8%, from USD 65.6 billion in 2024). Domestic issuance was USD 163.9 billion (+17.5%). [confidence: high] [source: 10, 11]
23. By issuer type in 2025: sovereign USD 137.5 billion (+15.6%; about 52.3% of the total), quasi-sovereign USD 52.3 billion (+66.5%), financial institutions USD 39.6 billion (+28.8%), corporate USD 33.5 billion (+39.4%). [confidence: high] [source: 10, 11]
24. Global sukuk outstanding was about USD 1.02 trillion at 31 December 2025 (USD 1.015 trillion in IIFM's press release), up 12.5%. Of this, domestic was USD 734.9 billion (72.4%) and international USD 280.6 billion (27.6%). IIFM expects outstanding to reach around USD 1.2 trillion by 2027. [confidence: high for the figures; the 2027 figure is IIFM's projection] [source: 10, 11]
25. Cumulative global issuance from January 2001 to December 2025 was USD 2.47 trillion (long-term USD 1,651.6 billion, short-term USD 822.1 billion). International cumulative issuance was USD 634.6 billion and domestic USD 1,839.1 billion. Sukuk have been issued in 42 jurisdictions and 30 currencies. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
26. Cumulative issuance by jurisdiction, 2001–2025: Malaysia USD 1,080.77 billion (43.69%), Saudi Arabia USD 379.16 billion (15.33%), Indonesia USD 237.93 billion (9.62%), supranationals including the IsDB USD 186.71 billion (7.55%), UAE USD 147.60 billion (5.97%), Türkiye USD 138.18 billion (5.59%). [confidence: high] [source: 11]
27. In 2025 the IILM issued USD 22.9 billion of short-term USD sukuk across 69 issuances, 32.1% of global short-term volume. 2025 also saw debut international sukuk from Jordan and an expanded international programme from Egypt. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
28. Data-provider differences: a contributed chapter in the IIFM report cites S&P figures of USD 264.8 billion of global issuance in 2025 (from USD 144.7 billion in 2020) and sustainable sukuk issuance of USD 21.5 billion in 2025 (+38%). The same chapter says wakalah, ijarah and murabahah (alone or in hybrids) are used in the vast majority of sukuk. [confidence: medium, a contributed chapter citing S&P; S&P's own publication not read] [source: 11]
29. Saudi Arabian sovereign and quasi-sovereign issuance and Malaysian domestic issuance are the main drivers of volume. IIFM notes that Saudi Arabia "has rapidly closed the gap" with Malaysia, with issuance driven by government projects. [confidence: medium, contributed chapter] [source: 11]

**D. Enforcement: the Dana Gas sukuk (English law; 2017)**

30. Dana Gas PJSC (UAE) raised USD 1 billion through sukuk in 2007. They were restructured in 2013 into certificates with face value USD 850,080,000, listed on the Irish Stock Exchange and due for redemption on 31 October 2017. Proceeds were invested under a UAE-law Amended and Restated Mudarabah Agreement dated 8 May 2013, with Dana Gas as mudarib. The structure included an English-law Purchase Undertaking. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
31. On 12 June 2017 Dana Gas announced it had received legal advice that the sukuk in its existing form was not Shari'a-compliant and therefore unlawful under UAE law. It started proceedings in England and the UAE on 13 June 2017. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
32. In *Dana Gas PJSC v Dana Gas Sukuk Ltd* [2017] EWHC 2928 (Comm) (17 November 2017), Leggatt J tried a preliminary issue. Assuming without deciding that the transaction was invalid under UAE law, he held that the English-law Purchase Undertaking remained valid and enforceable, so the trustee could oblige Dana Gas to pay the Exercise Price. [confidence: high] [source: 12]

## Financing and structure details (parties, tranches, amounts, tenors, guarantees, where verified)

- **Typical parties (per draft SS 62).** The originator (the party seeking finance and receiving proceeds); the issuer (often an SPV, sometimes the originator); the trustee (represents holders, usually an SPV); and the certificate holders. Where assets are registered in the SPV's name, the draft requires the SPV to represent holders and not be owned by the originator. [source: 2]
- **Project-finance relevance.** Istisna' (construction) combined with ijarah (lease of the completed asset) is the classic project structure. Draft SS 62 expressly permits istisna' sukuk to finance a project needing construction, with staged delivery aligned to distributions (fact 19). Musharaka and wakala structures with purchase undertakings are where SS 62's fixed-price restriction bites hardest (fact 17).
- **Co-financing example.** Sadara Chemical Company's SAR 7.5 billion (USD 2 billion) musharaka-based project sukuk (closed 2 April 2013) sat alongside a conventional and ECA debt package of about USD 10.5 billion. See facts/sadara.md for the verified structure.
- **Dana Gas structure.** Mudaraba (UAE law) plus a Purchase Undertaking (English law) plus an Irish listing. Splitting governing law across documents preserved holders' English-law claim even when the UAE-law core was challenged (facts 30–32).

## What went wrong or right, and why (well-established analysis only, attributed to named sources)

- AAOIFI's own framing of SS 62 (press release, 2023) is that it aims to give clear guidance on Shari'ah compliance in the issuance of sukuk, covering a broad range of structures. Its April 2025 statement responded to industry "queries", and AAOIFI then confirmed the draft would be amended to meet varying issuer needs. That confirms the draft's ownership and purchase-undertaking provisions were contested. [source: 5, 8]
- Affendi Rashdi, IIFM Vice-Chairman, writes in his foreword to the 2026 report that sukuk are "fundamentally tied to real economic activity", and that any weakening of this principle risks undermining the instrument's credibility and long-term sustainability. This is the same asset-backing concern behind draft SS 62. [source: 11]
- *Dana Gas* shows the enforcement gap that Shari'ah-compliance risk creates. An originator challenged its own instrument's compliance under onshore law, and holders relied on the English-law purchase undertaking. Leggatt J's ruling, on assumed UAE-law invalidity, protected the English-law claim. [source: 12]

## Teaching angles by chapter (for each listed chapter: the transferable lesson and the angle to take, 2–4 sentences)

- **Chapter 33 (Islamic project finance).** Teach the contract building blocks (istisna' for construction, ijarah for operations, musharaka/mudaraba for equity-like sharing, wakala for agency, murabaha for working capital) through the AAOIFI standard that governs each. Then use draft SS 62 to show the central tension. Lenders want debt-like certainty (a fixed-price purchase undertaking, no real asset risk). The standard-setter insists on genuine ownership and risk-bearing (facts 14–18). Use *Dana Gas* to show that Shari'ah-compliance risk is also a legal and enforcement risk, and why sukuk documents split governing law. Close with Sadara (facts/sadara.md) as the co-financing template and the IIFM data as context on market size. Present SS 62 as a live draft whose final form may differ.

## Do not state (claims commonly repeated but unverified or wrong, and uncertain details)

- Do not say SS 62 has been issued, finalised or made effective, or give an effective date. As of the latest verified AAOIFI statement (28 April 2025) it was a draft being amended, and no final version was found on 2026-10-03.
- Do not describe specific changes AAOIFI has made, or will make, to the draft. None were verified.
- Do not quantify the share of outstanding sukuk "non-compliant" with SS 62 (for example "USD X billion at risk"). Figures circulated in rating-agency and press commentary were not verified here.
- Do not repeat the oft-quoted claim that about 85% of sukuk were not Shari'ah-compliant (attributed to Sheikh Muhammad Taqi Usmani, late 2007), or give details of AAOIFI's February 2008 sukuk resolutions, without checking primary sources. Neither was verified here.
- Do not say Malaysia or Saudi Arabia mandates AAOIFI Shari'ah standards. Neither appears on AAOIFI's adoption list. Malaysia's Islamic finance rulings come from its own national Shariah authority (not verified here in detail).
- Do not mix data sources: IIFM's 2025 issuance is USD 262.9 billion, while S&P (as cited in the IIFM report) is USD 264.8 billion. Always name the source and its definitions.
- Do not give issue dates for GS 17, FAS 33, FAS 34 or the FAS 29 revision. They were not verified.
- Do not translate or quote the Arabic text of SS 62. The English translation is unofficial, and the Arabic prevails.
- Do not state the Dana Gas final settlement terms or the outcome of the UAE proceedings. They were not verified here.
