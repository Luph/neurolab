# Restructuring and insolvency regimes: Chapter 11, UK schemes and restructuring plans, StaRUG, WHOA, French accelerated safeguard, EU Directive 2019/1023

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: TG Jones restructuring plans sanctioned 1 July 2026; Serta damages ruling on remand 7 July 2026)

## Summary

Project finance lenders rely on non-recourse security and step-in rights. When a project fails, the outcome often depends on which restructuring tool is available and how courts apply it.

- **United States.** Chapter 11 of the Bankruptcy Code gives the debtor an automatic stay. A plan needs class votes of two-thirds in amount and a majority in number. It can be imposed on a dissenting class ("cramdown") if it is "fair and equitable," which in practice means the absolute priority rule.
- **United Kingdom.** England offers the long-standing Part 26 scheme of arrangement (75% in value and a majority in number of each class, no cross-class cram-down). Since 26 June 2020 it also offers the Part 26A restructuring plan. A Part 26A plan needs 75% in value of each voting class but allows cross-class cram-down if no dissenting creditor is worse off than in the "relevant alternative" and one in-the-money class approves. Court of Appeal decisions in Adler (January 2024), Thames Water (March/April 2025) and Petrofac (July 2025) have added a requirement in practice that restructuring benefits be fairly allocated. The Petrofac decision set aside a sanctioned plan because new-money providers took a disproportionate share of value.
- **Continental Europe.** The EU Preventive Restructuring Directive (EU) 2019/1023 requires Member States to offer preventive frameworks with:
  - a stay of individual enforcement actions of up to four months initially and 12 months in total;
  - class voting with a threshold of no more than 75%;
  - cross-class cram-down subject to a best-interest-of-creditors test and either an absolute or a relative priority rule.

  Germany implemented it through the StaRUG (in force 1 January 2021), the Netherlands through the WHOA (1 January 2021), and France through Ordinance No. 2021-1193 (in force 1 October 2021). The French ordinance created classes of affected parties and a reformed accelerated safeguard procedure.

A second EU directive harmonizing aspects of insolvency law, Directive (EU) 2026/799, entered into force on 21 April 2026. It covers avoidance actions, pre-packs, directors' filing duties, creditors' committees and asset tracing.

## Verified facts

### United Kingdom (England and Wales)

1. **Part 26A eligibility.** Part 26A of the Companies Act 2006 was inserted by the Corporate Insolvency and Governance Act 2020 with effect from 26 June 2020. It applies where:
   - the company "has encountered, or is likely to encounter, financial difficulties" affecting its ability to carry on business as a going concern (Condition A); and
   - the compromise or arrangement aims to eliminate, reduce, prevent or mitigate those difficulties (Condition B) (s. 901A).

   [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. **Voting threshold.** The court may sanction a Part 26A plan if 75% in value of each class present and voting agrees (s. 901F(1)). Unlike a Part 26 scheme, there is no headcount (numerosity) test. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. **Cross-class cram-down conditions.** Under s. 901G, the court may sanction despite a dissenting class if two conditions are met:
   - Condition A: no member of the dissenting class would be any worse off than in the "relevant alternative," meaning whatever the court considers most likely to occur if the plan is not sanctioned;
   - Condition B: the plan has been agreed by 75% in value of a class that would receive a payment, or have a genuine economic interest, in the relevant alternative.

   Sanction remains discretionary. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. **Part 26 schemes.** Part 26 schemes of arrangement remain available and do not require financial difficulty. A Part 26 scheme requires a majority in number representing 75% in value of each class present and voting, and has no cross-class cram-down. Current text: legislation.gov.uk, Companies Act 2006, Parts 26 and 26A. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. **Adler (Court of Appeal, 23 January 2024).** In *Kington S.à r.l. v Adler / AGPS Bondco plc* [2024] EWCA Civ 24, Snowden LJ gave the lead judgment. The Court of Appeal set aside the High Court's April 2023 sanction of the Adler plan, the first Part 26A cram-down to reach the Court of Appeal. It held that where a dissenting class would be at least partly in the money in the relevant alternative, departures from pari passu treatment must be justified. It also held that Part 26A does not permit confiscating or cancelling out-of-the-money debt or equity for no consideration. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
6. **Thames Water (Court of Appeal, March and April 2025).** In *Kington S.à r.l. and others v Thames Water Utilities Holdings Ltd* [2025] EWCA Civ 475, the Court of Appeal announced on 17 March 2025 that it was dismissing appeals against the sanction of Thames Water's Part 26A plan. The plan provided super-senior interim funding of approximately GBP 3 billion. Reasons followed on 15 April 2025. The appeal by Mr Maynard MP succeeded in part: the releases of Thames Water's directors and advisers were removed from the sanctioned plan. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
7. **Petrofac (Court of Appeal, 1 July 2025).** In *Saipem S.p.A. and others v Petrofac Ltd and Petrofac International (UAE) LLC* [2025] EWCA Civ 821 (Snowden LJ, Zacaroli LJ, Sir Christopher Floyd), the Court of Appeal set aside Marcus Smith J's sanction of 20 May 2025 of two inter-conditional Part 26A plans. The facts and holdings were as follows:
   - **Liabilities.** Secured creditors were owed about USD 909 million. Unsecured liabilities were possibly about USD 3 billion, including disputed joint venture claims of about USD 1.67 billion by Saipem and Samsung.
   - **Value split.** Independent valuers put post-restructuring equity at USD 1.5 to 1.85 billion. Of this, about USD 329 million went to existing creditors: about USD 270 million (an approximately 29% return) to secured creditors and about 1.4% to unsecured creditors. About USD 1 billion went to the providers of USD 350 million of new money.
   - **No-worse-off test.** The Court held that this test compares the value of the rights being compromised with the value of the new rights. Indirect commercial benefits to competitor-creditors from the company's failure are excluded.
   - **Fair allocation.** Cram-down will be exercised only if the plan fairly allocates the benefits preserved or generated by the restructuring. Its proper purpose is to overcome unreasonable hold-outs after a genuine attempt to negotiate with all stakeholders, not to let assenting classes appropriate an inequitable share. A class being out of the money in the relevant alternative does not by itself make a de minimis share fair.

   [confidence: high] [source: 6]
8. **Petrofac at the Supreme Court.** On 13 October 2025 the UK Supreme Court refused Petrofac permission to appeal (UKSC/2025/0140). It cited the prospect of settlement and noted that the general point would be determined in the Waldorf leapfrog appeal. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
9. **Waldorf Production.** The facts are as follows:
   - **First plan.** Waldorf's first Part 26A plan was refused sanction in August 2025 on fair-allocation grounds.
   - **Leapfrog appeal.** The Supreme Court granted permission for a leapfrog appeal on 9 October 2025, with a hearing listed for 24–25 February 2026. The appeal was later withdrawn.
   - **Second plan.** On 5 May 2026 the High Court sanctioned a second plan over HMRC's opposition. The plan facilitated a sale of Waldorf to Harbour Energy for about USD 205 million. The court held there is no jurisdictional bar to cramming down HMRC.
   - **Effect.** As a result, the Supreme Court has not yet ruled on Part 26A.

   [confidence: medium (law-firm reports; judgment on judiciary.uk)] [source: 8, 9]
10. **TG Jones (1 July 2026).** Hildyard J sanctioned two inter-conditional Part 26A plans for TG Jones High Street Ltd, with reasons cautioning against using Part 26A to impose losses on dissenting creditors. [confidence: medium (single trade-press source)] [source: 10]
11. **Practice Statement.** An updated Practice Statement for schemes and Part 26A plans was issued on 18 September 2025, effective 1 January 2026. It emphasizes early identification of jurisdictional and class issues and better case management. [confidence: medium] [source: 11]

### United States

12. **Chapter 11 mechanics (Bankruptcy Code, 11 U.S.C.).** The main provisions are:
    - **Automatic stay (§362).** Filing triggers a stay of creditor enforcement.
    - **Class acceptance (§1126(c)).** A class accepts if holders of at least two-thirds in amount and more than one-half in number of the allowed claims voting accept.
    - **Confirmation (§1129(a)).** Confirmation requires, among other things, at least one accepting impaired class (§1129(a)(10)) and that each dissenting creditor receives at least its liquidation value (the "best interests" test, §1129(a)(7)).
    - **Cramdown (§1129(b)).** Cramdown over a dissenting class requires that the plan not "discriminate unfairly" and be "fair and equitable." For unsecured classes this embodies the absolute priority rule.

    Current text: uscode.house.gov, Title 11. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
13. **Purdue Pharma (Supreme Court, 27 June 2024).** In *Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, decided 5–4, the Court held that the Bankruptcy Code does not authorize a Chapter 11 plan to release and enjoin claims against non-debtors without the consent of affected claimants. The majority described the holding as narrow and did not call consensual third-party releases into question. [confidence: high] [source: 13]
14. **Serta Simmons (Fifth Circuit, 31 December 2024).** The Fifth Circuit held that Serta's June 2020 "uptier" exchange with a subset of lenders was not a permitted "open market purchase" under the credit agreement. An open market purchase must occur on a market generally open to participation, here the secondary syndicated loan market. The court reinstated the excluded lenders' breach-of-contract claims and rejected equitable mootness. On remand, on 7 July 2026, the Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas awarded the excluded lenders approximately USD 261.13 million plus prejudgment interest at 9% per year, computed on a "but-for" pro rata basis. [confidence: high for 2024 ruling; medium for remand amount (single law-firm source)] [source: 14, 15]

### Germany: StaRUG

15. **StaRUG.** The Unternehmensstabilisierungs- und -restrukturierungsgesetz entered into force on 1 January 2021. It is a court-supervised preventive restructuring framework for debtors facing imminent illiquidity. Its main rules are:
    - **Approval.** A restructuring plan requires 75% of voting rights in each group, measured against the total in the group, not only those voting.
    - **Cram-down.** A dissenting group can be crammed down if:
      - it is not worse off than in the next-best alternative;
      - it participates appropriately in the value distributed;
      - the majority of groups approve.

    [confidence: high] [source: 16, 17]
16. **StaRUG used against shareholders.** Two cases show how far the StaRUG can reach equity:
    - **Leoni AG (2023).** A plan confirmed by the Local Court of Nuremberg in 2023 reduced capital to zero and issued new shares solely to an investor vehicle, wiping out existing shareholders. The Federal Constitutional Court did not accept shareholders' constitutional complaints.
    - **Varta AG (December 2024).** The Local Court of Stuttgart confirmed a similar plan; the Regional Court of Stuttgart rejected shareholder remedies in January 2025.

    [confidence: medium] [source: 18]

### Netherlands: WHOA

17. **WHOA.** The Wet homologatie onderhands akkoord was approved by the Senate on 6 October 2020 and entered into force on 1 January 2021. It allows a debtor, or in some cases an appointed restructuring expert, to propose a plan outside formal insolvency:
    - **Class approval.** A class accepts with two-thirds in value of those voting, with no headcount test.
    - **Cram-down.** The court can confirm the plan over dissenting classes.
    - **Proceedings.** Proceedings can be public, with EU recognition, or private.

    [confidence: high] [source: 19, 20]

### France: accelerated safeguard

18. **Ordinance 2021-1193.** Ordinance No. 2021-1193 of 15 September 2021, in force 1 October 2021, transposed Directive 2019/1023. It made four main changes:
    - it replaced creditors' committees with "classes of affected parties" (classes de parties affectées);
    - it introduced cross-class cram-down;
    - it merged accelerated financial safeguard into a single accelerated safeguard (sauvegarde accélérée);
    - it set class approval at a two-thirds majority of the votes cast.

    [confidence: high] [source: 21, 22]
19. **Accelerated safeguard procedure.** The procedure is available only to a debtor already in conciliation that has a draft plan likely to gain sufficient support. The plan must be adopted within two months of opening, extendable once, with a total of no more than four months (Code de commerce art. L. 628-8). Current text: Légifrance, Code de commerce Book VI. [confidence: high] [source: 22, 23]

### European Union

20. **Directive 2019/1023 stay and votes.** Directive (EU) 2019/1023 of 20 June 2019 (OJ L 172, 26.6.2019) sets these limits:
    - **Stay.** The initial stay of individual enforcement actions is limited to no more than four months (Art. 6(6)). The total, including extensions, may not exceed 12 months (Art. 6(8)).
    - **Voting.** The required majority in each class may not exceed 75% of the amount of claims or interests (Art. 9(6)).

    [confidence: high] [source: 24]
21. **Directive 2019/1023 cram-down and transposition.** Cross-class cram-down (Art. 11(1)) requires, among other things:
    - approval by a majority of voting classes including a secured or senior class or, failing that, by at least one in-the-money class other than equity;
    - that dissenting classes be treated at least as favorably as any other class of the same rank and more favorably than any junior class;
    - that no class receives more than its full claim.

    Art. 11(2) permits an absolute priority rule alternative instead. Transposition was due by 17 July 2021 (Art. 34), with a one-year extension available on notification by 17 January 2021. [confidence: high] [source: 24]
22. **Directive (EU) 2026/799 (insolvency harmonization).** The directive was adopted by the European Parliament on 10 March 2026 and approved by the Council on 30 March 2026. It was published in the Official Journal on 1 April 2026 and entered into force on 21 April 2026. Member States have two years and nine months to transpose it. It covers five areas:
    - avoidance actions;
    - pre-pack proceedings, with automatic transfer of essential executory contracts to the purchaser;
    - a directors' duty to file within three months of becoming aware of insolvency;
    - creditors' committees;
    - insolvency practitioners' access to bank account registers.

    [confidence: high] [source: 25, 26]

## Financing and structure details

The Petrofac figures in item 7 are the best documented recent example of how value is split in a restructuring plan:

- about USD 270 million to secured creditors, against claims of about USD 909 million;
- about 1.4% to unsecured creditors;
- about USD 1 billion of equity to providers of USD 350 million of new money.

The Thames Water plan centered on super-senior interim liquidity of approximately GBP 3 billion from creditors. [source: 4, 6]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Fair allocation as the new battleground (England).** The Court of Appeal in Petrofac, following Adler and Thames Water, stated that cram-down is not a tool for assenting classes to take an inequitable share. Where new money captures a disproportionate share of value, the plan is at risk unless there has been a genuine attempt to negotiate with all stakeholders. Commentators at Kirkland & Ellis, Latham & Watkins and Macfarlanes read this as shifting leverage toward out-of-the-money but strategic creditors. [source: 6, 27]
- **Contract wording controls liability management (US).** The Serta rulings show that the drafting of "open market purchase" and pro rata sharing clauses decides whether non-pro-rata uptiers are allowed, and that excluded lenders can recover damages. [source: 14, 15]
- **Equity is no longer safe in Germany.** Leoni and Varta show the StaRUG can eliminate out-of-the-money shareholders, implementing the Directive's aim of preventing equity hold-outs. [source: 18]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 64 (Distress, restructuring and enforcement).** Map each tool on four dimensions:
  - **Entry test.** Financial difficulty (UK Part 26A), imminent illiquidity (StaRUG), insolvency likely (WHOA), or no test at all (Chapter 11 and Part 26 schemes).
  - **Voting.** 75% value (UK), two-thirds value plus majority in number (US), two-thirds of votes cast (WHOA and France), 75% of the group (StaRUG).
  - **Cram-down standard.** Absolute versus relative priority, and no-worse-off tests.
  - **Stay protection.**

  Then use Petrofac's numbers to run an exercise on fair allocation of restructuring surplus to new money. Explain why project lenders still prefer share-pledge enforcement and step-in under direct agreements to a court process, and how a Part 26A plan or Chapter 11 can override those remedies. For Case T, contrast a creditor-led recapitalization with the risk that a dissenting class (an EPC contractor with large claims, as in Petrofac) blocks or delays cram-down.
