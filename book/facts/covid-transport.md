# COVID-19 and transport concessions: force majeure, relief and renegotiation

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: the end of the GB rail emergency-agreement period, with the last operators moving to National Rail Contracts or public ownership by October 2023; core coverage is March 2020 to 2022)

## Summary

The COVID-19 pandemic hit demand-risk transport concessions harder than almost any other asset class. Ridership and traffic collapsed within weeks of lockdowns in March 2020 while fixed costs and debt service continued. Governments chose between letting contractual risk allocation play out (force majeure, relief events, material adverse government action, termination) and intervening by statute or by negotiation. This sheet documents three well-sourced responses. First, Great Britain's rail franchises: on 23 March 2020 the Department for Transport (DfT) offered Emergency Measures Agreements (EMAs) that suspended the franchise financial mechanisms and moved all revenue and cost risk to government for a management fee capped at 2% of the pre-pandemic cost base. These were followed from 20 September 2020 by Emergency Recovery Measures Agreements (ERMAs) with a 1.5% cap, which were designed to end franchising altogether. Second, UK PFI/PPP availability contracts: HM Treasury's guidance of 2 April 2020 stated that the government did not regard COVID-19 as force majeure, and asked authorities to keep paying unitary charges and to suspend performance deductions where performance suffered despite best efforts. Third, Spain: Royal Decree-law 8/2020 of 17 March 2020 (Article 34.4) gave works and services concessionaires a statutory right to economic rebalancing, by extending the term by up to 15% or modifying economic clauses. That right applied only where the contracting authority accepted that performance had become impossible. Together these show the toolkit: risk transfer to the state for a fee, payment continuity with suspended deductions, and statutory rebalancing. In each case the remedy depended on contract wording and on government policy, not on force majeure alone.

## Verified facts

1. On 23 March 2020 the Secretary of State for Transport (Grant Shapps) announced that train operators on DfT-let franchises could temporarily move onto Emergency Measures Agreements. The EMAs suspended the normal financial mechanisms of the franchise agreements and transferred all revenue and cost risk to the government, initially for 6 months, with options to extend or cancel early. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Under the EMAs, operators ran day-to-day services for a "small, pre-determined management fee", set at a maximum of 2% of the franchise's pre-COVID-19 cost base and intended to incentivise reliability, punctuality and other targets. The DfT's Operator of Last Resort stood ready if an operator declined an EMA. The statement said that allowing operators to enter insolvency would cause more disruption and higher costs to the taxpayer. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. At the time of the 23 March 2020 announcement, passenger numbers had fallen by up to 70% and total ticket sales were down by two-thirds from the equivalent date in 2019. Advance tickets became refundable without charge. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. On 21 September 2020 the government announced Emergency Recovery Measures Agreements, stating that they had come into force the previous day. They ran for up to 18 months, contained provisions to bring the current franchises to an end when they expired, set tougher performance targets, and capped management fees at 1.5% of the franchise's pre-pandemic cost base. (The NAO's chart dates the ERMAs to 22 September 2020.) [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
5. The NAO (26 April 2021) put total government support to franchised operators between March and September 2020 at GBP 4.1 billion, with a further GBP 4 billion to GBP 5 billion expected for the rest of 2020-21 depending on passenger numbers. Net government funding in 2019-20 included GBP 300 million of COVID-19 support provided in March 2020. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
6. The NAO described the change as transferring substantially all risk and reward from operators to the DfT, making the government directly exposed to operators' income and expenditure. It said that the extent of risk transfer made operator financial failure extremely unlikely. Non-franchised (open access) operators were not eligible for emergency support. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. After the ERMAs, the DfT replaced franchises with directly awarded National Rail Contracts (NRCs) or public ownership. Examples include Southeastern, which moved to public ownership on 17 October 2021, and Great Western, which moved to an NRC on 26 June 2022. CrossCountry's emergency agreement term was extended to October 2023. The DfT published period-by-period operational support payments from 1 March 2020 to 31 March 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
8. HM Treasury guidance note "Supporting vital service provision in PFI/PF2 (and related) contracts during the COVID-19 emergency" (2 April 2020) said that the COVID-19 emergency "is not, and is not to be, regarded as an event of force majeure" and that the government did not regard it as force majeure as a matter of contract. It asked contracting authorities to maintain unitary charge payments, using the net unitary charge over the previous three months as a performance baseline. Where performance was affected despite best efforts, authorities should apply a temporary moratorium on payment-mechanism deductions and points and record the arrangements in bespoke contract documents. Temporarily closed assets had to be kept ready for immediate return to use, and any increased costs were to be shown on an open-book basis. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
9. That PFI guidance was reviewed on 24 June 2020. From 1 July 2020, transition from relief was governed by the Cabinet Office's Procurement Policy Note 04/20, which supplemented PPN 02/20 (supplier relief), and no separate PFI transition guidance was issued. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
10. The Cabinet Office published "Guidance on responsible contractual behaviour in the performance and enforcement of contracts impacted by the COVID-19 emergency" on 7 May 2020, with an update on 30 June 2020. It urged parties to act responsibly and fairly, support the COVID-19 response and protect jobs and the economy. The guidance is non-statutory. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
11. Spain, Royal Decree-law 8/2020 of 17 March 2020, Article 34.4: for works and services concession contracts in force with public sector entities, the situation created by COVID-19 and the measures taken by the state, regions or local authorities entitle the concessionaire to restoration of the contract's economic balance. This is done, as appropriate, by extending the initial term by up to 15% or by modifying the economic clauses. Compensation covers lost revenue and increased costs, including additional wage costs actually paid, during the COVID-19 situation, on request and with reliable proof. It applies only where the contracting body, at the contractor's request, has found that the contract could not be performed because of that situation, and only to the affected part of the contract. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

## Financing and structure details

- GB rail franchises were not project-financed concessions. They were operating contracts with parent-company support and premium or subsidy profiles. The EMA/ERMA design is still the clearest worked example of converting a revenue-risk contract into a cost-plus management contract: revenue went to the government, costs were reimbursed, and the operator earned a capped fee (2% EMA, 1.5% ERMA) with performance elements. [source: 1, 2, 3]
- UK PFI/PPP availability contracts: payment continuity (unitary charge maintained at the trailing three-month net level) together with a deduction moratorium protected SPV debt service without any change to the risk allocation in the contract. [source: 5]
- Spain RDL 8/2020: rebalancing by term extension (capped at 15%) or by changing economic clauses, conditional on a finding of impossibility of performance. Term extension is the classic tool for compensating a toll concession without immediate cash from the budget. [source: 7]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- NAO (2021): the emergency agreements kept services running and made operator failure extremely unlikely, but they moved the government's exposure from deferred (at franchise end) to immediate, and they marked the end of the established commercial model of franchising. [source: 3]
- DfT (23 March 2020): the government judged that allowing operators to enter insolvency would cause more disruption and cost more than paying a capped fee. This is a public-sector application of the going-concern logic in distressed situations. [source: 1]
- HM Treasury (2 April 2020): by declaring that COVID-19 was not force majeure under PFI contracts, while keeping payments flowing and suspending deductions, government avoided both contractual termination rights and SPV payment defaults. The cost was a period of reduced performance standards. [source: 5]
- Spain (RDL 8/2020): the statutory rebalancing right was limited to contracts whose performance had become impossible, so concessions that kept operating at lower demand did not obviously fall within it. This shows how statutory relief can be narrower than the economic harm. This reading follows the text. Litigation and administrative practice were not verified here.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 64 (Distress, restructuring and enforcement): use the GB rail EMAs as the state-as-stabiliser case. Faced with sector-wide revenue collapse, the counterparty government chose a negotiated risk transfer over insolvency and Operator of Last Resort step-ins, priced as a capped fee. Contrast it with the UK PFI approach (payments maintained, deductions suspended) to show two ways a public counterparty can keep project companies solvent without formal restructuring, and what each costs the public purse.
- Chapter 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels): use Spain's Article 34.4 to teach the economic-equilibrium (rebalancing) principle of civil-law concessions and its usual remedies, term extension and tariff or economic-clause changes. Stress the drafting lesson that the trigger, here impossibility of performance, decides who gets relief. Toll concessions that stayed open with low traffic face a different legal path from closed assets. Pair it with Case T's force majeure, relief event and material adverse government action clauses.
- Chapter 80 (Rail, urban transit, airports and ports): use the EMA (2%) to ERMA (1.5%) to National Rail Contract sequence to show how a demand shock can permanently change the contracting model, from revenue-risk franchise to gross-cost management contract. Discuss what a lender to a demand-risk rail, airport or port concession should take from it: pandemic-type risk tends to be resolved by government policy as much as by contract, so diligence should cover the grantor's track record and fiscal capacity, not just the force majeure clause.

## Do not state

- Do not state that COVID-19 was treated as force majeure across UK concessions or PPPs. UK government guidance for PFI said the opposite. Outcomes under private contracts depend on their wording.
- Do not give specific rebalancing amounts or term extensions for named Spanish toll roads or airports, or claim that Spanish toll concessionaires generally received compensation under RDL 8/2020. This was not verified, and the impossibility condition limits the provision.
- Do not state details of India's Ministry of Finance force majeure Office Memorandum (reported as 13 May 2020, with extensions of 3 to 6 months for PPP concessions) or the NHAI toll suspension dates and concession extensions. These are commonly cited but were not verified here.
- Do not state Brazil ANAC/ANTT COVID rebalancing decisions or amounts, French concession measures (Ordonnance 2020-319), or concession extensions for airports in Greece, Chile, Peru or elsewhere. None were verified here.
- Do not state the final total of GB rail emergency support across 2020-2024 as a single figure. Only the NAO's March to September 2020 figure (GBP 4.1 billion) and its GBP 4 billion to GBP 5 billion estimate for the rest of 2020-21 are verified.
- Do not state that the EMA financial provisions were backdated to 1 March 2020 as a high-confidence fact. It appears in secondary summaries and is consistent with DfT payment data starting 1 March 2020, but the EMA text was not checked.
- Do not quote Grant Shapps beyond the official statement text.
