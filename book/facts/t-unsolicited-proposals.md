# Unsolicited PPP proposals: World Bank 2017 Policy Guidelines, Swiss challenge, bonus and best-and-final-offer systems, country examples

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest source used: World Bank, "USP II Alternate Approaches" note on open-book approaches to USP procurement, June 2023; country frameworks are described as of the source dates given, mostly 2007–2018)

## Summary

An unsolicited proposal (USP) is a proposal by a private party to develop an infrastructure project that the government has not asked for. In the World Bank's framing, the private proponent "reaches out to the government" without an explicit request. Governments allow USPs because the private sector can bring project ideas, technical skill and finance. They worry that USPs bypass planning and fiscal controls, attract little competition and invite corruption. The World Bank Group's *Policy Guidelines for Managing Unsolicited Proposals in Infrastructure Projects* (2017, three volumes, PPIAF-funded) recommend treating USPs as an exception to publicly originated projects. They should be held to the same or higher standards as public PPPs and, wherever possible, tendered competitively and transparently. Countries that allow USPs usually run two stages. The first is government screening and approval. The second is a competitive tender in which the original proponent gets some reward. The three classic reward mechanisms are:
- a **bonus system** (Chile, Korea, Argentina, Colombia), which adds points or a price margin to the proponent's bid;
- a **Swiss challenge** (Philippines, Guam, India, Italy, Taiwan), which gives the proponent the right to match a better bid;
- a **best-and-final-offer (BAFO) system** (South Africa, Argentina), in which the proponent goes straight to the final round.

The alternative is direct compensation of the proponent's development costs, with full competition. Evidence on how well the mechanisms work is thin and mostly survey-based. Chilean officials found that winning margins were usually larger than the bonus. Practitioners in some countries say Swiss challenges deter challengers. The World Bank's 2018 benchmarking found that 57 percent of 135 economies explicitly allowed USPs, 3 percent prohibited them, and 10 percent did not regulate them although they happened in practice.

## Verified facts

**Definitions and the World Bank Group position (global; current guidance)**

1. The World Bank defines a USP as a privately initiated process in which a private-sector entity proposes to develop an infrastructure project "without an explicit request from the government". It describes USPs as an alternative that "should not constitute a 'by default' procurement option, but rather an exception". [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The *Policy Guidelines for Managing Unsolicited Proposals in Infrastructure Projects* are published by the World Bank in three volumes (Volume I, Volume II and Volume III). The World Bank's 2023 note cites them as "World Bank 2017". The procurement stage ("Stage IV: Procurement") is Volume I, chapter 7. Volume II contains guidelines for developing a USP policy, including a fair-market-pricing section (2.4) and Box 18 (5.3.3). [confidence: high for existence and structure; medium for the 2017 date, which comes from the 2023 note's citation and not from the Guidelines themselves, which could not be retrieved] [source: 1]
3. According to the 2023 World Bank note, the Guidelines recommend the following:
   - USPs, when accepted, should be "managed and used with caution as an exception to publicly originated projects";
   - USP-specific processes, regulations and institutions should ensure that privately initiated PPPs meet "equal or higher standards" than publicly initiated PPPs;
   - USP policy should integrate with PPP policy, public investment requirements, fiscal management, national infrastructure planning, environmental and social requirements, and climate goals;
   - USPs should be tendered transparently and competitively whenever possible. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The 2023 note says that direct negotiation with the USP proponent gives the least competitive pressure and is the most likely to enable corrupt and nepotistic practices, or the perception of them. It is therefore "typically not the default option". The note treats an open-book approach as one way to bring price discipline to a direct negotiation. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. PPIAF's 2014 global study (*Unsolicited Proposals – An Exception to Public Initiation of Infrastructure PPPs*, August 2014) recommends that a USP framework cover four things:
   - reimbursement and protection of intellectual property;
   - procedures for introducing competition and rewards (Swiss challenge, bonus system, direct compensation, automatic short-listing and so on);
   - eligibility;
   - the types of government support, if any. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

**The mechanisms (as defined in World Bank/PPIAF sources)**

6. PPIAF (2014) defines the four common follow-up mechanisms as follows:
   - **Swiss challenge:** an open tender in which the USP proponent has the right to match the winning bid in order to win the contract.
   - **Bonus system:** an open tender in which the proponent receives bonus points, "generally five to 10 percent of the points".
   - **Automatic short-listing:** in a multi-stage tender, the proponent skips the preliminary stages and is invited straight to the last stage, where the remaining bidders submit best and final offers.
   - **Regular procurement:** the proponent competes on equal terms. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
7. Hodges and Dellacha (PPIAF Gridlines No. 19, March 2007) give a bonus-system example. A toll-road proponent has a 10 percent bonus, and the lowest rival bid is USD 0.19 per km. The proponent would win with a bid of USD 0.20 per km, because its bid is within 10 percent of the lowest. The bonus may apply to the technical score (Korea) or the financial score (Argentina, Chile). [confidence: high] [source: 3]
8. Hodges and Dellacha (2007) describe the Swiss challenge as "most common in the Philippines" and also used in Guam, India, Italy and Taiwan. In the Philippines and Guam the original proponent has 30 days to match a better approved offer. In India the government sets the time frame case by case. If the proponent matches in the Philippines, the project is immediately awarded to it. In Guam the project is then awarded on technical merit. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
9. Hodges and Dellacha (2007) describe the BAFO system in two countries:
   - In **South Africa**, the two most advantageous bids go to a final round, and the original proponent is admitted to that round even if it is not one of the two.
   - In **Argentina**, the proponent wins outright if its offer is within 5 percent of the best offer. If the gap is more than 5 percent but less than 20 percent, the two bidders submit best and final offers in a second round with no bonus. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
10. Hodges and Dellacha (2007, Table 1) report USP volumes for four countries:

    | Country | Period | Presented | Accepted | Under review | Rejected | Tendered or completed |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | Chile | 1995–March 2006 | 200+ | 26 | 38 | 140+ | 12 |
    | Korea | July 1999–April 2006 | 141 | 101 | 7 | 33 | 65 |
    | South Africa | 1999–2006 | 4 | 0 | 3 | 1 | 0 |
    | Taiwan, China | March 2002–May 2006 | 193 | 29 | 22 | 142 | 29 |

    [confidence: high] [source: 3]
11. Hodges and Dellacha (2007, Table 4) report approval and bidding timelines:
    - Chile: 45 days for preliminary approval, 12 months for final approval, 12 months to call open tenders, about 2–4 months for challenges; total 27.5–29.5 months.
    - Korea: 15 days, then 4 months; total 6.5–8.5+ months.
    - Philippines: 8+ months in total, including a 2-month challenge period and 1 month to countermatch.
    - South Africa: 17 months in total.
    - Chile: a rejected concept may be reused by the government in a public bid after three years. [confidence: high] [source: 3]

**Country examples**

12. **Philippines (BOT Law, Republic Act No. 6957 as amended, as described by PPIAF in 2014).** USPs may be accepted only if all four conditions hold:
    - the project involves a new concept or technology, or is not on the priority project lists;
    - no direct government guarantee, subsidy or equity is required;
    - the agency has published an invitation for comparative proposals for three consecutive weeks and received none for 60 working days;
    - if a lower-priced proposal is submitted, the original proponent may match it within 30 working days.

    PPIAF also notes that Philippine USP submissions must include a full draft contract. [confidence: high for the framework as described in 2014; check whether the 2023 PPP Code has replaced it, see Do not state] [source: 2]
13. **Philippines, NLEX–SLEX connector.** PPIAF (2014) reports that the USP proponent preferred the regulated unsolicited route, with an eventual Swiss challenge, to a contractual option of extending an existing alignment. The reason was that the USP route gave a sound legal basis for the award. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
14. **Colombia (Law 1508 of 2012 and regulations, as described by PPIAF in 2014).** USPs that need government contributions are tendered competitively after a feasibility study. The original proponent gets a bonus of 3–10 percent on its bid score, and is compensated for preparation costs if it loses. For USPs needing no public funds, the government invites competing proposals, and the original proponent may match a superior one. PPIAF reports that Colombia was "overwhelmed by the sheer quantity of proposals" once USPs were allowed. [confidence: high] [source: 2] See colombia-4g.md for Law 1508 generally.
15. **South Africa.** USPs are governed by National Treasury Practice Note No. 11 of 2008/2009 ("Unsolicited Proposals"). PPIAF (2014) reports two features:
    - the framework requires rejection of a USP that relates to known institutional requirements;
    - the proponent is reimbursed for certain audited costs, and for the purchase of any intellectual property rights, before the project is procured. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
16. **Chile.**
    - The Concessions Law lets any party propose a project. If the Ministry of Public Works (MOP) approves it, the project is auctioned and the proponent gets a bid bonus. Engel, Fischer and Galetovic (NBER Working Paper 8803, 2002) model a toll bonus *b*: a proponent bidding toll *p* wins if *p/(1+b)* is below the other bids, but may charge *p*.
    - They cite the 1999 Chicureo road (about USD 170 million), proposed by a landowner group that would benefit from it.
    - They argue that the bonus rests on a poor analogy with patents and can reduce welfare. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
17. **Chile, how well the bonus works.** PPIAF (2014) reports that Chilean authorities found the gap between winner and runner-up "almost always bigger than the bonus points", suggesting the bonus does not change outcomes. It also reports that original proponents won fewer than half of competitively procured USP tenders with more than one bidder. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

**Global incidence and benchmarking (World Bank, Procuring Infrastructure PPPs 2018; data as of 2017)**

18. Of the 135 economies assessed, 57 percent explicitly allowed USPs and 3 percent prohibited them. Ten percent did not regulate USPs although USPs occurred in practice; this share was highest in East Asia (20 percent). [confidence: high] [source: 5]
19. Where USPs were competitively tendered:
    - in 48 percent of economies, bidders other than the proponent had a shorter minimum bid period than for publicly originated PPPs;
    - in another 34 percent, no minimum was defined.

    The report's good-practice benchmark is at least 90 days for other bidders. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
20. The report says that, according to the PPI Database, approximately 4 percent of infrastructure projects in the low- and middle-income economies it covers are privately initiated. Average USP scores were highest in OECD high-income economies (72), then South Asia (68) and Latin America and the Caribbean (66), and lowest in East Asia and Pacific (46). Scores in Latin America ranged from 13 to 100. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
21. PPIAF (2014) adds evidence from the PPI Database:
    - 32 projects in its study countries reached financial close as USPs, and 182 after direct negotiation, between 2000 and 2012;
    - by sector, about 17 percent of energy PPPs and 16 percent of water and sewerage PPPs were initiated as USPs, against 10 percent of transport PPPs;
    - transport was 14 percent of all USPs.

    PPIAF notes that only Chile and Peru, among its study countries, document USP numbers well. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

## Financing and structure details

Not a single transaction. The mechanisms affect the bid stage, but they shape financing in three ways.
- **Bid bonds and locked financing.** Hodges and Dellacha (2007) note that delays can lock up a proponent's financing, guarantees and bid bonds. [source: 3]
- **Development cost reimbursement.** It is paid by the government, the winning bidder, or both, and is often built into the tariff. This puts challengers at a cost disadvantage, because the reimbursement adds to their financed costs. [source: 3]
- **Timing.** Challengers need time to arrange financing. A 60-day challenge window (the Philippines, per Hodges and Dellacha) may be too short for due diligence and a financed bid. [source: 3]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Competition is the core problem.** The World Bank (2023) and PPIAF (2014) both say that directly negotiated USPs give the weakest price discipline and the greatest corruption risk. Every mechanism is a trade-off between rewarding initiative and preserving competition. [source: 1, 2]
- **Mechanism effects (PPIAF 2014, Hodges and Dellacha 2007).**
  - Large bonuses discourage challengers; small ones discourage proposals.
  - Challengers are reluctant to spend on bids that can be matched.
  - Challengers usually get much less preparation time than the proponent.
  - Some practitioners, for example in Tanzania, call the mechanisms "artificial" and distorting.
  - Some favor full competition plus direct compensation of the proponent's costs. [source: 2, 3]
- **Academic critique.** Engel, Fischer and Galetovic argue that a proponent bonus cannot be justified the way patents are, because a landowner-proponent already captures the project's benefits. In some cases the bonus simply raises tolls. [source: 4]
- **Capacity.** PPIAF reports governments being overwhelmed by proposal volumes, as in Colombia. Processing delays then erode private interest. [source: 2]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 57 (public-sector case for PPPs).** Present USPs as a governance problem rather than a procurement detail. Their appeal is private ideas and preparation money. Their risk is projects that bypass planning, value-for-money tests and fiscal controls. Use the World Bank's 2017 position (an exception, held to "equal or higher standards") and the 2018 benchmarking statistics to show how widely countries diverge. Ask students how a PPP unit should screen a USP for fit with the national pipeline before granting proponent status.
- **Chapter 58 (procuring and designing PPPs), cross-reference.** Work the bonus arithmetic (the USD 0.19 versus 0.20 per km example) and the Argentina 5 percent / 20 percent BAFO rule. Contrast these with the Swiss challenge right to match. Then discuss information disclosure and challenge periods as the hidden levers that decide whether competition is real.

## Do not state

- Do not give a publication month, author list or page references for the 2017 Guidelines. They could not be retrieved directly. The ppp.worldbank.org and ppiaf.org hosts returned 403. The 2017 date comes from the World Bank's 2023 citation.
- Do not present country rules (Philippines, Colombia, South Africa, Chile, Korea, Argentina, India) as current law. They are described as of 2007–2018 sources.
  - The Philippines enacted a new PPP Code in 2023 (Republic Act No. 11966, as understood), which may have changed the BOT Law USP rules. This was not verified here.
  - Peru's private-initiative ("iniciativas privadas") regime has been amended several times. Its current terms were not verified.
- Do not state the year of the BOT Law amendment (commonly given as 1994, RA 7718) as verified; only RA 6957 was named in the source.
- Do not state the percentages in PPIAF Figure 20 (use of each mechanism by survey respondents). The extracted chart labels are ambiguous.
- Do not claim USPs systematically produce worse value for money. The cited sources give survey and case evidence, not a causal estimate.
- Do not name the Kenya standard gauge railway as a USP. PPIAF cites it only as an apparent example of bilateral engagement without competition.
