# Airport and port revenue models: Heathrow's regulated RAB price control (CAA H7/H8) and the landlord-port concession model (World Bank Port Reform Toolkit)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: CAA decision CAP3308 setting a 2027 holding price cap for Heathrow, September 2026; CAA H8 Initial Proposals CAP3232, March 2026; World Bank Port Reform Toolkit, 3rd edition, July 2025)

## Summary

Heathrow Airport Limited (HAL) is economically regulated by the UK Civil Aviation Authority (CAA) under the Civil Aviation Act 2012, through a licence that caps the airport charge per passenger (the "maximum yield"). The CAA uses a "single till" building-block method. It projects efficient operating costs, regulatory depreciation and an allowed return on the Regulated Asset Base (RAB). It deducts projected commercial revenues (retail, car parking and similar), other regulated charges and cargo revenue. It then divides the result by forecast passengers. The H7 control (January 2022 to December 2026) was set in March 2023 after a pandemic-era process. It made a £300 million covid-related RAB adjustment, set a vanilla WACC point estimate of 3.18% (on a RAB indexed to RPI), and introduced a traffic risk-sharing (TRS) mechanism. Allowed charges were £30.19 per passenger (nominal) in 2022, falling to about £25 nominal in 2024–26 (£21.03 in 2020 CPI prices). The CMA largely upheld the decision in October 2023, finding a few errors (a minor cost-of-debt point, the passenger "shock factor", and the mechanistic AK-factor clawback) that it remitted. For H8 (2027–2031), the CAA's March 2026 initial proposals raised the CPIH-real vanilla WACC to 4.87% (from an H7 equivalent of 4.06%), because the cost of debt rose. They proposed a midpoint average charge of about £28.80 per passenger. Because the final H8 decision is due in April 2027, the CAA set a holding cap for 2027 of £28.658 (2027 prices). Ports are usually organised differently. The World Bank's Port Reform Toolkit (3rd edition, 2025) calls the landlord port the most common model. The public port authority owns the land and provides common-user infrastructure (quay walls, breakwaters, access). Private terminal operators invest in superstructure and equipment and run the terminals under concessions, typically 10–30 years and up to 95. The authority recovers its costs from port dues on ships and from concession fees, which are often the bidding parameter. The contrast for lenders: Heathrow's debt is serviced from a regulated, RAB-based yield with traffic-risk sharing, while a terminal concessionaire bears market (volume and tariff) risk and pays fees to the landlord.

## Verified facts

### A. Heathrow: regulatory framework (UK; current rules in HAL's licence under CAA12 and CAA price-control documents at caa.co.uk)

1. Method: the CAA estimates HAL's total efficient costs, nets off commercial revenues ("single till"), other regulated charges and cargo revenue to get the revenue to be recovered from airport charges, then divides by forecast passengers to get the maximum yield per passenger. Efficient costs comprise opex, regulatory depreciation and the return on the RAB. The RAB grows with investment and falls with regulatory depreciation. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 5]
2. H7 runs from January 2022 to December 2026. The CAA's Final Decision was published on 9 March 2023 (CAP2524). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
3. In April 2021 the CAA made a targeted £300 million adjustment to HAL's RAB because of covid-19 (HAL had sought a much larger one). This was retained in the Final Decision. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. H7 WACC (Final Decision): post-tax cost of equity range 5.59%–8.32%; cost of debt 0.67%; vanilla WACC range 2.64%–3.73%; point estimate 3.18% (down from 3.26% at Final Proposals). The CAA stressed that HAL's RAB is indexed by RPI, unlike CPI-based Ofgem and Ofwat utility WACCs, so the rates are not directly comparable. The notional gearing was 60%, and the CAA tested financeability against BBB+ metrics, including an FFO/net debt average of 8.2% for 2022–24 against the 7.0% threshold S&P cites for HAL's class A debt. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. H7 revenue requirement (2020 CPI-real, £ million, total 2022–26): operating costs 5,995; regulatory depreciation 4,629; asymmetric risk allowance 78; allowed return 3,208; gross requirement 13,909; less commercial revenues (including other regulated charges) 5,159 and cargo 92; net revenue requirement 8,658, over 375.5 million forecast passengers (unprofiled average yield £23.06). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. H7 price cap per passenger: 2022 £26.96 (2020 CPI) / £30.19 nominal; 2023 £26.06 / £31.32 nominal (holding cap £31.57); 2024–26 flat at £21.03 (2020 CPI), equal to £25.43, £25.24 and £25.28 nominal. The CAA chose a flat 2024–26 profile over a declining one for financeability. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. TRS: a mechanism to reduce HAL's gains or losses from passenger volumes outside its control, which allowed a five-year control to be kept. It is retained in the H8 proposals, along with an asymmetric risk allowance. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 5]
8. Appeals: HAL, British Airways, Delta and Virgin Atlantic appealed to the CMA in April 2023. The CMA's final determinations (17 October 2023) found the CAA "was not wrong in most of the decisions". It upheld the £300 million RAB adjustment and nearly all of the WACC. It found errors in one minor cost-of-debt element, in the passenger forecast "shock factor", and in the mechanistic application of the AK-factor clawback of 2020–21 over-recovery. It ordered the CAA to reconsider these and expected only a small overall impact. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
9. H8 (January 2027 to December 2031), Initial Proposals (CAP3232, March 2026): HAL's July 2025 business plan sought a 17% rise in average charges from about £28 to £33 per passenger (2024 CPI) with £9.5 billion of capex. Airlines proposed about £25 with £5.5 billion of capex. The CAA's midpoint average charge was £28.80, against an H7 average of £28.40, with an efficient capex envelope of about £5.8 billion (2024 CPIH). WACC: 60% notional gearing; CPIH-real risk-free rate about 2.33%; total market return 6.7–6.9%; cost of equity about 7.45%; cost of debt 3.14% CPIH-real (embedded 2.8%, new 3.9%, with new debt about 15%); vanilla WACC 4.87%, against an H7 CPIH-equivalent of 4.06% (cost of debt 1.53%). [confidence: high] [source: 5]
10. 2027 holding cap (CAP3308, September 2026): because the final H8 decision is scheduled for April 2027, the CAA modified HAL's licence to set a forecast holding price cap for 1 January to 31 December 2027 of £28.658 (2027 prices). This rose from £28.398 proposed in June 2026. It was based on the midpoint of the £25.79–£27.44 (2024 CPIH-real) Initial Proposals range for 2027, with an NPV-neutral true-up later. Early costs of capacity expansion (a third runway) are handled by a separate decision of July 2026 that adjusts 2026 allowed revenue. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

### B. Landlord-port concession model (World Bank Port Reform Toolkit, 3rd edition, July 2025)

11. Port management models range from public service port and tool port, through landlord port (traditional; with a public-private terminal operator; with DBFM contracts), to a port development and management company and full privatisation. In a traditional landlord port, the public sector provides basic infrastructure and the private sector provides superstructure and operations. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
12. The landlord port is "the most common port management model". Private stevedoring companies provide and maintain their own superstructure and equipment. The port authority provides land and common-user infrastructure (quay walls, breakwaters, land, hinterland connections), and retains nautical services, which may themselves be concessioned. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
13. Revenue model: "The landlord's costs should be recovered by port dues received from vessels calling the port and from concession payments from the various terminal operators." The terminal operator pays a concession fee whose level depends on the business case. It is "often determined as a bidding parameter in a competitive awarding procedure". [confidence: high] [source: 7]
14. Contract horizons (Toolkit Table 4.4): management contracts 2–5 years (up to 10); lease contracts 10–15 years (up to 25); DBFOM or concession 10–30 years (up to 95); full privatisation in perpetuity. Under leases and concessions, the private party bears market risk. Concession forms include BOT, BOOT, ROT and ROOT. London Gateway and the Port of Southampton are cited as full privatisation, where the operator owns the land. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
15. Advantages cited: a clear public/private split, balanced risk allocation, an operator with long-term incentives to invest, and competition. Disadvantages cited: risk of overcapacity, risk of mistiming capacity additions, and large public expenditure. Limitations cited in Module 3: the model depends on the port authority's institutional capacity; some authorities mishandled unsolicited proposals, causing "terminalization" or fragmentation; local and national interests can conflict; a regulator may be needed where competition is weak; and investment income is under pressure. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
16. Best practices cited for reform to the landlord model: corporatised, commercially oriented port authorities; throughput and performance specifications in concession agreements; open, transparent, contested terminal awards (used to admit intra-port competition or expand capacity); and authorities acting as proactive land developers. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
17. For special-purpose facilities, a fixed fee is paid for occupation of public land and, where relevant, for public infrastructure or equipment provided. [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

- Heathrow: the CAA assesses financeability on a notional 60%-geared company at BBB+. The CAA observed that the actual gearing of Heathrow Funding Limited was higher in 2022 and that this is for HAL's owners to manage. The CAA also noted that HAL's owners had not injected equity during the pandemic. Debt is serviced from regulated yield plus single-till commercial revenue. TRS and the asymmetric-risk allowance reduce volume risk. [source: 1]
- Landlord port: the terminal concessionaire's lenders take volume and tariff risk (unless tariffs are regulated) and must rank the concession fee (fixed and/or variable) against debt service. The port authority's own financing rests on port dues and concession income. [source: 7]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- H7 was set amid pandemic uncertainty. The CMA noted that the core dispute was how far HAL's investors should be relieved of covid costs, and who should bear future traffic shocks. The CMA backed the CAA on most points. [source: 2]
- H8: the CAA attributes upward pressure on charges to a higher real cost of debt and higher depreciation from more capex, offset by higher passenger forecasts. [source: 5]
- Landlord model: the Toolkit's listed pitfalls (overcapacity, fragmentation, mistimed expansion) are the main risks to a terminal concession's volumes. [source: 7, 8]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 21: Contrast two revenue contracts. Heathrow's is a regulated licence cap: price is set, volume risk is partly shared through TRS, and commercial revenue cross-subsidises aeronautical charges (single till). A port terminal's is a concession: the operator sets or receives tariffs, bears volume risk and pays the landlord a fee, which is often the bid variable. Walk through the H7 building blocks table to show how a RAB-based price per passenger is derived.
- Ch 80: For airports, use H7 versus H8 WACC (3.18% RPI-real; 4.06% versus 4.87% CPIH-real) to show how financing conditions feed directly into user charges, and why regulatory gearing assumptions differ from actual securitised structures. For ports, use the Toolkit's model spectrum and contract horizons (concessions of 10–30 years, up to 95) to discuss tenor matching and handback, and how the fee structure and minimum performance terms affect bankability.
