# Sustainable finance standards: ICMA GBP/SBP/SLBP, LMA GLP/SLLP, EU Taxonomy, EU Green Bond Standard, ISSB S1/S2, Paris Article 6, ICVCM

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: ICMA Principles Annual General Meeting publications, 22 June 2026; Climate Bonds Initiative 2025 market data, 17 March 2026; first Article 6.4 credit issuance approved, late February 2026; EU Taxonomy simplification delegated act published in the Official Journal, 8 January 2026)

## Summary

Labelled sustainable debt rests on a stack of mostly voluntary, process-based market standards, with a growing layer of law on top. In bonds, the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) hosts the Green Bond Principles (GBP), Social Bond Principles (SBP), Sustainability Bond Guidelines (SBG) and Sustainability-Linked Bond Principles (SLBP). Since 6 November 2025 it has also hosted the Climate Transition Bond Guidelines. In loans, the Loan Market Association (LMA), Asia Pacific Loan Market Association (APLMA) and Loan Syndications and Trading Association (LSTA) publish the Green Loan Principles (GLP), Social Loan Principles (SLP) and Sustainability-Linked Loan Principles (SLLP), last revised on 26 March 2025. On 16 October 2025 they added an exposure draft of Transition Loan Principles. These principles define labels by process: use of proceeds, project selection, management of proceeds and reporting for use-of-proceeds instruments, and KPIs, targets, pricing features, reporting and verification for sustainability-linked instruments. They do not set an environmental performance threshold. The EU has legislated one. The EU Taxonomy Regulation (EU) 2020/852 defines "environmentally sustainable" activities, and the European Green Bond Regulation (EU) 2023/2631 (applying since 21 December 2024) creates a voluntary "European Green Bond" (EuGB) label. That label requires Taxonomy alignment of proceeds, with a 15% flexibility pocket, and ESMA-registered external reviewers. A simplification delegated act cut Taxonomy reporting burdens from 1 January 2026. On disclosure, the ISSB's IFRS S1 and S2 are being adopted jurisdiction by jurisdiction; the ISSB says more than 40 jurisdictions are using them or taking steps to. Targeted IFRS S2 amendments (December 2025) take effect from 1 January 2027. In carbon markets, Article 6 of the Paris Agreement supports country-to-country trading (6.2) and a UN crediting mechanism (6.4, the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism, PACM). PACM approved its first issuance, for a Myanmar cookstove programme, in February 2026. The ICVCM's Core Carbon Principles (CCP) label is the main integrity benchmark for voluntary credits; CCP-labelled credits were about 4% of 2024 issuance. Climate Bonds Initiative recorded USD 1.0 trillion of aligned green, social and sustainability bond issuance in 2025, the third year in a row above USD 1 trillion.

## Verified facts

### A. ICMA bond principles (global, voluntary; current texts at icmagroup.org, "The Principles, Guidelines and Handbooks")

1. The GBP are voluntary process guidelines with four core components: (i) Use of Proceeds, (ii) Process for Project Evaluation and Selection, (iii) Management of Proceeds, and (iv) Reporting. Two key recommendations for heightened transparency are a Green Bond Framework and External Reviews. The current edition is dated June 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. The June 2025 GBP update extended the definition of Green Projects to include "activities". It also clarified the link to the Green Enabling Projects Guidance, first published in June 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3]
3. On 26 June 2025 the Principles' Executive Committee published "Sustainable Bonds for Nature: A Practitioner's Guide". It also allowed green bonds aligned to the GBP whose proceeds go exclusively to nature-related projects to use the secondary designation "Nature Bond". [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. On 6 November 2025, at the 11th Annual Conference of the Principles in Tokyo, the Executive Committee published the Climate Transition Bond Guidelines (CTBG). These create "Climate Transition Bond" as a standalone label for use-of-proceeds bonds financing climate transition projects, with a definition, safeguards and a preliminary, non-exhaustive list of eligible project categories. They also make recommendations for high-emitting issuers of climate-transition-themed sustainability-linked bonds. A revised Climate Transition Finance Handbook was released at the same time. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
5. Safeguards for transition projects reported by practitioners include: alignment with a credible issuer transition strategy, no viable low-carbon alternative, and emission reductions beyond business-as-usual. Example categories include carbon capture, fuel switching and early retirement of high-emitting assets. [confidence: medium] [source: 5]
6. At the 2026 Annual General Meeting (Milan, 22 June 2026) the Executive Committee published four items:
   - a Climate Transition Bond Guidelines FAQ;
   - a comparison of the GBP and the European Green Bond Standard, which describes the two as complementary;
   - a paper on structural demand for sustainable bonds;
   - updates to the Guidance Handbook, the Guidelines for External Reviews (now distinguishing pre-issuance, post-issuance and entity-level evaluation) and the impact reporting frameworks.
   In the green impact reporting framework, annual absolute gross GHG emissions became a core indicator. ICMA said over 96% of issuers in 2025 referenced its Principles, and it put the sustainable bond market at about USD 7 trillion. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
7. The SLBP define sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs) as bonds whose financial or structural characteristics, typically the coupon, vary with whether the issuer meets predefined Sustainability Performance Targets (SPTs). They have five core components: selection of KPIs, calibration of SPTs, bond characteristics, reporting and verification. The proceeds are for general purposes, not ring-fenced. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

### B. LMA/APLMA/LSTA loan principles (global, voluntary; current texts at lma.eu.com and lsta.org)

8. On 26 March 2025 the LMA, APLMA and LSTA published revised GLP, SLP and SLLP and updated guidance. The revision separated mandatory requirements ("shall"), recommendations and options more clearly. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
9. The GLP mirror the GBP's four core components: use of proceeds, project evaluation and selection, management of proceeds, reporting. Since the 2025 revision, loans that intentionally mix eligible green and social projects may be called "Sustainability Loans". [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
10. The 2025 SLLP state that sustainability-linked loans exist to support transition to more sustainable business practices, which is not limited to climate. They now say SPTs "shall" be ambitious, where the earlier text said "should". The SLLP core components are KPI selection, SPT calibration, loan characteristics (the margin ratchet), reporting and verification. [confidence: high] [source: 8, 9]
11. On 16 October 2025 the LMA, with the APLMA and LSTA, published a Guide to Transition Loans. It contains an exposure draft of the Transition Loan Principles (TLP) for use-of-proceeds transition loans, which require a credible entity-level transition strategy. The associations said they would refine the text over 6 to 12 months before publishing a recommended final version. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 10]

### C. EU Taxonomy (EU law; texts on EUR-Lex; Commission "EU Taxonomy Navigator")

12. Regulation (EU) 2020/852 (the Taxonomy Regulation) classifies an activity as environmentally sustainable if it:
    - makes a substantial contribution to at least one of six environmental objectives (climate change mitigation; climate change adaptation; water and marine resources; circular economy; pollution prevention and control; biodiversity and ecosystems);
    - does no significant harm (DNSH) to the others;
    - meets minimum (social and governance) safeguards;
    - meets the technical screening criteria set in delegated acts. The main ones are the Climate Delegated Act (EU) 2021/2139, the Complementary Climate Delegated Act (EU) 2022/1214 for certain gas and nuclear activities, and the Environmental Delegated Act (EU) 2023/2486. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
13. On 4 July 2025 the Commission adopted a delegated act simplifying Taxonomy reporting. It was published in the Official Journal on 8 January 2026 and applies from 1 January 2026, covering financial year 2025 reporting, with an option to apply from financial year 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 12, 13, 14]
14. The main simplifications:
    - companies need not assess Taxonomy eligibility or alignment for activities that together make up less than 10% of turnover, CapEx or OpEx (they report these in summary);
    - non-financial companies' reporting data points fall from 78 to 28 per aligned activity (about 64% fewer), and banks' by up to 89%;
    - the pollution DNSH criteria (Appendix C) are narrowed to focus on substances of very high concern listed for authorisation by the European Chemicals Agency;
    - financial institutions may defer some KPI reporting to 2028. [confidence: medium] [source: 13, 14]

### D. European Green Bond Standard (EU law, voluntary label; Regulation (EU) 2023/2631 on EUR-Lex; ESMA register of external reviewers)

15. Regulation (EU) 2023/2631 on European Green Bonds was adopted on 22 November 2023 and has applied since 21 December 2024. Issuers may call a bond a "European Green Bond" or "EuGB" only if they comply with it. [confidence: high] [source: 15, 16]
16. Proceeds must go to Taxonomy-aligned activities. A flexibility pocket allows up to 15% to fund activities that meet the Taxonomy's other requirements but for which no technical screening criteria yet exist. [confidence: high] [source: 15, 16]
17. Before issuance, an issuer publishes a factsheet approved by an external reviewer. It publishes an allocation report every 12 months until proceeds are fully allocated, and an impact report at least once during the bond's life. External reviewers must register with ESMA. A transitional regime for reviewers ran from 21 December 2024 to 21 June 2026. The Commission also created optional disclosure templates for other bonds marketed as environmentally sustainable or sustainability-linked. [confidence: high] [source: 16, 17]

### E. ISSB (IFRS S1 and S2; texts at ifrs.org; jurisdictional adoption tracked by the IFRS Foundation)

18. IFRS S1 (General Requirements for Disclosure of Sustainability-related Financial Information) and IFRS S2 (Climate-related Disclosures) were issued in June 2023. They are effective for annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2024, but they apply to a company only when a jurisdiction or the company adopts them. IFRS S2 builds on the TCFD recommendations: governance, strategy, risk management, and metrics and targets. [confidence: high] [source: 18, 19]
19. On 11 December 2025 the ISSB issued "Amendments to Greenhouse Gas Emissions Disclosures" (amending IFRS S2). The amendments:
    - allow entities to limit Scope 3 Category 15 measurement to financed emissions, excluding for example derivatives, facilitated financing and some insurance activities;
    - require a subtotal for financed emissions;
    - extend jurisdictional reliefs on measurement methods and global warming potential (GWP) values;
    - give more flexibility in choosing an industry-classification system.
    They are effective for reporting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027, with early application permitted. [confidence: high] [source: 18, 19]
20. In its April 2026 update to the IFRS Advisory Council (information as of end-March 2026), the IFRS Foundation said more than 40 jurisdictions had decided to use the ISSB Standards or were taking steps to introduce them. It listed Ghana, Indonesia, Kenya, Rwanda and Thailand among jurisdictions with 2027 effective dates. [confidence: high] [source: 19]
21. S&P Global counted 21 jurisdictions that had adopted the standards, voluntarily or mandatorily, with reporting starting between 1 January 2024 and 1 January 2026. Mandatory rules took effect at the start of 2026 in jurisdictions including Chile, Qatar and Mexico. [confidence: medium] [source: 20]

### F. Paris Agreement Article 6 (UNFCCC; current rules at unfccc.int, Article 6.4 Supervisory Body pages)

22. Article 6.2 provides for cooperative approaches: bilateral transfers of "internationally transferred mitigation outcomes" (ITMOs) between countries, with "corresponding adjustments" to avoid double counting. Article 6.4 establishes a UN-supervised crediting mechanism, now called the Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism (PACM), overseen by the Article 6.4 Supervisory Body. It issues A6.4 emission reductions (A6.4ERs). [confidence: high] [source: 21, 22]
23. Under the rules, modalities and procedures adopted at COP26 (decision 3/CMA.3), the mechanism takes two cuts at issuance. A share of proceeds of 5% of A6.4ERs goes to the Adaptation Fund. At least 2% of issued A6.4ERs are cancelled to deliver an overall mitigation in global emissions (OMGE). [confidence: high] [source: 22, 23]
24. Projects under the Kyoto Protocol's Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) could apply to transition to PACM. In February 2026 the Supervisory Body approved the first issuance of A6.4ERs under PACM. The project is a CDM-transitioned improved-cookstove programme of activities in Myanmar (UNFCCC reference 10471), financed by South Korean companies; the buyers are mainly South Korean emissions-trading-system participants. Applying updated, more conservative parameters cut credited volumes by about 40% compared with CDM calculations. Carbon Market Watch and researchers criticised the project as over-crediting. [confidence: high] [source: 24, 25]

### G. ICVCM Core Carbon Principles (voluntary carbon market; icvcm.org)

25. The Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market (ICVCM) sets the Core Carbon Principles (CCPs) and an Assessment Framework. It approves crediting programmes as "CCP-Eligible" and methodologies as "CCP-Approved". Only credits under approved methodologies from eligible programmes may carry the CCP label. [confidence: high] [source: 26, 27]
26. On 6 August 2024 the ICVCM decided that credits under eight existing renewable energy methodologies, mostly of CDM origin (for example grid-connected renewable electricity), could not carry the CCP label. The reason was additionality: many such projects would likely have gone ahead without carbon revenue. Press reports estimated that about 236 million credits, roughly a third of the market's eligible supply, were affected. [confidence: high] [source: 28]
27. The ICVCM's CCP Impact Report 2025 gave these figures: 36 CCP-approved methodologies as of November 2025; about 51 million unretired credits approved to carry the label (MSCI, October 2025); CCP-labelled credits about 4% of 2024 issued volume; and an average price premium of around 25% (ClearBlue Markets, Calyx Global). By March 2026, search summaries of ICVCM material reported 38 approved methodologies out of 59 assessed. [confidence: medium] [source: 26, 27]

### H. Market size and indicative norms

28. Climate Bonds Initiative (17 March 2026) recorded aligned 2025 issuance of USD 653.5 billion green, USD 217.3 billion sustainability, USD 141.2 billion social and USD 14 billion sustainability-linked bonds. Annual aligned issuance passed USD 1 trillion for the third consecutive year, though slightly below 2024 and 2021. Cumulative aligned GSS+ volume reached USD 6.8 trillion at end-2025. CBI's "aligned" figures exclude bonds that fail its screening, so they differ from total labelled issuance. [confidence: high] [source: 29]
29. Drivers of pricing for sustainability-linked instruments (no dated range was verified; see "Do not state"):
    - investor and lender demand for labelled paper;
    - regulatory scrutiny of SPT ambition (the 2025 SLLP "shall be ambitious" change);
    - the cost of external verification;
    - the borrower's underlying credit quality, which dominates the margin.
    [confidence: medium] [source: 8, 9]

## Financing and structure details

- **Use-of-proceeds instruments** (green, social and sustainability bonds or loans; EuGB; climate transition bonds): the instrument usually ranks pari passu with the issuer's other debt, and the label attaches to how proceeds are allocated and reported. In project finance, a single-asset borrower's entire facility can be green because the whole project is the eligible asset. [source: 1, 7]
- **Performance-linked instruments** (SLBs, SLLs): proceeds are general purpose, and pricing steps up or down with SPT performance. These suit a whole company better than a single project. [source: 1, 9]
- **EuGB**: the issuer publishes a factsheet with a pre-issuance external review, then allocation reports and an impact report; reviewers are ESMA-registered. [source: 16, 17]
- **Article 6 credits as project revenue**: a project may sell A6.4ERs or ITMOs to compliance buyers (for example, in the first PACM issuance, Korean ETS participants), less the 5% share of proceeds and 2% OMGE deductions at issuance. [source: 23, 24]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Labels without thresholds invite greenwashing claims; the response is regulation and tighter principles.** The EU chose a legislated, Taxonomy-linked label with registered reviewers (EuGB) over relying solely on the GBP. ICMA describes the two as complementary. [source: 6, 15]
- **SLL and SLB credibility**: the 2025 SLLP moved SPT ambition from "should" to "shall", in response to concerns about weak targets. [source: 8, 9]
- **Carbon credit integrity**: the ICVCM's rejection of legacy renewable energy methodologies on additionality grounds split the market into labelled and unlabelled credits. Labelled credits sell at a reported premium of about 25%. The first PACM issuance shows that the UN mechanism's integrity will be judged project by project: Carbon Market Watch argued the Myanmar cookstove project over-credits even after a 40% haircut, and noted that the ICVCM had previously rejected the methodology as insufficiently rigorous. [source: 25, 26, 28]
- **Reporting burden**: the EU's 2025-26 simplification (the 10% materiality threshold and fewer data points) shows the trade-off between comparability and cost of compliance. [source: 13, 14]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 84 (Sustainable finance and climate risk)**: Organise the chapter around three questions: who sets the label (market body or legislator), what the label certifies (process, use of proceeds or performance), and who checks it (second-party opinion or registered reviewer). Use the GBP four components and the EuGB's 85/15 rule to show the difference between a voluntary process standard and a legislated performance standard. For transition finance, contrast ICMA's CTB label (November 2025) with the still-draft Transition Loan Principles. Use the first PACM issuance and the ICVCM's renewable energy decision to teach additionality and over-crediting risk when a project counts on carbon revenue. For climate risk disclosure, teach IFRS S2's TCFD-based structure and the December 2025 financed-emissions relief, and tell readers to check adoption status in their own jurisdiction on the IFRS Foundation's jurisdiction profiles.

## Do not state

- Do not say that the final Transition Loan Principles have been published. As of the latest verified source the TLP were an exposure draft (October 2025); whether a final version appeared in 2026 was not verified.
- Do not give a single figure for the first PACM issuance volume. Sources conflict: one reports 603,368 tCO2e available on issuance, another 60,000 credits. Do not give an exact issuance date beyond "late February 2026".
- Do not state the EuGB's grandfathering period for changes in Taxonomy criteria without checking the text of Regulation (EU) 2023/2631. It was not verified here.
- Do not state the number of EuGBs issued to date. It was not verified.
- Do not cite a specific basis-point "greenium" or SLL margin ratchet size as fact. No dated, sourced range was verified; present it as an indicative range only after checking a current market source (for example, LSEG LPC or Environmental Finance data).
- Do not give the Delegated Regulation number for the Taxonomy simplification act (it appears as OJ L 2026/73, but the formal number was not confirmed).
- Do not say the SBP, SBG or SLBP were revised in June 2025 or June 2026. Only the GBP June 2025 edition and the 2026 guidance documents were verified.
- Do not count the jurisdictions that have adopted ISSB as an exact number without giving date and source; counts differ by definition (adopted vs. "taking steps").
- Do not describe COP29 (Baku, November 2024) Article 6 decisions in detail. They were not verified here beyond the fact that PACM rules were operational by 2025-26.
