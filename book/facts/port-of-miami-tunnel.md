# Port of Miami Tunnel: availability payments and geotechnical risk sharing

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: tunnel opened to traffic 3 August 2014; concession runs to 15 October 2044)

## Summary

The Port of Miami Tunnel (POMT, now PortMiami Tunnel) consists of twin bored road tubes, each about 3,900 feet long and 41 feet in outside diameter. They run under Government Cut, the main shipping channel, and link Dodge Island (the port) with Watson Island, the MacArthur Causeway and I-395. The project also widened the MacArthur Causeway bridge and rebuilt the port roadways. The Florida Department of Transportation (FDOT) procured it as a 35-year design-build-finance-operate-maintain concession, paid by milestone payments during construction and availability payments for 30 years of operation. There are no user tolls. Miami Access Tunnel (MAT), owned 90% by Meridiam and 10% by Bouygues Travaux Publics, reached commercial close on 2 June 2009 and financial close on 15 October 2009. That followed the 2008 collapse of the original deal when equity sponsor Babcock & Brown failed. Total project cost was about $1.1 billion, financed with $341.5 million of senior bank debt from a ten-bank club, a $341 million TIFIA loan, about $80 million of equity and FDOT funds. FDOT's share was offset by contributions from Miami-Dade County and the City of Miami. The deal is a standard teaching case for two design features. It was the second availability-payment transportation P3 in the U.S., after I-595 in Florida. Its geotechnical risk-sharing band put the first $10 million of changed-ground-condition costs on the concessionaire, the next $150 million on FDOT and the next $20 million on the concessionaire, with a mutual termination right beyond that. Bouygues Civil Works Florida bored the tunnels with a Herrenknecht TBM, then the largest-diameter soft-ground TBM in the U.S. The tunnel opened on 3 August 2014, a few months after the originally scheduled spring 2014 completion.

## Verified facts

1. The project comprises three parts: twin tunnels beneath Government Cut between Watson Island and Dodge Island (PortMiami); connections to the port roadway system on Dodge Island; and widening of the MacArthur Causeway bridge with realignment of the eastbound roadway and frontage road. Each tunnel is about 3,900 feet long and 41 feet in outside diameter, with its bottom about 120 feet below the surface of Government Cut at the deepest point. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. FDOT used a 35-year concession with MAT Concessionaire, LLC, running from 15 October 2009 to 15 October 2044 and including a 55-month design and construction period. The concessionaire must hand back the tunnel in the condition the agreement prescribes. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4]

[confidence: high as to each published figure; amounts changed over time] [source: 1, 4, 5]
13. Geotechnical risk sharing. Under the agreement:
    - the concessionaire bears the first $10 million of additional costs from changed geotechnical conditions;
    - FDOT bears the next $150 million;
    - the concessionaire bears the next $20 million;
    - beyond that, either party may terminate.

    FHWA's project profile describes this as a $180 million geotechnical contingency for the risk of boring through soft ground under Biscayne Bay. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
14. FHWA describes the project as the second U.S. application of availability payments to finance a transportation project, after the I-595 Corridor Roadway Improvements, also in Florida. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
15. The TBM was built by Herrenknecht for the project's geology. FDOT described it in 2012 as the largest-diameter soft-ground TBM in the United States at that time, and said it cost more than $45 million. It was nicknamed "Harriet". Construction used about 12,000 precast segments, eight per ring. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 7]

    - It launched from Watson Island on 11 November 2011.
    - It broke through on Dodge Island on 31 July 2012, completing the eastbound tunnel.
    - It relaunched on 29 October 2012.
    - It completed the westbound tunnel at Watson Island on 6 May 2013.

    [confidence: high] [source: 5]
17. In 2010 FDOT expected construction to finish at the end of April 2014, and its 2012 fact sheet said May 2014. The tunnel opened to the public on 3 August 2014. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3, 4]
18. FDOT presented the P3 as transferring most construction cost-overrun, schedule and long-term O&M risk to the private party, giving FDOT a fixed long-term cost structure with payment deductions for underperformance. It also cited access to tunnelling expertise that FDOT lacked in-house and private financing to deliver the project earlier. [confidence: high as to FDOT's and FHWA's stated rationale] [source: 4, 6]

## Financing and structure details

- **Grantor:** FDOT, with Miami-Dade County and the City of Miami as local funding partners.
- **Concessionaire:** MAT Concessionaire, LLC (Meridiam 90%, Bouygues Travaux Publics 10%).
- **Contractors:** Bouygues Civil Works Florida (design-build) and Transfield Services, later Webber Infrastructure Management (O&M).
- **Debt:**
  - Senior bank debt of $341.5m from ten banks, split into milestone debt ($313.4m) and MAP debt ($28.1m).
  - TIFIA loan of $341m plus $40.1m of capitalized interest, subordinated in priority, with a senior-first amortization profile.
- **Equity:** about $80m.
- **Leverage:** debt was 80% of the $903m private-side uses.
- **Reading the structure (analysis, not a sourced statement):** the "milestone debt" label and its size, close to the $350m final acceptance payment, suggest that this tranche bridged construction costs until FDOT's final acceptance payment. The MAP debt and TIFIA were to be serviced from availability payments. FDOT's FAQ confirms that MAT would use the $350m final payment "to repay some of its debt and equity" [4]. Do not state a precise repayment waterfall beyond that.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Bounded, shared ground risk.** FHWA presents the $10m / $150m / $20m band as an allocation, not a transfer. The concessionaire keeps a first-loss incentive to manage ground risk and a second private layer above the public layer. It is not exposed to unlimited risk, and the public sector knows its maximum exposure [6]. FHWA also cautions that the public sector can still lose money on the part of the geotechnical risk it retains [6].
- **Availability payments made a non-tolled crossing financeable.** The tunnel carries port trucks and cruise traffic with no toll. Availability payments funded by state and local contributions let FDOT use private finance and life-cycle incentives without taking traffic risk off its own books [1, 6].
- **Market-timing risk at close.** The original deal failed in December 2008 when Babcock & Brown's finances collapsed, and it was revived with Meridiam in 2009 [5]. A club of ten banks at relatively small tickets, plus a large TIFIA loan, closed the financing in a dislocated market [1, 2].
- **Delivery.** The tunnel opened about three months after the 2014 target [2, 3, 4]. Bored tunnelling, the highest-risk element, was finished by May 2013 [5].

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 58 (Procuring and designing PPPs):** Use POMT as the worked example of an availability-payment regime. Cover milestone payments plus a large final acceptance payment to cut private financing need, a capped inflation-indexed availability payment with deductions, a 35-year term with handback, and a public funding stack assembled from state and local partners. Then use the geotechnical band to teach how to share an uninsurable, unpriceable risk. Contrast it with full transfer, where bidders would add large contingencies, and with full retention, which removes incentives.
- **Ch 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels):** This is a non-tolled tunnel, a useful counterpoint to Case T's user-pay toll regime. Discuss when availability payments beat tolls: when traffic is captive or policy-driven, when diversion to a free alternative (the Port Bridge) would undermine tolls, or when the public goal is decongestion. Then turn to tunnel-specific risks: TBM selection for the geology, ground treatment, interfaces with live port and causeway traffic, and why tunnels are where geotechnical risk sharing matters most.

## Do not state

- Do not say FDOT actually paid out a specific amount from the geotechnical contingency, or that MAT's claims were resolved in a particular way. Wikipedia reports (citing the Miami Herald) a July 2011 MAT request to draw on a "$150 million" reserve to grout limestone and an FDOT denial on 19 July 2011. These were not verified against primary sources.
- Do not state a daily delay penalty (Wikipedia says $115,000 a day) or the total penalty paid by the contractor. Not verified.
- Do not present $607m, $667m and $668m as conflicting "errors". They are FDOT figures from different dates. Give the date with any figure used.
- Do not state equity as exactly $80.5m. FDOT's fact sheet says $80.3m, FHWA's primer says $80.5m. Use "about $80 million."
- Do not state any post-completion refinancing, change in ownership, or traffic outcome beyond the first-month press reports. Not verified.
- Do not call POMT "the first availability-payment P3 in the U.S." FHWA says the second, after I-595.
- Do not name a "Miami-Dade 50% share" as a single number. Local contributions were stated in different-year dollars and changed between 2007 and later documents.
