# Project-financed waste-to-energy in England: the Defra waste PFI programme, Norfolk's Willows EfW termination (2014) and Herefordshire and Worcestershire's EnviRecover (council-funded)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered in the sources: NAO "Oversight of three PFI waste projects", June 2014; events end May 2014)

Cross-references: facts/uk-pfi.md (PFI generally, gain-sharing and portfolio data); facts/northern-lights.md (Encyclis's Protos EfW carbon-capture contract under the UK CCUS programme).

## Summary

In England, energy-from-waste (EfW) plants were mostly procured by local authorities as 25-year PFI or PPP contracts. A unitary charge is paid for treating residual waste. Central government part-funded it through "Waste Infrastructure Credits", grants paid in equal instalments over the contract life. The policy driver was the EU Landfill Directive (1999/31/EC) and its targets for diverting biodegradable municipal waste. Defra set up the Waste Infrastructure Delivery Programme in July 2006. After the 2007 Spending Review, GBP 3.2 billion of PFI credits were available, of which Defra planned to award GBP 2.8 billion to 39 projects. In October 2010 it withdrew provisional credits from seven projects, judging that England would meet its 2020 target without them. The National Audit Office's 2014 review of three troubled contracts is the best primary record of how EfW project finance fails before construction. In Norfolk, a GBP 582 million contract with Cory Wheelabrator for the Willows plant near King's Lynn (signed February 2012) lost its GBP 91 million credit allocation in October 2013 after missing a planning long-stop date. The council terminated it in April 2014 at an estimated cost of GBP 33.7 million, much of it bank fees and hedge breakage. In Herefordshire and Worcestershire, a 1998 contract with Mercia Waste Management went 15 years without its EfW plant. When planning finally came in 2012, the project could not raise construction finance. The two councils then became the sole lenders, using Public Works Loan Board borrowing, and Defra cut its support from GBP 143 million to GBP 113 million.

## Verified facts

### Programme

1. Local authorities have statutory responsibility for municipal waste disposal. In July 2006 Defra established the Waste Infrastructure Delivery Programme to support and accelerate local-authority waste infrastructure procured through PFI. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The 2007 Comprehensive Spending Review added GBP 2 billion of PFI credits to the GBP 1.2 billion already allocated, making GBP 3.2 billion available. Defra planned to award GBP 2.8 billion of "Waste Infrastructure Credits" to 39 local-authority waste projects. Credits are paid as grant in equal quarterly instalments over the contract life. The grant is higher than the credit value to preserve purchasing power, and the amount is set when the contract is signed. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. By 2014 Defra was overseeing GBP 1.7 billion of credits allocated to 28 local authorities with PFI waste projects. Once paid, the grant was not ring-fenced to the waste contract. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. On 20 October 2010 Defra withdrew provisional PFI credit allocations from seven projects: Cheshire West and Chester with Cheshire East; Coventry, Solihull and Warwickshire ("Project Transform"); Gloucestershire; Leicestershire; Milton Keynes and Northamptonshire; North London Waste Authority; and the South London Waste Partnership. Its reason was that they were no longer needed to meet the 2020 EU landfill diversion target. Eleven projects kept provisional allocations, and Defra said it remained committed to 21 signed waste PFI projects. It estimated savings of GBP 3 million a year in 2014-15, rising to GBP 26 million a year from 2017-18. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

### Norfolk: Willows Power and Recycling Centre (Cory Wheelabrator)

5. Defra provisionally allocated GBP 91 million of credits in May 2009, which Norfolk estimated would have been worth GBP 169 million of grant over 25 years. Cory Wheelabrator was appointed preferred bidder in November 2010. The planning application was submitted in June 2011. The 25-year contract, worth about GBP 582 million (GBP 610 million after an October 2013 revised project plan), was signed in February 2012. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. The Secretary of State for Communities and Local Government called in the planning application in August 2012. The inquiry ran from February to May 2013. Defra's 2012 funding agreement included a planning long-stop date of 10 June 2013, which was missed. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
7. Defra reran its capacity model, a Monte Carlo simulation that found the 2020 target would be met with 95% certainty without Norfolk's capacity. In October 2013 ministers revoked the GBP 91 million of credits. Norfolk's cabinet decided to proceed anyway. It estimated GBP 20 million of lifetime savings against landfill, against the "at least GBP 200 million" originally expected. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
8. A planning decision was expected by 14 January 2014 but was postponed with no new date. On 7 April 2014 Norfolk announced its intention to terminate. By April 2014 it estimated the expected savings had fallen to GBP 12 million and were falling by GBP 140,000 a day, reaching zero by June 2014. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
9. The estimated termination cost was GBP 33.7 million:
   - GBP 20.3 million (a contractual cap) for the contractor's costs, "mainly to fees paid by the contractor to secure bank financing";
   - GBP 11.8 million to unwind hedging taken out against long-term foreign exchange and interest-rate movements;
   - GBP 1.6 million for 90% of the contractor's planning-inquiry costs.

   Norfolk had set aside GBP 19 million and would fund about GBP 14.7 million from other budgets. Terminating before a contractual breakpoint on 1 May 2014 avoided about GBP 4.7 million of extra compensation for a month's delay. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

### Herefordshire and Worcestershire: EnviRecover, Hartlebury (Mercia Waste Management)

10. In December 1998 the two councils signed a 25-year integrated waste contract with Mercia Waste Management, worth about GBP 680 million and including an EfW plant. The government committed GBP 143 million of grant (GBP 57.4 million of credits). The grant was paid from the start of services, whether or not the plant had been built, and totalled GBP 89.5 million by 31 March 2014. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. The history of the plant:
    - an EfW application at Kidderminster failed on appeal in July 2002;
    - an autoclave plan at Hartlebury received planning permission in 2005 but collapsed in 2007 over the lack of an end market for its by-product, which was a planning requirement;
    - an EfW at Hartlebury was resolved for approval in March 2011, called in in May 2011, and approved by the Secretary of State in July 2012. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. By 2012 "the delay in building the plant meant the project was unable to secure the financing necessary for construction". The councils agreed in February 2014 to finance the contractor themselves and finalised the contract changes on 21 May 2014. They became the sole source of construction finance, borrowing under the prudential regime (from the National Loans Fund through the Public Works Loan Board) and on-lending to the contractor. The NAO flagged the conflict between their roles as sole lender and contracting authority. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
13. Defra and HM Treasury had not been satisfied on two points: whether the councils needed to be the sole financier rather than one lender alongside banks, and the value of the plant at contract expiry, given the shortened operating period. Because the councils' approach no longer met PFI requirements, Defra cut its support by GBP 30 million, from GBP 143 million to GBP 113 million, about GBP 3.1 million a year from 2014-15. It also required the councils to bear any EU fines their actions caused. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

### Surrey (for comparison)

14. Surrey's June 1999 25-year integrated waste contract with SITA Surrey, estimated at GBP 740 million, originally envisaged two EfW plants. Defra committed GBP 204.7 million of grant and had paid GBP 124 million by 31 March 2014, although the planned facilities had not been built. In 2013 GBP 13.3 million was re-profiled until a gasifier was expected to operate in 2016-17. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

## Financing and structure details

- Typical structure: a 25-year PFI or PPP waste-treatment contract between the waste disposal authority and an SPV. The SPV is paid a performance-related unitary charge, part-funded by central grant (credits) paid over the contract life. Senior bank debt is hedged for interest-rate and sometimes FX risk, because EfW technology and equipment are often imported. [source: 1]
- Pre-financial-close risk is the key credit risk. In Norfolk the bank financing was arranged (fees incurred, hedges executed) while planning consent was still outstanding, so planning failure triggered capped compensation plus hedge breakage. [source: 1]
- Public substitution: when private finance became unavailable after long delays (Hartlebury), the authorities took the lender role through PWLB borrowing. This ended PFI-credit eligibility and cost them GBP 30 million of central support. [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- NAO (2014): all three projects suffered "significant delays" from planning difficulties, local opposition, complex commercial issues and technology uncertainty. Defra cannot intervene in the quasi-judicial planning process. Grant terms in early agreements (Surrey, Herefordshire and Worcestershire) started payments before infrastructure was delivered, which made funding hard to withdraw. Norfolk's later agreement had stricter terms, including the planning long-stop, and those terms were used. [source: 1]
- The NAO did not judge the value for money of the contracts. Treat any claim that these projects were "poor value" as opinion. [source: 1]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 81 (Social infrastructure, water, desalination and waste-to-energy):** Use Norfolk as the clearest worked example of pre-construction risk in a waste PPP. The GBP 33.7 million break-up shows exactly what "compensation on planning failure" pays for: bank fees, hedge breakage and bid costs. Use it to discuss whether hedges should be executed before consents are final, and how a breakpoint date changes the cost of waiting. Use Hartlebury to show what happens when delay outlasts lender appetite: the public authority becomes the lender, with the conflicts the NAO describes. Then contrast grant support tied to service start (Surrey) with support tied to milestones (Norfolk).
