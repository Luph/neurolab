# Research on judgment: deliberate practice, the pre-mortem, reference-class forecasting, Brier scores and calibration

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest item covered: Tetlock and Gardner, Superforecasting, 2015; Macnamara et al. meta-analysis, 2014; the research base is settled and not time-sensitive)

## Summary

Five research findings give a practitioner a concrete plan for building judgment. Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (Psychological Review, 1993) argued that expert performance mainly reflects years of deliberate practice. They defined this as effortful activity designed to improve performance, with feedback, that is "not inherently enjoyable". A 2014 meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald found that deliberate practice matters but explains much less of the variance in performance than claimed: 26% in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under 1% in professions. Gary Klein's pre-mortem (Harvard Business Review, September 2007) asks a team to assume the project has already failed and to write down why. It draws on 1989 research by Mitchell, Russo and Pennington, which found that "prospective hindsight" raised the ability to identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%. Reference-class forecasting comes from Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 work on intuitive prediction and the "outside view". Bent Flyvbjerg made it a practical method (Project Management Journal, 2006), and the UK Department for Transport and HM Treasury adopted it in 2004 as optimism-bias uplifts. The Brier score (Brier, Monthly Weather Review, 1950) measures probability forecasts by squared error. Tetlock's Good Judgment Project used it to identify "superforecasters" in a government-funded forecasting tournament. Mellers et al. (2014) found that training, teaming and tracking improved both calibration and resolution. For deal professionals the transferable lesson is to keep a written, scored record of your own forecasts, and to start every estimate from a reference class.

## Verified facts

### A. Deliberate practice

1. Citation: K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Th. Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, "The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance", Psychological Review, vol. 100, no. 3, pp. 363–406 (July 1993). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. The abstract states that individual differences in performance, even among elite performers, are "closely related to assessed amounts of deliberate practice". It also says that many characteristics once thought to reflect innate talent result from intense practice "extended for a minimum of 10 years". [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. The authors write that deliberate practice "requires effort and is not inherently enjoyable". They stress immediate informative feedback, and say that without adequate feedback efficient learning is impossible and improvement only minimal. They report that studies show essentially no benefit from practice exceeding about 4 hours a day. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
4. The violin study drew on students at the Music Academy of West Berlin. By age 18 the best violinists had accumulated an average of 7,410 hours of practice, against 5,301 hours for the good violinists and 3,420 hours for the lowest group (students training as music teachers). In a second study, expert pianists had accumulated 7,606 hours by age 18, against 1,606 hours for amateurs. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
5. Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, "Deliberate Practice and Performance in Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions: A Meta-Analysis", Psychological Science 25(8): 1608–1618 (2014). Deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance in performance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education and less than 1% for professions. The authors conclude that deliberate practice "is important, but not as important as has been argued". [confidence: high] [source: 3]

### B. The pre-mortem

6. Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, September 2007 (reprint F0709A). [confidence: high] [source: 4]
7. Klein cites 1989 research by Deborah J. Mitchell (Wharton), Jay Russo (Cornell) and Nancy Pennington (University of Colorado). It found that prospective hindsight, "imagining that an event has already occurred", increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%. [confidence: high for what Klein reports; the underlying 1989 paper was not read] [source: 4]
8. Procedure as Klein describes it:
   - the team is briefed on the plan;
   - the leader announces that the project has "failed spectacularly";
   - each person independently writes down every reason they can think of for the failure, especially reasons they would not normally raise for fear of being impolitic;
   - going round the room, starting with the project manager, each person reads one reason until all are recorded;
   - the project manager then reviews the list to strengthen the plan.
   Klein argues the method makes it safe for knowledgeable dissenters to speak and reduces a "damn-the-torpedoes" attitude in people overinvested in a project. [confidence: high] [source: 4]

### C. Reference-class forecasting

9. Kahneman and Tversky, "Intuitive Prediction: Biases and Corrective Procedures", in S. Makridakis and S. C. Wheelwright (eds), TIMS Studies in the Management Sciences 12: Forecasting (North-Holland, 1979). Flyvbjerg cites this as the foundation of reference-class forecasting. It was developed further in Lovallo and Kahneman, "Delusions of Success", HBR, July 2003. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
10. Flyvbjerg, "From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right", Project Management Journal 37(3): 5–15 (August 2006). The method has three steps:
    - identify a relevant reference class of past, similar projects;
    - establish a probability distribution of outcomes for that class;
    - compare the specific project with the distribution to arrive at the most likely outcome.
    [confidence: high] [source: 5]
11. Flyvbjerg reports average cost overruns in constant prices of 44.7% for rail, 33.8% for bridges and tunnels and 20.4% for roads. Rail passenger forecasts were wrong by an average of -51.4%, with 84% of rail projects wrong by more than ±20%. Accuracy had not improved over the 70 years for which cost data were available. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
12. First practical use: the paper by Flyvbjerg and Cowi (2004), "Procedures for Dealing with Optimism Bias in Transport Planning", led the UK Department for Transport and HM Treasury, in summer 2004, to use the method in appraising large transport projects. This followed HM Treasury's 2003 Green Book revision on optimism bias. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
13. Illustrative uplifts from the paper:
    - roads: 15% for a 50% risk of overrun, 32% at the 80th percentile, 45% for a 10% risk;
    - rail: 40% at 50%, 68% for a 10% risk;
    - the metro-rail example uses a 57% uplift at the 80th percentile.
    Flyvbjerg says the 80th percentile is the risk level the Department for Transport typically accepts for large local transport investments. [confidence: high as of the 2004–06 data; current DfT guidance not checked] [source: 5]
14. The American Planning Association endorsed reference-class forecasting in April 2005, according to Flyvbjerg (2006). [confidence: medium; reported by Flyvbjerg, APA text not checked] [source: 5]

### D. Brier score and calibration

15. Glenn W. Brier, "Verification of Forecasts Expressed in Terms of Probability", Monthly Weather Review 78(1): 1–3 (January 1950). [confidence: high] [source: 6]
16. Definition. In the common binary form, the Brier score is the mean of (f − o)² over N forecasts, where f is the forecast probability and o is 1 if the event happened and 0 if not. It ranges from 0 (perfect) to 1, and lower is better. Brier's original version sums the squared error over every outcome category. For a yes/no event that gives double the binary value, so the range is 0 to 2. Tetlock's books use the 0–2 convention. Worked check (computed here):
    - a forecast of 80% scores 0.04 if the event happens and 0.64 if it does not on the binary scale (0.08 and 1.28 on the 0–2 scale);
    - a permanent 50% forecast scores 0.25 (0.5 on the 0–2 scale).
    [confidence: high for the mathematics; medium for the claim about Tetlock's convention] [source: 6, 7]
17. Murphy (1973) decomposes the Brier score into reliability (calibration), resolution and uncertainty: BS = REL − RES + UNC. Being calibrated means your 70% forecasts come true about 70% of the time. Resolution means your forecasts move away from the base rate when the evidence justifies it. [confidence: medium; secondary source] [source: 7]
18. Mellers et al., "Psychological Strategies for Winning a Geopolitical Forecasting Tournament", Psychological Science 25(5): 1106–1115 (2014). Five university-based research groups competed in a two-year geopolitical forecasting tournament. The Good Judgment group found three drivers of accuracy:
    - training: probability training corrected biases and taught forecasters to use reference classes;
    - teaming;
    - tracking: the top 2% from year 1 were placed in elite teams.
    All three improved calibration and resolution. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
19. Philip E. Tetlock and Dan Gardner, Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction (Crown, September 2015 in hardback/ebook). The publisher says the government-funded Good Judgment Project involved "tens of thousands of ordinary people". It also says the best forecasters beat other benchmarks and prediction markets, and even "the collective judgment of intelligence analysts with access to classified information". [confidence: high that the publisher makes the claim; the underlying comparison was not independently verified] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

Not applicable. The direct project finance link is the DfT/HM Treasury optimism-bias uplift (fact 13). It is the reason UK public-sector capex and schedule estimates carry explicit, percentile-based uplifts. It is also the analogue of a lender's technical-adviser contingency and P50/P90 thinking.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Flyvbjerg (2006) attributes forecasting failure to optimism bias and to strategic misrepresentation, meaning deliberate low-balling to win approval. He argues that the outside view bypasses both. He accepts that choosing the reference class is harder for genuinely novel technologies. [source: 5]
- Macnamara et al. (2014) caution that practice explains little of the variance in professions. Ericsson's framework is therefore a guide to how to practise, not a promise that hours alone produce expertise. [source: 3]
- Mellers et al. (2014) show that accuracy can be trained, and that tracking performance and putting good forecasters together compounds the gains. [source: 8]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 87 (The practitioner's craft and career)**: Build the "deliberate plan for building judgment" on three habits. First, a forecasting log: before credit committee, write down dated probabilities for COD on time, the first-year DSCR band and refinancing success, then score them with a Brier score once outcomes are known. Second, a pre-mortem at every mandate kick-off and before signing. Third, a reference-class check on every capex, schedule and traffic number. Teach Ericsson accurately, as targeted practice with fast feedback, and use Macnamara's 2014 result to explain why professions give weak feedback unless you create your own.
- **Chapter 88 (The frontier)**: Use reference-class forecasting to discipline appraisal of new structures and technologies (SMRs, hydrogen, LDES). Name the closest reference class even when it is imperfect, then widen the distribution because the class is a poor fit. Run a structured pre-mortem as part of the framework for evaluating new structures. Flyvbjerg's caveat that the reference class is hardest to choose for novel technology is the key teaching point.

## Do not state

- Do not call the "10,000-hour rule" a finding of Ericsson et al. (1993). The paper's age-18 averages are 7,410 hours (best violinists) and 7,606 hours (expert pianists). The rule is a popularisation, and the paper does not prescribe a fixed number of hours.
- Do not give any details of the Mitchell, Russo and Pennington (1989) paper (title, journal, sample) beyond what Klein reports. It was not read.
- Do not quote superforecasters' exact Brier scores or claim a specific percentage by which the Good Judgment Project beat intelligence analysts (a figure of about 30% is often repeated). Neither was verified from a primary source.
- Do not state current DfT or Green Book optimism-bias uplift percentages. The figures above are from the 2004–06 work, and current supplementary guidance was not checked.
- Do not attribute the "dart-throwing chimpanzee" line to Tetlock's Expert Political Judgment (2005) as a quotation. It was not verified.
