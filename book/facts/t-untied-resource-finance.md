# Untied and resource-backed lending: JBIC import and untied loans, Korean ECAs and Chinese policy-bank offtake-linked finance

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest items: JBIC–Rincon untied loan, 10 March 2026; JBIC–ADNOC facility, 30 September 2025)

## Summary

Export credit agencies normally lend because something is exported from their country. Resource-importing states also use a second family of instruments in which the link is to imports of oil, LNG, copper, lithium and other materials into the lender's country, or to strategic supply chains, rather than to exports. Japan's JBIC offers two such products. Import loans finance imports of strategic resources by Japanese companies, and may be lent to foreign exporters; the loan amount may not exceed the import contract value. Untied loans are not conditional on buying Japanese goods, and are justified by objectives such as securing stable supplies of energy and minerals for Japan. Recent examples are a USD 3 billion co-financed facility for ADNOC (September 2025, JBIC's seventh crude-supply loan to ADNOC), USD 666 million for Codelco (March 2025) and a USD 1,175 million lithium loan to Rio Tinto's Rincon Mining (March 2026). Korea's ECAs take part in LNG and resource projects whose output goes to Korean buyers; for example, KEXIM and K-sure's USD 1.5 billion in Sabine Pass Trains 1–4 tracked KOGAS's offtake (facts/sabine-pass.md). Chinese policy banks, above all China Development Bank (CDB), made large loans linked to oil supply. In 2009 CDB lent Petrobras USD 10 billion for 10 years alongside a 10-year agreement to export 150,000 to 200,000 barrels a day to Sinopec's Unipec, which Petrobras said did not amount to a securitisation. CDB's oil-backed sovereign loans to Ecuador and Venezuela went further: proceeds of oil sales flowed into lender-controlled accounts (Gelpern et al., 2021). The US EXIM Supply Chain Resiliency Initiative (2024–25) is a newer import-authority analogue (t-critical-minerals-policy.md). For project lenders, the key questions are where cash is trapped, who controls the collection account, how the offtake is priced, and how the loan ranks against other creditors in a restructuring.

## Verified facts

### Japan (JBIC); current rules as of 2026-10-03, found on JBIC's "Support Menu" pages

1. JBIC import loans support imports of strategically important goods, including natural resources, by Japanese companies. They also cover cases where Japanese companies or affiliates receive natural resources in countries where they do business. Borrowers can be Japanese importers, Japanese companies and affiliates, or foreign exporters. Eligible resources include oil, LNG, coal, uranium, metallic ore, metals, hydrogen and fuel ammonia, among others. The loan may not exceed the value of the import contract. Rates are linked to JBIC's funding cost, including Fiscal Loan Fund borrowings, and the repayment period is set after reviewing the import contract. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. JBIC untied loans finance projects and imports of goods by foreign countries, balance-of-payments support, and overseas businesses of "eligible foreign companies" in supply chains of strategically important goods or technologies for Japan. Under a Ministry of Finance ordinance (as of 1 October 2023), eligible fields include development of important resources, batteries, semiconductors and renewable-equipment manufacturing. The loans are not conditional on investment or procurement from Japan. Stated uses include securing stable supplies of energy and mineral resources for Japan. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. On 30 September 2025 JBIC signed a facility of up to USD 1.8 billion (JBIC portion) with ADNOC, co-financed by Mizuho (agent) and HSBC Tokyo, for a total of USD 3 billion. Its stated purpose was to support ADNOC in ensuring stable crude imports by Japanese companies. JBIC called it the seventh loan it has provided to ADNOC to secure long-term crude supply to Japan, and classified it as an import loan. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. On 28 March 2025 JBIC signed a loan of up to USD 466 million (JBIC portion; USD 666 million in total with a private co-lender) to Codelco, to fund stable imports of copper concentrate by Japanese manufacturers (import loan). JBIC cited the Seventh Strategic Energy Plan's goal of at least 80% self-sufficiency in base metals by 2030. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. On 10 March 2026 JBIC signed a loan agreement of up to USD 1,175 million with Rincon Mining Pty Limited (a Rio Tinto subsidiary) for the Rincón lithium carbonate project in Salta, Argentina. JBIC's portion is USD 240 million. Co-lenders are Citibank Tokyo (agent), Mizuho, IFC, Export Finance Australia and IDB Invest, and JBIC partially guarantees the private banks' portion. JBIC classified it as an untied loan. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

### Korea

6. KEXIM and K-sure provided USD 1.5 billion of the 2013 Sabine Pass LNG Trains 1–4 financing, which facts/sabine-pass.md links to the KOGAS offtake. KEXIM also lent to Barakah (facts/barakah.md) and Ichthys (facts/ichthys.md). [confidence: high] [source: cross-reference to existing sheets]
7. KEXIM's own website could not be reached from this research environment, so KEXIM product rules (for example import-credit or overseas investment credit terms) are not stated here. [confidence: high]

### China

8. On 19 May 2009 Petrobras announced that it had concluded negotiations with CDB for a USD 10 billion, 10-year bilateral loan to finance its investment plan, including goods and services bought from China. At the same time it signed a long-term export agreement with Unipec Asia (a Sinopec subsidiary) for 150,000 barrels a day in the first year and 200,000 barrels a day for the next nine years. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
9. Petrobras signed the final CDB agreements on 3 November 2009, with drawings in tranches. It stated that the Unipec export agreement would start with the first CDB drawdown notice, that the CDB loan was a trigger for the export agreement, and that the agreements were independent and did not represent a securitisation structure. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
10. On 4 December 2017 Petrobras signed a new USD 5 billion CDB financing maturing in 2027, plus a commercial agreement with Unipec Asia for preferential supply of 100,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day, on market conditions, for 10 years. The remaining USD 2.8 billion of the 2009 loan was prepaid, ending the 2009 supply agreement early. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
11. Gelpern, Horn, Morris, Parks and Trebesch ("How China Lends", March 2021) analysed 100 contracts between Chinese state-owned lenders and government borrowers in 24 countries. They found that 29% of the Chinese contracts used liens, escrow or special accounts, against 7% for OECD bilateral creditors and 1% for multilateral creditors. Six of eight CDB loans in the sample had some form of security, against 22% of China Eximbank loans. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
12. In the same study, account arrangements typically required a minimum balance, in most cases equal to annual principal, interest and fees. In 70% of Chinese transactions with a special account, all project revenues had to be deposited there. In 38%, the account was funded from unrelated sources, including oil export revenues (Ecuador, Venezuela) and bauxite (Ghana). In five CDB contracts (Argentina, Ecuador, Venezuela) the lender could block withdrawals. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
13. The study describes CDB's 2010 USD 1 billion oil-backed loan to Ecuador's Ministry of Finance. Of the loan, 80% was freely usable for projects and 20% was reserved for purchases from selected Chinese contractors. It was backed by a PetroEcuador–PetroChina contract to sell at least 380,000 barrels of fuel oil a month and 15,000 barrels of crude a day. PetroChina paid the proceeds into a CDB-held account governed by Chinese law, and CDB could debit that account for the Republic's debts to CDB under any agreement. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
14. The authors note that special accounts are standard in limited-recourse project finance but rare in official sovereign lending. They cite the US emergency loans to Mexico (1982 and 1994), which routed oil proceeds through Mexico's account at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, as an exception. [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Market norms and drivers (indicative)

- Size caps: JBIC import loans are capped at the import contract value. EXIM SCRI sizing follows the US offtake share. In each case the volume of contracted resource flows sizes the debt rather than project cash flow alone. [source: 1, t-critical-minerals-policy.md]
- Pricing: JBIC states that rates link to its funding cost. Specific margins on the ADNOC, Codelco or Rincon loans are not disclosed. [source: 1, 3–5]
- Drivers: the importer country's energy and mineral security policy (Japan's Strategic Energy Plan and critical minerals policy), the producer's credit, the existence of a long-term offtake to the lender country, and co-financing appetite among commercial banks.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 78 (Mining and critical minerals): Contrast three ways resource flows secure a loan. In the Japanese import or untied loan, offtake to Japan justifies the loan, but the borrower's credit and the co-lenders carry the risk. In the Petrobras–CDB pair, a loan and a parallel export contract are expressly not a securitisation. In the CDB–Ecuador model, sales proceeds go to a lender-controlled account with set-off rights across all of the debtor's CDB debts. Ask students how each would rank in a sovereign or corporate restructuring, and why lender control of the collection account matters more than the label on the loan.

- Do not state KEXIM or K-sure product terms, or Korean "resource development" loan programmes. KEXIM sources were not reachable.
