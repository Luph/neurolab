# Mozambique LNG (Area 1, Cabo Delgado, Mozambique)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: reported dispute between the Government of Mozambique and TotalEnergies over force majeure-period costs, late May 2026; construction ongoing after the 29 January 2026 restart)

## Summary

Mozambique LNG is a two-train onshore liquefaction project of approximately 13.1 million tonnes per annum (mtpa) at the Afungi site in Cabo Delgado province, supplied by the Golfinho and Atum fields in Offshore Area 1. Anadarko took the final investment decision in June 2019 on a project of approximately USD 20 billion; Total (now TotalEnergies) became operator after buying Anadarko's 26.5 percent interest in September 2019. On 17 July 2020 the project signed USD 14.9 billion of senior debt, described by Total as the largest project financing ever in Africa, from eight export credit agencies (US EXIM, JBIC, NEXI, UK Export Finance, SACE, ECIC, Atradius DSB and EXIM Thailand), 19 commercial bank facilities and the African Development Bank. An Islamist insurgency in Cabo Delgado culminated in the attack on the town of Palma beginning on 24 March 2021; Total evacuated the site and on 26 April 2021 declared force majeure, suspending the project for more than four years. After security improved with Rwandan and regional troop deployments, TotalEnergies moved to lift force majeure in October 2025 (formally lifted on 7 November 2025), reported that the budget had risen from about USD 15.5 billion to about USD 20.5 billion because of roughly USD 4.5 billion spent during the suspension, and targeted first LNG in 2029. Before restart the lender group had to re-approve amended terms: US EXIM re-approved its loan in 2025, but on 1 December 2025 the UK ended UK Export Finance's participation and the Netherlands' Atradius also did not reconfirm, so the partners agreed to replace those two ECAs' share (about 10 percent of external financing) with additional equity. Full restart of onshore and offshore activities was announced on 29 January 2026; the Government of Mozambique has since disputed part of the force majeure-period costs TotalEnergies seeks to recover.

## Financing and structure details

- Senior debt (July 2020): USD 14.9 billion (TotalEnergies later cited USD 15.4 billion from about 30 lenders); direct and covered ECA loans from US EXIM, JBIC, NEXI, UKEF, SACE, ECIC, Atradius DSB and EXIM Thailand; 19 commercial bank facilities; AfDB loan. [source: 1, 8]
- Reported individual commitments: US EXIM up to USD 5 billion (2019), re-approved at approximately USD 4.7 billion (2025); AfDB USD 400 million; UKEF approximately USD 1.15 billion. [source: 3, 12]
- Restart re-documentation: amended financing terms required each lender's re-approval; two ECAs (UKEF, Atradius) withdrew; the gap (about 10 percent of external financing) is being filled with additional sponsor equity. [source: 8, 11]
- Equity: seven partners including the state company ENH at 15 percent. [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The force majeure was driven by an armed insurgency, a political-violence risk that ECA and political risk cover can address for lenders but that cannot keep a construction site open. The UK government's own stated reason for withdrawing was that risks had increased since 2020. [source: 11]
- A long suspension converts a construction project into a restructuring: the sponsors had to keep paying (about USD 4.5 billion according to TotalEnergies), the cost and schedule changed, and every lender had to re-approve, which gave each lender an exit option; two ECAs took it. [source: 10, 8, 11]
- Host-government relations became a second front: because the government (through ENH) is a partner and must approve the revised development plan and cost recovery, disputes over force majeure costs affect project economics. [source: 13]
- Positive: the sponsors kept the lender group largely intact (about 90 percent confirmed) and were willing to fill the gap with equity. [source: 8]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 60 (Political risk and its protection): Use Mozambique LNG to separate what political-risk instruments do from what they cannot do: ECA and multilateral participation and PRI protect lenders against loss, but they cannot keep a site operating during an insurgency. Show how force majeure suspends obligations but leaves sponsors paying carry costs, how security arrangements (host forces and Rwandan troops) became a condition of restart, and how reputational and human-rights scrutiny led one ECA government to exit in 2025.
- Ch 76 (LNG and FPSOs): Teach the LNG-specific lesson that a greenfield onshore plant in a frontier location carries site-security and community risk that offshore or brownfield projects avoid. Walk through the restart economics: cost rising from about USD 15.5 billion to about USD 20.5 billion, a four-year delay to first LNG, and the need to re-document the debt and replace withdrawn ECA tranches with equity; use it to discuss how fixed long-term offtake contracts cope with a multi-year delay (details unverified, so treat generically).

## Do not state

- Do not state casualty figures for the Palma attack; estimates vary widely and remain uncertain.
- Do not state the Netherlands' (Atradius) commitment amount; secondary sources disagree (for example, figures around USD 640 million versus a combined UK-Netherlands total of about USD 2.2 billion reported in headlines).
- Do not state that first LNG will be in 2030; one trade source says so, but TotalEnergies' January 2026 statement says 2029.
- Do not state that TotalEnergies requested a specific concession extension (for example, ten years) or that the government granted it; not verified.
- Do not state tenors, pricing or the commercial bank list for the 2020 financing; not verified.
- Do not state as fact allegations of human-rights abuses by security forces near the site or a criminal complaint outcome; these are allegations and contested (TotalEnergies has disputed them).
- Do not reconcile USD 14.9 billion and USD 15.4 billion; cite both with their sources.
- Do not state the current headline project cost as "USD 20 billion" without noting TotalEnergies' figure of about USD 20.5 billion.
