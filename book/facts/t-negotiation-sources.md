# Negotiation sources: BATNA (Fisher and Ury 1981), ZOPA and reservation prices (Raiffa 1982), anchoring (Tversky and Kahneman 1974; Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest item checked: Galinsky, Rodriguez-Mincey and Grayson, Current Opinion in Psychology, online 31 August 2026)

## Summary

Four works anchor the negotiation vocabulary that Chapter 56 uses.

- **Fisher and Ury (1981).** Roger Fisher and William Ury, *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In*, Houghton Mifflin, Boston, 1981. Second edition 1991 with Bruce Patton as co-author; third edition 2011. This book introduced the **BATNA**, the Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement, as the yardstick against which any proposed deal is measured.
- **Raiffa (1982).** Howard Raiffa, *The Art and Science of Negotiation*, Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1982. This is the standard early analytical treatment of reservation prices and the zone in which agreement is possible. The acronym **ZOPA** ("zone of possible agreement") is the later common label for that zone.
- **Tversky and Kahneman (1974).** Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science* 185(4157): 1124–1131, 27 September 1974, doi:10.1126/science.185.4157.1124. The paper identified "adjustment from an anchor" as one of three judgment heuristics.
- **Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001).** Adam D. Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler, "First offers as anchors: The role of perspective-taking and negotiator focus," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 81(4): 657–669, October 2001, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.81.4.657. In three experiments, whoever made the first offer got a better outcome, and first offers strongly predicted final prices. The advantage disappeared when the other side focused on the first mover's alternatives, the first mover's reservation price, or its own target.

## Verified facts

1. **Getting to Yes, first edition:** Roger Fisher and William Ury, *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In* (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1981), ISBN 0-395-31757-6. Later editions: 2nd edition 1991, with Bruce Patton added as co-author; 3rd edition 2011. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. **BATNA origin:** BATNA ("Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement") was developed by Fisher and Ury of the Harvard Program on Negotiation in *Getting to Yes* (1981). The book advises negotiators facing a stronger counterparty to develop their BATNA. Page references are given here for the 3rd edition only (2011, pp. 99–108); 1981 page numbers were not checked. [confidence: high for origin; medium for page references] [source: 2, 3]
3. **Raiffa (1982):** Howard Raiffa, *The Art and Science of Negotiation* (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 1982), ISBN 0-674-04813-X (cloth) / 0-674-04812-1 (paper). [confidence: high] [source: 4]
4. **Tversky and Kahneman (1974):** "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science*, vol. 185, no. 4157, pp. 1124–1131, published 27 September 1974, doi:10.1126/science.185.4157.1124, PMID 17835457. The abstract names three heuristics: representativeness, availability, and adjustment from an anchor. Anchoring is "usually employed in numerical prediction when a relevant value is available". These heuristics are economical and usually effective but lead to systematic and predictable errors. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
5. **Galinsky and Mussweiler (2001):** "First offers as anchors: The role of perspective-taking and negotiator focus," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, vol. 81, no. 4, pp. 657–669 (October 2001), doi:10.1037/0022-3514.81.4.657, PMID 11642352. The first author was then at the University of Utah. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
6. **Galinsky and Mussweiler findings:**
   - In three experiments, whichever party (buyer or seller) made the first offer obtained a better outcome.
   - First offers strongly predicted final settlement prices.
   - The first-offer advantage was eliminated when the other party focused on information inconsistent with the first offer: the opponent's alternatives (Experiment 1), the opponent's reservation price (Experiment 2), or its own target (Experiment 3).
   - The effects held face-to-face and by e-mail. [confidence: high] [source: 8]
7. **Recent extension (2026):** Galinsky, Rodriguez-Mincey and Grayson, "Flexible ambition: How to make bold first offers without backlash," *Current Opinion in Psychology* 73:102433 (online 31 August 2026), doi:10.1016/j.copsyc.2026.102433. They describe a "first-offer double bind": modest first offers produce poor outcomes, while ambitious ones risk backlash. They propose making ambitious offers that signal flexibility: framing them as offers rather than requests, stressing the other side's benefits, using ranges, and using multiple equivalent simultaneous offers (MESOs). [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

Not applicable (source sheet). Application to project finance: the borrower's BATNA is its alternative funding route, such as another bank group, a bond, an ECA or DFI package, or delay. The lenders' BATNA is their alternative use of capital and hold capacity. The ZOPA in a term-sheet negotiation is bounded by each side's reservation terms on margin, tenor, DSCR and covenants.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Galinsky and Mussweiler show that anchoring is powerful but can be neutralised: the defence is to focus on the anchor-setter's alternatives and reservation point, or on your own target, before responding [source: 8]. This links the anchoring and BATNA literatures directly.
- The 2026 review notes the cost side of aggressive anchors, namely backlash and impasse, and proposes ranges and MESOs as mitigants [source: 9].

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 56 (negotiating project finance):** Define BATNA (Fisher and Ury 1981) and reservation price and ZOPA (Raiffa 1982), and have the reader map them for Case P: what the sponsor does if the bank group walks, and the lenders' alternative deals. Then teach first offers as anchors (Tversky and Kahneman 1974; Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001). Whoever drafts the first term sheet anchors the negotiation, so the defence is to prepare your own target and the other side's alternatives before reading their draft. Close with flexible ambition (2026): ranges and multiple equivalent offers, such as margin-for-tenor trade-offs, as a way to anchor high without triggering backlash.

## Do not state

- That Raiffa coined the acronym "ZOPA". Raiffa (1982) analyses reservation prices and the zone of agreement, but the exact term and acronym used in the 1982 text were not verified. Attribute the concept to Raiffa and call ZOPA the conventional later label.
- Direct quotations from *Getting to Yes* or Raiffa, and page numbers for the 1981 or 1982 editions. Not checked against the books.
- The numbers in Tversky and Kahneman's "wheel of fortune" experiment (commonly reported as anchors of 10 and 65 giving median estimates of 25% and 45% for the share of African countries in the UN). Plausible, but not checked against the original text for this sheet. Verify before printing.
- That Bruce Patton co-authored the 1981 first edition. He was added from the 1991 second edition.
- Galinsky and Mussweiler effect sizes or sample sizes. Only the abstract was checked.
