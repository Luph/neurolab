# Commodity prepayment financings to oil-producing states (Chad/Glencore, Kurdistan, Angola)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Chad's G20 Common Framework treatment with an agreement in principle with Glencore, reported in the World Bank–IMF DSA dated end-2022)

## Summary

An oil prepayment (or "oil sale advance") is a loan repaid in barrels, or from the proceeds of specified cargoes. A trader or bank advances cash to a producer, often a national oil company (NOC) or government, against a future offtake contract. Because repayment comes from crude that the lender lifts and sells, it behaves like secured, self-liquidating debt even where formal security is weak. In a sovereign, however, it counts as public debt and can crowd out other creditors. Chad is the best-documented case. In 2013 Chad took USD 600 million of oil sale advances from Glencore Energy. In 2014 the national oil company SHT borrowed about USD 1.4 billion from Glencore to buy Chevron's 25% stake in the Doba consortium. When oil prices fell in 2014–16 the debt became unsustainable. It was restructured in 2015 and again in 2018, with a longer maturity and lower rate, a cash sweep that rises with oil prices, and a capped deferral right. It was then treated again under the G20 Common Framework in 2022. The Kurdistan Regional Government's ministry of natural resources borrowed by selling future oil output to international companies during its 2014–16 fiscal crisis. Angola built up about USD 13 billion of oil-collateralised external loans, mainly from Chinese lenders, and in its IMF programme committed to contract no new oil-collateralised debt.

## Verified facts

Chad and Glencore
1. In May and August 2013 Chad signed two agreements with Glencore Energy totalling USD 600 million (4.5% of GDP). They were non-concessional oil sales advances at 6.9% annual interest plus a 2.75% upfront fee, repayable over two years against crude shipments. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. In 2014 the national oil company SHT took an oil sales advance loan of USD 1.4 billion to finance the purchase of Chevron's 25% participation in Chad's largest oil consortium (Doba). The IMF/IDA described it as non-concessional and "justified as a commercial operation to acquire a strategic income-generating asset". [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. Falling oil prices in 2014–16 reduced revenues available to repay the advances. In late 2015 Chad signed a rescheduling with Glencore that consolidated the advances and extended maturities. Staff said this gave some flow relief but was insufficient and raised the debt's present value. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. Glencore accounted for about 98% of Chad's commercial debt in 2017. Commercial debt rose from virtually nil in 2008 to roughly half of external public debt, peaking at 53–54% in 2016–17. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
5. Chad reached agreement in principle with Glencore in February 2018 and signed the final restructuring in June 2018. The deal had a significantly longer maturity, lower interest rate and fees, and "contingency mechanisms to adjust debt service depending on oil revenues". [confidence: high] [source: 3]
6. Under the 2018 contract, debt service comprises mandatory amortisation and interest plus a cash-sweep component that falls (to zero at a threshold Doba price) as oil prices fall. Chad may also defer some mandatory payments when prices fall, with cumulative deferred amortisation capped at USD 75 million. The Glencore debt was expected to be retired by about 2029. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
7. In 2022 official bilateral creditors and the main private creditors agreed a debt treatment for Chad under the G20 Common Framework, and Chad signed an agreement in principle with Glencore. Glencore agreed to reprofile part of the debt service due in 2024. Official creditors would contribute only if the private contribution was not enough to bring debt service to revenue below 14% in 2024. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
8. At end-2021 Chad's debt to Glencore Energy was about USD 926 million of USD 949 million commercial external debt. Staff noted that the cash-sweep clause had pushed debt service above the 14% threshold in 2022–23 as oil prices rose. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
9. Glencore sold its Chad upstream operations to Perenco in June 2022 for USD 197 million. [confidence: high] [source: 7]

Kurdistan Region of Iraq
10. The World Bank's 2016 KRG reform roadmap reports that the KRG Ministry of Natural Resources covered much of the regional deficit. It borrowed about USD 1.5 billion from the domestic private sector and another USD 1.5 billion "from international companies and suppliers by selling its future oil output". Arrears to international oil companies also mounted. KRG debt rose from 2.9% of regional GDP in 2012 to 75.5% in 2015. [confidence: high for the World Bank's statements; amounts are approximate estimates] [source: 8]

Angola
11. At end-2019 Angola's central government plus Sonangol and TAAG had USD 49.8 billion of external debt, including about USD 13 billion in loans "collateralized with oil receipts". [confidence: high] [source: 9]
12. Angola's IMF Extended Fund Facility included a zero ceiling on new oil-collateralised debt. Before COVID-19 the government prioritised repaying oil-backed loans and committed to contract no new oil-collateralised financing. In 2020 it reprofiled debt with China Development Bank (CDB) and ICBC. It also agreed to use the balance of a CDB debt service reserve account (an escrow with CDB) to pay CDB interest in 2020–22. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
13. Publishing the stock of oil-collateralised debt and the balances of dedicated debt service accounts was a prior action of the World Bank's second Angola development policy financing (2021). [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

- **Mechanics (from the Chad documents).** Cash is advanced and repaid through delivery or sale of crude cargoes. Pricing is a fixed rate plus an upfront fee. In the restructured form, a cash sweep linked to the oil price and a capped deferral right make debt service partly state-contingent. [source: 1, 4, 5]
- **Collateral through escrow.** Angola's loans used dedicated debt service accounts held with the lender, such as the CDB DSRA. [source: 9]
- **Not verified:** the identities and amounts of individual KRG prepay counterparties; Angola's individual loan terms.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Wrong (Chad).** Short tenors and price-sensitive repayment hit at the 2014 price fall. The IMF/World Bank attribute the 2016–17 debt distress mainly to the Glencore advances and lower prices. The fix was to build price contingency into the contract. [source: 3, 4]
- **Wrong (Angola).** Oil-collateralised debt reduced transparency and flexibility. The IMF programme responded with a ban on new collateralised borrowing and with disclosure requirements. [source: 9]
- **Right, for the lender.** In every case, repayment from cargoes the lender lifts gives it de facto seniority. That seniority is why official creditors under the Common Framework insisted on comparable private-creditor treatment. [source: 6]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 75.** Teach prepayments as offtake-linked secured lending. Cover how the cargo-lifting mechanism creates seniority, why tenor and price sensitivity matter, and how state-contingent features (cash sweep, deferral cap) can be designed in. Then use Chad to show the sovereign-debt externality: a commercially rational prepay can trigger a debt restructuring that brings in the IMF, the World Bank and the Common Framework. Close with Angola's ban on new oil-collateralised debt as the policy response.

## Do not state

- Kurdistan prepayment amounts by named trader (for example Glencore, Vitol, Petraco), dates, or repayment terms. These are widely reported but were not verified; the KRG and Reuters sites were not accessible.
- Exact interest rate, maturity and fee of the 2015 and 2018 Chad–Glencore agreements beyond the qualitative descriptions above. IMF country reports were not accessible (access denied).
- The final terms or completion date of the Chad Common Framework agreement with Glencore.
- That Glencore's Chad loan was "USD 1.45 billion". The World Bank/IMF figure is about USD 1.4 billion.
- Specific Angolan loan terms with CDB, ICBC or China Eximbank, or Sonangol trader prepays.
