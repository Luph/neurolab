# Digital deal execution: e-signing finance documents, virtual signings and closings, data rooms, and AI in diligence

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest items covered: Law Commission "Deeds" project listed as upcoming and not yet started, viewed 2026-10-03; Ayinde v Haringey [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), 6 June 2025; Industry Working Group final report, 14 March 2023)

Cross-references: conditions precedent, signing and closing mechanics are in facts/t-loan-docs.md. Contract formation principles are in facts/t-contract-law.md.

## Summary

In England and Wales, electronic signatures are legally effective for commercial documents, including deeds. The question is formalities, not validity.
- **Law Commission.** Its September 2019 report (Law Com No 386) concluded that an electronic signature is capable in law of being used to execute a document, including a deed, provided the signatory intends to authenticate it and any formalities are satisfied. It also concluded that a deed signed "in the presence of a witness" requires the witness to be physically present, even if both use electronic signatures. The Lord Chancellor accepted that no primary legislation was needed (March 2020) and convened an Industry Working Group. That group's final report (March 2023) recommended signing-platform standards, international alignment, wider adoption of e-signatures and a Law Commission review of deeds. As of October 2026, the Law Commission lists "Deeds" as an upcoming project on which work has not started.
- **Virtual signings.** Practice still works around the 2008 Mercury decision. Mercury required a document to be a "discrete physical entity" at signing, and the market follows a 2009 Law Society and City of London Law Society practice note on how to run virtual signings.
- **Companies.** Under the Companies Act 2006, s.44, a UK company executes a document by two authorised signatories or by one director in the presence of an attesting witness.
- **United States.** The ESIGN Act (2000) provides that a signature, contract or record may not be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic.
- **AI in diligence.** The verified authority is cautionary. In Ayinde v Haringey (June 2025) the Divisional Court held that freely available generative AI tools "are not capable of conducting reliable legal research". It held that lawyers using AI have a professional duty to check its output against authoritative sources.

## Verified facts

### A. England and Wales: electronic signatures and deeds

1. Law Commission, "Electronic execution of documents" (Law Com No 386, HC 2624), ordered to be printed 3 September 2019. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The Law Commission's statement of the law, proposition (1): "An electronic signature is capable in law of being used to execute a document (including a deed) provided that (i) the person signing the document intends to authenticate the document and (ii) any formalities relating to execution of that document are satisfied." Formalities may come from statute or contract, for example a requirement for witnessing or for a handwritten signature. Electronic signatures are admissible in evidence (Electronic Communications Act 2000, s.7). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. The report notes that courts have held a typed name at the foot of an email, clicking an "I accept" box and a SWIFT message header to be valid signatures where a statute required a signature and was silent on electronic form. The email example is Golden Ocean Group v Salgaocar [2012] EWCA Civ 265. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. The Law Commission's view is that the requirement that a deed be signed "in the presence of a witness" requires the witness's physical presence, even where both signatory and witness sign electronically. Video witnessing was therefore not accepted under the then law. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. Government response (written statement HCWS143 by the Lord Chancellor, Robert Buckland, 3 March 2020):
   - agreed that primary legislation was not necessary to confirm the validity of electronic signatures;
   - endorsed the Commission's draft statement of the law;
   - accepted the recommendation for an Industry Working Group, to include video witnessing in its work;
   - accepted that a wider review of the law of deeds should take place, subject to priorities.
   [confidence: high] [source: 2]
6. The Industry Working Group on the Electronic Execution of Documents was set up in spring 2021. Its judicial chair was initially Mr Justice Birss, later Mr Justice Fraser. It published an interim report on 1 February 2022 and a final report on 14 March 2023. The final report's main recommendations were:
   - enhanced certification via the ICO and a review of the NCSC Technical Assurance Principles;
   - a self-certification scheme with published basic performance standards for signing platforms;
   - work towards an international standard or mutual recognition;
   - government consideration of wholesale adoption of e-signatures;
   - a Law Commission review of deeds;
   - a review of statutory declarations;
   - a standing successor body.
   Members disagreed on whether accreditation should be mandatory, and the report made no positive recommendation on that point. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
7. On the Law Commission's "Deeds" project page (viewed 2026-10-03), the project is upcoming and "work has not yet commenced on it". The page records that in R (Mercury Tax Group Ltd) v HMRC [2008] EWHC 2721 (Admin), Underhill J referred to a document needing to be "a discrete physical entity (whether in a single version or in a series of counterparts) at the moment of signing". It says the legal industry has largely found workarounds, aided by a 2009 paper from a joint working party of the Law Society Company Law Committee and the City of London Law Society Company Law and Financial Law Committees. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
8. Companies Act 2006, s.44(2): a document is validly executed by a company if signed on its behalf by two authorised signatories (every director, plus the secretary where there is one), or by a director in the presence of a witness who attests the signature. Under s.44(6), a person signing for more than one company must sign separately in each capacity. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
9. The Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 (c. 38) lets electronic versions of trade documents, such as bills of lading and bills of exchange, be possessed and indorsed with the same effect as paper originals. [confidence: high; exact commencement date not checked] [source: 7]

### B. United States

10. Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act (ESIGN), Public Law 106-229, enacted 30 June 2000, 15 U.S.C. §7001(a): for transactions in or affecting interstate or foreign commerce, a signature, contract or other record "may not be denied legal effect, validity, or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form". A contract may not be denied effect solely because an electronic signature or record was used in its formation. [confidence: high] [source: 8]

### C. AI in diligence and legal work (England and Wales)

11. Ayinde v London Borough of Haringey and Al-Haroun v Qatar National Bank QPSC and QNB Capital LLC [2025] EWHC 1383 (Admin), Divisional Court (President of the King's Bench Division and Johnson J), 6 June 2025:
    - freely available generative AI tools such as ChatGPT "are not capable of conducting reliable legal research";
    - they can give confident but untrue answers, cite sources that do not exist, and misquote genuine sources;
    - those who use AI for research have "a professional duty therefore to check the accuracy of such research by reference to authoritative sources" before using it to advise clients or before a court. The court lists authoritative sources such as the government's legislation database, the National Archives judgments database, the official Law Reports and reputable legal publishers' databases.
    [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

How the legal points shape a project finance closing (practice described at the level the sources support):
- Simple contracts can be e-signed through a platform.
- Deeds, such as security documents, guarantees and powers of attorney, raise two issues. First, the witnessing formality: under the Law Commission's view the witness must be physically present (fact 4). Second, the counterpart and signature-page mechanics that follow from Mercury (fact 7).
- Companies must also satisfy s.44 (fact 8).
- Documents governed by foreign law, or registrable at foreign registries, may need wet-ink or notarised execution. Check each jurisdiction.
- A digital closing still needs a CP checklist, release instructions and a funds flow agreed in advance. The technology does not remove the sequencing.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The Law Commission and the government chose clarification over legislation (facts 2 and 5). This was reasonable because the common law was already pragmatic about the form of signature. The remaining friction is in deeds and witnessing, which is why the IWG and the Commission's pending deeds review focus there. [source: 1, 2, 3, 5]
- Ayinde shows the professional risk of relying on unverified AI output. The same duty logically applies to AI summaries of data-room documents: the human signing the report owns its accuracy. [source: 9]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 55 (Running a financing to close)**: Teach a closing execution matrix. For each document record its type (simple contract or deed), its governing law, the signing entity's execution method under s.44, the witnessing requirement, whether it is registrable, and whether e-signature is acceptable. Explain the Mercury-driven signature-page protocol and why the agent's counsel holds pages in escrow until release. Point readers to the Law Commission report and the Law Society/CLLS note for the current English practice. Remind them that foreign-law documents follow local rules.
- **Chapter 88 (The frontier)**: Treat digital execution and AI-assisted diligence as process innovations to evaluate like any new structure: what risk does each remove, and what new risk does it add? E-signatures remove courier and timing risk but add platform, identity and audit-trail risk, which is the focus of the IWG's certification debate. AI review can speed first-pass data-room triage, but Ayinde makes verification against primary sources a professional duty. Present only verified statements about AI capability, and avoid vendor claims.
