# Adaptation and resilience finance: the adaptation finance gap, catastrophe bonds and resilience instruments for infrastructure

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest items covered: Artemis Q3 2026 cat bond market report, 1 October 2026; Jamaica's third World Bank cat bond, priced May 2026; COP30 adaptation finance outcome, November 2025; UNEP Adaptation Gap Report 2025, 29 October 2025)

## Summary

Adaptation needs far exceed adaptation finance.
- **UNEP 2025.** The Adaptation Gap Report 2025, "Running on Empty" (about 29 October 2025), estimates developing countries' adaptation costs at a plausible central range of USD 310–365 billion a year to 2035. International public adaptation finance from developed countries was about USD 26 billion in 2023, down from about USD 28 billion in 2022. That leaves a gap of about USD 284–339 billion a year, and needs roughly twelve times current flows. UNEP judges realistic private adaptation finance at about USD 50 billion a year by 2035 (15–20% of needs), against about USD 5 billion flowing to publicly identified priorities in 2023.
- **International goals.** The COP26 Glasgow goal to double adaptation finance from 2019 levels by 2025 (usually read as about USD 40 billion) was off track. COP30 in Belém agreed to "at least triple" adaptation finance by 2035, five years later than least developed countries had asked, and without a stated baseline year.
- **Returns.** The Global Commission on Adaptation's "Adapt Now" (September 2019) found benefit-cost ratios of 2:1 to 10:1. It estimated that USD 1.8 trillion invested in five areas over 2020–30 could yield USD 7.1 trillion of net benefits.
- **Catastrophe bonds.** Capital markets increasingly fund resilience through cat bonds. Total issuance reached a record USD 18.9 billion in the first nine months of 2026, and the outstanding market was USD 65.5 billion at end-Q3 2026. For infrastructure, the reference deal is the New York MTA's USD 200 million MetroCat Re (2013), a parametric storm-surge bond issued after Hurricane Sandy. For sovereigns, Jamaica's USD 150 million World Bank cat bond paid out in full after Hurricane Melissa in October 2025, and Jamaica then bought USD 200 million of new cover in May 2026.

### B. The economic case (Global Commission on Adaptation)

9. Global Commission on Adaptation, "Adapt Now: A Global Call for Leadership on Climate Resilience", 13 September 2019. Findings:
   - benefit-cost ratios for resilience investment range from 2:1 to 10:1, and in some cases higher;
   - investing USD 1.8 trillion globally from 2020 to 2030 in five areas could generate USD 7.1 trillion of total net benefits. The five areas are early warning systems, climate-resilient infrastructure, improved dryland agriculture, global mangrove protection and more resilient water resources;
   - adaptation offers a "triple dividend": avoided losses, economic gains, and social and environmental benefits.
   [confidence: high] [source: 4]

### C. Catastrophe bonds and infrastructure resilience

10. MetroCat Re Ltd. (Series 2013-1):
    - sponsor: First Mutual Transportation Assurance Company, the captive insurer of New York's Metropolitan Transportation Authority;
    - issued: July 2013;
    - size: USD 200 million, upsized from a USD 125 million target;
    - term: three years;
    - trigger: parametric, based on storm-surge heights from named storms measured at tide gauges around New York City;
    - pricing: 4.5% (below 5%–5.5% guidance);
    - rating: BB- (S&P).
    S&P described it as the first storm-surge cat bond it had rated. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
11. Jamaica's cat bond history:
    - IBRD CAR 130 (2021): USD 185 million;
    - IBRD CAR Jamaica 2024 (May 2024): USD 150 million, covering four hurricane seasons, maturing late 2027. Its parametric trigger (storm location and minimum central pressure) was hit by Hurricane Melissa in October 2025, and Jamaica received the full USD 150 million within weeks;
    - IBRD CAR Jamaica 2026: priced in May 2026, upsized from USD 150 million to USD 200 million, oversubscribed, priced in the lower half of guidance, matures about May 2030.
    [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7]
12. Artemis Q3 2026 report (1 October 2026):
    - cat bond and related ILS issuance in the first nine months of 2026 was a record USD 18.9 billion, against the previous nine-month record of USD 18.6 billion in 2025;
    - Q3 2026 issuance was USD 948 million;
    - the outstanding market was USD 65.5 billion at end-Q3 2026, up 7% from end-2025.
    These figures cover all cat bonds, mostly insurer and reinsurer sponsored, not just infrastructure or sovereign deals. [confidence: high] [source: 8]

## Financing and structure details

- **Parametric cat bond mechanics.** The sponsor, or the IBRD on a sovereign's behalf, enters a risk transfer agreement. Investors' principal is held as collateral and is lost, wholly or partly, if a defined physical parameter is hit, such as surge height or central pressure in a box. Investors earn a risk margin. Payout comes within weeks because no loss adjustment is needed. The cost is basis risk: the trigger can miss a real loss, or pay when the loss was small. (Sources 5 and 6 describe the triggers. The general mechanics are standard and drawn from them.)
- **For an infrastructure owner**, a cat bond or parametric cover sits at the top of a layered programme above traditional property insurance. It can protect revenue and fund rapid reconstruction, which matters for DSCR covenants and for force majeure periods.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right: Jamaica.** A layered disaster-risk programme with a parametric top layer delivered USD 150 million within weeks of Melissa. Investors returned and upsized the next bond, which suggests a payout need not close market access. [source: 6, 7]
- **The gap persists** because adaptation projects often lack a revenue stream. UNEP judges private finance can meet only 15–20% of needs, so public, concessional and blended money remains central. [source: 1]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 84 (Sustainable finance and climate risk)**: Teach physical climate risk as both a credit risk and a funding gap. Start with UNEP's needs-versus-flows numbers (USD 310–365 billion against about USD 26 billion), then show how a lender's physical-risk assessment feeds design standards, insurance requirements and reserve sizing. Use MetroCat Re to show how an infrastructure owner can transfer tail physical risk to capital markets, and note basis risk.
- **Chapter 88 (The frontier)**: Present adaptation as the hardest frontier because most resilience assets earn avoided losses, not revenue. Evaluate the structures with the chapter's framework: who pays, what triggers payment, who bears basis risk. The structures are parametric cat bonds (Jamaica), resilience-linked availability payments in PPPs, and blended funds. Use the "Adapt Now" 2:1 to 10:1 benefit-cost range to explain why the economics can be strong even when the project finance is weak.
