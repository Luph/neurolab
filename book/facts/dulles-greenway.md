# Dulles Greenway: ramp-up risk

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Atlas Arteria's H1 2026 results release, 27 August 2026, reporting progress on TRIP II's 2025 toll rate case and Virginia's March 2026 tolling legislation)

## Summary

The Dulles Greenway is a 14-mile (about 22 km) private toll road in Loudoun County, Northern Virginia, running from the end of the public Dulles Toll Road near Dulles International Airport to Leesburg. It was built under the Virginia Highway Corporation Act of 1988, which let private operators build toll roads without eminent domain, with tolls set by the Virginia State Corporation Commission (SCC) at levels that are reasonable to users, do not materially discourage use, and give the operator no more than a reasonable return. The owner, Toll Road Investors Partnership II (TRIP II), financed construction in 1993 to 1995 with USD 40 million of equity and about USD 310 million of debt: fixed-rate first mortgage notes from a group of insurance companies and a bank construction loan, at a weighted average rate of about 9.8%. The road opened on 29 September 1995, six months ahead of schedule, at a USD 1.75 toll. Traffic fell well short of projections because the corridor developed more slowly than expected; TRIP II cut the toll to USD 1.00 in March 1996 and was in default almost immediately. The debt was restructured in 1999 into MBIA-insured, mostly zero-coupon bonds, and again in 2005, when the concession was extended to 2056. Macquarie bought TRIP II in 2005 for USD 617.5 million, and Atlas Arteria now holds the whole economic interest. Thirty years after opening, the Greenway carries about 15 million trips a year and earns positive cash flow, but it has reported accounting losses in almost every year, its accreting debt is above USD 1.1 billion, it has failed its distribution coverage tests (with distributions locked up until at least the end of 2028), and the SCC's hearing examiner found in 2020 that the investors' unearned authorised return was unlikely ever to be substantially recovered. The case is the classic early US illustration of ramp-up risk and of a structure that defers debt service in the hope that growth will catch up.

## Verified facts

1. Legal basis: the Virginia Highway Corporation Act of 1988 (Code of Virginia § 56-535 et seq.). Operators have no eminent domain power (§ 56-541); the SCC sets tolls that are reasonable to the user in relation to the benefit obtained, will not materially discourage use, and give the operator no more than a reasonable return (§ 56-542 D). The Act bars the Commonwealth from incurring obligations for the project's financing. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
2. Project: about 14 miles in a 250-foot right of way from the Dulles Toll Road terminus to Leesburg; it opened on 29 September 1995, six months ahead of schedule. The SCC granted the certificate of authority in 1990. TRIP II operates under an SCC certificate and a Comprehensive Agreement with the Virginia Department of Transportation (VDOT). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 4]
3. Original financing (FHWA): TRIP II put up USD 40 million of equity and raised USD 310 million of privately placed taxable debt; ten institutional investors led by CIGNA Investments, Prudential Power Funding Associates and John Hancock Mutual Life provided USD 258 million of long-term fixed-rate notes (due 2022 and 2026); Barclays, NationsBank and Deutsche Bank provided part of the construction funding and USD 40 million of revolving credit; the debt was secured by a first mortgage over the developer's interest. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
4. TRIP II's own account (2019 SCC testimony): cost at opening about USD 315 million for right of way, construction, surfacing, safety fixtures and tolling equipment; total funding USD 40 million equity and about USD 311 million debt, of which about USD 254 million fixed-rate First Mortgage Notes with insurance companies and a USD 57 million bank construction loan, at a weighted average interest rate of about 9.80%, with some notes accruing interest for later payment. FHWA gives the project cost as USD 350 million. [confidence: high; note the two sources differ slightly on the debt split] [source: 3, 5]
5. TRIP II acquired all land in fee simple by private negotiation in the early 1990s; a small eastern section is leased from the Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority for USD 600,000 a year, rising to USD 2 million a year from 2036. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
6. Tolls: the 1990 certificate set USD 1.50 to end-1993, USD 1.75 for 1994 to 1995, and USD 2.00 for 1996 to 1997. The road opened at USD 1.75. Traffic fell short of projections owing to a difficult economy and slower than expected development in the corridor. In December 1995 TRIP II asked to suspend the scheduled USD 2.00 increase; in February 1996 it asked to cut the toll to USD 1.00, which took effect in March 1996. The SCC then allowed the approved toll to act as a ceiling below which TRIP II could price freely. Use rose but revenue did not, and tolls were raised again in July 1997. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 5]
7. Default: weaker than anticipated traffic left cash flow insufficient for debt service "and caused a default situation almost immediately" (TRIP II, paraphrased); FHWA records that the owners began to default in 1996. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 5]
8. 1999 restructuring (closed 29 April 1999, SCC-approved): USD 35 million of 7.125% current-interest bonds due 2035 (1999A); about USD 297.8 million original principal of zero-coupon bonds yielding 7.3% maturing serially 2003 to 2035 (1999B, USD 820.9 million face at maturity); USD 43 million of first-tier subordinated compound-interest bonds (9.33%); and USD 29 million of second-tier subordinated bonds (11%). Senior bonds were insured by MBIA. Total debt rose by about USD 149 million in 1999 to USD 419 million, with proceeds funding about USD 9 million of issue costs, about USD 29 million of bond insurance premiums and reserve accounts. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
9. Concession extension: in 2001 the SCC extended the certificate by 20 years, from 2036 to 2056 (expiry 15 February 2056). [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5, 6]
10. 2005 refinancing (2 March 2005, with VDOT support and SCC approval): three series of insured zero-coupon senior bonds, 2005A (USD 162.4 million principal, 5.425%, due 2045), 2005B (USD 53.8 million, 5.7%, due 2043) and 2005C (USD 174.4 million, 5.55% to 5.65%, serial 2036 to 2056). Of about USD 391 million raised, about USD 128 million redeemed the 1999 subordinated bonds, about USD 98 million funded capital works including widening to six lanes, about USD 77 million paid issue costs (including about USD 57 million of bond insurance), about USD 18 million funded reserves, and about USD 70 million was distributed to investors. Total debt reached USD 882 million at end-2005. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
11. Variable peak and off-peak tolls were introduced in September 2004, the first variably priced tolls in the Washington area per FHWA. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
12. Ownership: the original partners included the Shenandoah Group (a family-owned company), Kellogg Brown & Root and Autostrade International; Shenandoah bought Autostrade's 30% in 2003. In August and September 2005 Macquarie Infrastructure Group agreed to buy TRIP II for USD 617.5 million: USD 533 million to Shenandoah for 86.7% and USD 84.5 million to KBR for 13.3%. Financial close on the larger stake was 9 September 2005 and on KBR's on 29 September 2005. Macquarie's 86.7% interest was structured as subordinated loans secured on the limited partner interests plus long-dated call options, not as direct ownership. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
13. In December 2006 MIG sold 50% of its economic interest to Macquarie Infrastructure Partners; MIG's remaining 50% passed to Macquarie Atlas Roads (now Atlas Arteria) on the 2 February 2010 demerger; in 2017 Atlas Arteria bought MIP's 50% for USD 445 million and now holds 100% of the economic interest. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 6]
14. Distribution lock-up covenants: distributions require net toll revenue of at least 1.25 times senior debt service (Minimum Coverage Ratio; failure locks up cash until met for 12 consecutive months) and at least 1.15 times after Improvement Fund transfers (Additional Coverage Ratio; failure locks up cash for 36 months). TRIP II had not met the Minimum Coverage Ratio since 2010 (as of 2019 testimony). [confidence: high] [source: 3]
15. The zero-coupon structure was chosen to allow lower tolls early in the Greenway's life, expecting traffic growth to cover rising debt service later; traffic has not grown as anticipated (TRIP II). Between October 2011 and February 2012 TRIP II paid about USD 34 million to buy back about USD 64 million face value of bonds. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
16. Statutory toll formula: a 2008 amendment (§ 56-542 I) allowed annual increases from 2013 to 2020 at the higher of CPI plus 1%, real GDP growth or 2.8%; approved increases ran from 2.8% to 3.17% a year from 2013 to 2019. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 5]
17. Losses and investor recovery (SCC hearing examiner, October 2020): except for a USD 0.8 million profit in 2017, the Greenway reported a loss in every year since opening, cumulatively USD 609.2 million; it stays viable because it usually generates small positive cash flow. The Reinvested Earnings Account (the cumulative shortfall against the authorised return) grew from about USD 3.0 billion to about USD 7.5 billion between 2013 and 2019, against an initial projection of a maximum of USD 0.4 billion by 2021; initial projections showed more than USD 1 billion of distributions to equity over the Greenway's life, versus about USD 100 million distributed to date; invested equity had risen to USD 144 million from an originally projected USD 34 million. All participants, including TRIP II, accepted that the balance was unlikely ever to be substantially recovered by equity. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
18. 2021 rate case: TRIP II applied on 20 December 2019 for five years of increases; about 730 public comments were received, all but one opposed. On 26 April 2021 the SCC approved only the off-peak increases for 2021 (to a USD 5.00 maximum two-axle off-peak toll) and 2022 (USD 5.25), declining peak increases because of COVID-19 uncertainty, and otherwise denied the application. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 6]
19. TRIP II filed a further toll application on 11 July 2023; no toll changes were made in 2024 or 2025. TRIP II filed a new rate case in December 2025. In March 2026 Virginia adopted legislation letting the SCC approve up to two years of increases at once with deadlines for its final orders (9 or 12 months). [confidence: high] [source: 4, 6, 8, 9]
20. Debt and lock-up today: at 31 December 2025 total debt was about USD 1.114 billion (accreted, before issue costs); TRIP II was not in compliance with the Minimum or Additional Coverage Ratios in 2024 or 2025, so distributions are prohibited until 31 December 2028 at the earliest. Senior debt service reserve was about USD 39.8 million. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
21. Traffic and earnings: 2025 traffic 15.00 million trips (up 8.2%), toll revenue USD 83.5 million, EBITDA USD 65.3 million; H1 2026 traffic 7.66 million (up 6.3%). Traffic was 6.3 million trips in 1996 and 21 million in 2006 (press figures). [confidence: high for 2025 and 2026; medium for 1996 and 2006] [source: 8, 9, 10]

## Financing and structure details

- Sponsor/borrower: Toll Road Investors Partnership II, L.P. (Virginia limited partnership); general partner Shenandoah Greenway Corporation; operator Autostrade International of Virginia O&M (per FHWA). [source: 4, 5]
- 1993 to 1995: USD 40 million equity; about USD 310 million debt (insurance-company fixed-rate notes plus bank construction loan and USD 40 million revolver), weighted average cost about 9.8%, first mortgage security. [source: 3, 5]
- 1999: four series of bonds (senior 1999A current-interest and 1999B zero-coupon, MBIA-insured; subordinated 1999C and 1999D compound-interest). [source: 3, 4]
- 2005: senior insured zero-coupon 2005A, 2005B and 2005C bonds maturing up to 2056; subordinated 1999 bonds redeemed. Interest on zeros accretes, so outstanding debt rises over time; mandatory early redemption funds sweep cash to the 2005A and 2005B bonds in specified years. [source: 3, 4]
- No public guarantee or public funds; the Act prevents state financial obligations. Regulation is by the SCC through toll ceilings, not by an availability payment or revenue guarantee. [source: 1, 4]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Ramp-up and land-use timing. TRIP II attributes the shortfall to a difficult economy and slower-than-anticipated development in the corridor; the forecasts assumed growth that came, but a decade or more later than the debt needed it. [source: 3]
- Price elasticity in a corridor with free alternatives. The toll cut from USD 1.75 to USD 1.00 raised use but not revenue (FHWA), showing that lowering price does not cure a demand shortfall when users are price-sensitive and free routes compete. [source: 5]
- Expensive original debt with no cushion. About USD 311 million of debt at about 9.8% on USD 40 million of equity (TRIP II figures) meant almost any shortfall caused default. [source: 3]
- Back-ended restructuring as a bet on growth. The 1999 and 2005 deals replaced cash interest with accreting zero-coupon bonds and lengthened the concession; this cured the default but raised debt from about USD 311 million at opening to USD 419 million after 1999, USD 882 million after 2005 and about USD 1.1 billion at end-2025 and paid about USD 86 million in bond insurance premiums. The 2005 refinancing also paid USD 70 million to investors. Distribution lock-ups have bound since 2010. [source: 3, 4]
- Regulatory cap on recovery. Toll increases need SCC approval under user-benefit and "materially discourage" tests; strong public opposition (730 comments, all but one opposed, in 2020) and SCC discretion limited increases, so the operator could not price its way out. [source: 2, 7]
- What went right: the road was built early, privately and without public money, it carries 15 million trips a year, and lenders have been protected through insurance and restructuring rather than enforcement.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels, Case T ramp-up): Use the Greenway as the reference case for ramp-up risk: a sound route that opened early, mispriced the timing of corridor growth, and defaulted within months. Walk through the toll cut (USD 1.75 to USD 1.00) as an elasticity lesson, then the 1999 and 2005 zero-coupon restructurings as an example of "extend and pretend" that preserves the asset but transfers value from equity to time. Contrast the Greenway's coverage-ratio lock-ups (1.25x and 1.15x) with Case T's covenant design, and show how a regulator-set toll ceiling turns a demand problem into a permanent one.

## Do not state

- Do not state specific original traffic forecasts (for example "34,000 vehicles a day forecast against 10,500 actual"); these widely repeated figures were not verified in any primary or official source for this sheet.
- Do not state that the original lenders lost principal or that the 1996 default led to foreclosure; sources describe a default cured by the 1999 restructuring, not enforcement.
- Do not state FHWA's "zero coupon bonds maturing in 2003, 2005"; TRIP II's statements show 1999B maturities from 2003 to 2035.
- Do not state the outcome dates of the 2023 rate case (a reported SCC rejection and a 2025 Supreme Court of Virginia decision); only that no toll changes were made in 2024 or 2025 is verified from TRIP II's audited statements.
- Do not describe the Greenway as owned outright by Atlas Arteria; Atlas Arteria holds 100% of the economic interest through a 13.4% direct interest plus subordinated loans and call options over the remaining limited partner interests.
- Do not state that IFM's takeover offer for Atlas Arteria (shown on Atlas Arteria's website) has completed or its terms; it was not researched for this sheet.
- Do not state the 2005 bond insurer's identity beyond "insured"; only the 1999 senior bonds are confirmed as MBIA-insured in the sources used.
