# Spain: retroactive cuts to renewable support and the treaty arbitrations that followed

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: the Real Decreto-ley 18/2026 of 29 June 2026 change to the 7% generation tax, and Spain's 15 September 2026 annulment memorial in the resubmitted Eiser case)

## Summary

Spain's "special regime" for renewable generation peaked with Royal Decree (RD) 661/2007 of 25 May 2007. It offered registered plants either a fixed regulated tariff or the market price plus a premium. Article 44(3) said future tariff revisions would not affect plants already commissioned by a cut-off date. Investment surged, especially in solar PV and concentrated solar power (CSP). So did the electricity system's "tariff deficit", the gap between regulated costs and regulated revenues. The state cut back in stages:

- 2010: RD 1565/2010 and RD-law 14/2010 (titled as urgent measures to correct the tariff deficit).
- December 2012: Law 15/2012 imposed a 7% tax on the value of electricity generated.
- February 2013: RD-law 2/2013 replaced the general consumer price index (CPI) in remuneration updates with a narrower CPI and removed the market-plus-premium option.
- July 2013: RD-law 9/2013 repealed RD 661/2007. The 2013 Electricity Sector Law (Law 24/2013), RD 413/2014 and Order IET/1045/2014 then replaced the old regime. Each standard plant now earns a "reasonable return" over its regulatory life, set for existing plants at the ten-year Spanish bond yield plus 300 bps, which came to 7.398% pre-tax. The return applies over the plant's whole life, so earlier receipts count against it.

Foreign investors brought dozens of claims under the Energy Charter Treaty (ECT):

- Spain won the first cases on the 2010 measures (Charanne, January 2016; Isolux, July 2016).
- From Eiser (May 2017) onward, most tribunals found that the 2013–14 overhaul breached fair and equitable treatment.
- Spain responded through annulment applications (the Eiser award was annulled in 2020 and resubmitted).
- It relied on EU-law objections after the CJEU's Achmea (2018) and Komstroy (2021) rulings, which say intra-EU arbitration is incompatible with EU law.
- RD-law 17/2019 locked in the 7.398% return until 2031 for plants whose owners dropped their claims.
- Spain withdrew from the ECT with effect from 17 April 2025.

## Verified facts

1. RD 661/2007 of 25 May 2007 regulated electricity production under the special regime. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Article 44(3) of RD 661/2007 provided for a review of tariffs, premiums and limits in 2010 and every four years after that. Reviews were to guarantee "reasonable rates of return with reference to the cost of money in capital markets". Revisions to the regulated tariff and the upper and lower limits would not affect installations commissioned before 1 January of the second year after the year of the revision. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. In the consolidated text of RD 661/2007, the regulated tariff for subgroup b.1.1 (solar PV) up to 100 kW is 44.0381 euro cents/kWh. The rate for over 100 kW up to 10 MW is 41.7500 c€/kWh. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. RD 661/2007 was repealed by the sole repealing provision of RD-law 9/2013 of 12 July 2013, with transitional effects kept until new rules were adopted. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
5. RD-law 9/2013, "adopting urgent measures to guarantee the financial stability of the electricity system", was published in the official gazette (BOE) on 13 July 2013. It replaced the special regime with market revenue plus a specific remuneration. That remuneration covers the investment costs that an efficient, well-run company cannot recover from the market, and is benchmarked to a reasonable return. For existing plants, the reasonable return was set at the average ten-year Spanish government bond yield plus 300 basis points. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
6. Other measures and dates, per BOE: [confidence: high] [source: 3]
   - RD 1565/2010 of 19 November 2010.
   - RD-law 14/2010 of 23 December 2010, "establishing urgent measures to correct the tariff deficit of the electricity sector".
   - Law 15/2012 of 27 December 2012 on tax measures for energy sustainability.
   - RD-law 2/2013 of 1 February 2013.
   - Law 24/2013 of 26 December 2013 (Electricity Sector Law).
   - RD 413/2014 of 6 June 2014.
   - Order IET/1045/2014 of 16 June 2014, which approved the remuneration parameters for "standard installations".
7. Law 15/2012 imposed a tax on the value of electricity production (IVPEE) at 7%. Article 15 of RD-law 18/2026 of 29 June 2026 amended the rate to 3.5% for 1 January to 31 December 2027 and 0% from 1 January 2028. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
8. RD-law 2/2013 replaced CPI in all remuneration update methods linked to CPI with CPI at constant taxes excluding unprocessed food and energy products. It also removed the market-plus-premium option. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 7]
9. RD-law 17/2019 of 22 November 2019 set the reasonable return for renewables, cogeneration and waste for the second regulatory period (2020–2025) at 7.09% pre-tax. It let installations under the pre-2013 regime keep 7.398% for 2020–2031. The conditions: [confidence: high] [source: 6]
   - The option was excluded where an arbitral or judicial proceeding based on post-RD 661/2007 changes was pending or had been started.
   - Owners could still qualify by proving to the Directorate General for Energy Policy and Mines, before 30 September 2020, that the proceeding had ended early and that they had irrevocably waived restarting it, or that they had waived any compensation awarded.
   - The preamble says the measure aims to cut new arbitrations and end existing ones, since these carried large claims and affected Spain's ability to attract investment.
10. Charanne v Spain (SCC Arbitration No. 062/2012), award of 21 January 2016: Spain prevailed on the 2010 measures. The tribunal required a specific commitment for legitimate expectations. Isolux v Spain (SCC 2013/153), 17 July 2016: Spain prevailed; the investment was made in 2012, after the 2010 changes. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
11. Eiser Infrastructure Ltd and Energía Solar Luxembourg S.à r.l. v Spain (ICSID Case No. ARB/13/36, ECT) went through these stages: [confidence: high] [source: 7, 8]
    - Award for the claimants on 4 May 2017.
    - Annulment decision by an ad hoc committee on 11 June 2020.
    - Resubmission award on 16 October 2025, with a dissent by Marcelo Kohen.
    - Second annulment proceeding registered on 19 February 2026; Spain filed its memorial on annulment on 15 September 2026.
12. Infrastructure Services Luxembourg S.à r.l. and Energia Termosolar B.V. v Spain (ICSID Case No. ARB/13/31, often called "Antin"): award of 15 June 2018, rectification decision of 29 January 2019, and decision on annulment of 30 July 2021. All proceedings are concluded. [confidence: high] [source: 7, 9]
13. NextEra Energy Global Holdings B.V. and NextEra Energy Spain Holdings B.V. v Spain (ICSID Case No. ARB/14/11) went through these stages: [confidence: high] [source: 10]
    - decision on jurisdiction and liability, 12 March 2019;
    - award, 31 May 2019;
    - provisional stay of enforcement lifted on 28 May 2020;
    - annulment application rejected on 18 March 2022.
14. As of mid-2020, in a review by Noilhac of 20 decisions, Spain had prevailed in three (Charanne, Isolux and Stadtwerke München, 2 December 2019) and lost 17. The review gives Spain's losses at about EUR 825 million and pending claims at about EUR 10 billion across more than 46 registered cases. [confidence: medium; the figures are the author's] [source: 7]
15. The tribunals that found for investors divided in their reasoning. Some read Article 44(3) as a stabilization commitment (for example Antin, OperaFund, Watkins). Others relied on a "fundamental stability"/"radical change" standard without any specific commitment (for example Eiser, Novenergia, RREEF). PV Investors (PCA 2012-14, 28 February 2020) held that Article 44(3) was not a stabilization clause, but still found a breach because the new regime did not provide a reasonable return. [confidence: high as a description of the review] [source: 7]
16. In Achmea (Case C-284/16, Grand Chamber, 6 March 2018), the CJEU held that Articles 267 and 344 TFEU preclude an investor–state arbitration clause in a BIT between EU member states. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
17. In Komstroy (Case C-741/19, Grand Chamber, 2 September 2021), the CJEU held (para. 66) that Article 26(2)(c) ECT must be interpreted as not applying to disputes between a member state and an investor of another member state about an investment in the first state. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
18. Spain's withdrawal from the ECT took effect on 17 April 2025. The EU and Euratom gave notice of withdrawal on 27 June 2024, effective 28 June 2025. Under ECT Article 47(3), investments made before withdrawal stay protected for 20 years. The EU and 26 member states also adopted an inter se declaration on the consequences of Komstroy. [confidence: medium for dates; high for the Article 47(3) principle] [source: 13]
19. On 16 August 2024 the US Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit decided NextEra Energy Global Holdings B.V. v Kingdom of Spain and Blasket Renewable Investments LLC v Kingdom of Spain, both on enforcing ECT awards against Spain in the United States. District court enforcement rulings followed in 2024 and 2025, for example JGC Holdings, RREEF, InfraRed and Infrastructure Services Luxembourg. [confidence: high for the existence and dates; holdings not verified here] [source: 14]

## Financing and structure details

- Typical structures in the claims, as described in the awards reviewed by Noilhac: foreign funds and utilities (for example Eiser, Antin, NextEra, RREEF, InfraRed, Cube, Masdar) held Spanish PV, CSP or wind SPVs through holding companies in other ECT states, often Luxembourg or the Netherlands. That structure gave them standing as investors of "another Contracting Party". [source: 7, 8, 9, 10]
- Remuneration architecture after 2013–14: revenue is market price plus a specific remuneration that covers the investment costs an efficient, well-run company cannot recover from the market. It is calculated for a "standard installation" (Order IET/1045/2014) and benchmarked to the reasonable return. The return is reset for each regulatory period: the first ran to 31 December 2019, and the second runs 2020–2025. [source: 2, 3, 6]
- Project-level debt terms (tenors, gearing and lenders for particular plants) are not verified here.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The regime's own text invited reliance. Article 44(3) promised that revisions would not affect plants already commissioned. Yet it also tied returns to "reasonable rates of return with reference to the cost of money", a phrase Spain later used to justify the 2013–14 reasonable-return model. Tribunals split on which reading governed. [source: 1, 7]
- Spain's stated reason was system finance. RD-law 14/2010 is titled as correcting the tariff deficit, and RD-law 9/2013 as guaranteeing the financial stability of the electricity system. [source: 2, 3]
- Investors who arrived after the 2010 changes, or who relied on general legislation rather than specific commitments, fared worse (Isolux, Charanne). This is the due-diligence point stressed by Noilhac. [source: 7]
- Winning was not the same as collecting. Spain sought annulment, relied on EU law (Achmea, Komstroy) and fought enforcement in other jurisdictions, including the United States. Meanwhile it offered a regulatory carrot under RD-law 17/2019 to settle claims. [source: 6, 11, 12, 14]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 19 (FiTs, CfDs, RAB):** Spain is the standard case of a feed-in tariff whose "stability" was a statute, not a contract, and so could be changed by the next statute. Compare an RD 661/2007 tariff with a two-sided CfD signed with a state counterparty. Then compare the replacement "reasonable return on a standard plant", which is in effect a RAB-style allowed return reset every six years. Have readers work through why the plant-life approach to the return was so damaging to plants that had already earned above it.
- **Chapter 54 (governing law, disputes and investment protection):** Use the Charanne/Isolux versus Eiser/Antin split to teach how legitimate expectations depend on timing, specific commitments and due diligence. Use Achmea and Komstroy for the intra-EU objection, Eiser's annulment and resubmission for annulment risk, and the D.C. Circuit cases for the enforcement stage. Use RD-law 17/2019 to show a state buying peace by trading regulatory certainty for withdrawal of claims, and the ECT's 20-year sunset clause after withdrawal.
- **Chapter 60 (political risk and its protection):** Regulatory change in an OECD, EU member state is political risk too. Teach readers to treat subsidy-dependent revenue as sovereign exposure, and to stress-test a support scheme against a fiscal squeeze such as a tariff deficit. Teach structuring through treaty-protected holding companies, and how intra-EU treaty protection collapsed. Point out that standard PRI usually excludes non-discriminatory regulatory change of this kind.

## Do not state

- Specific award amounts (for example "Eiser EUR 128 million", "Antin EUR 101 million", "NextEra EUR 290.6 million"). They are widely reported but were not checked against the awards here.
- The reason for the 2020 Eiser annulment (reportedly an arbitrator's undisclosed links with the claimants' damages expert firm), the amount of the 2025 resubmission award, or which side it favoured beyond the record that it exists. Not verified here.
- The holdings of the D.C. Circuit in NextEra/Blasket, any UK court rulings on enforcement against Spain, or any European Commission state-aid decision on paying the Antin award. All are reported but not verified here.
- That RD 661/2007 guaranteed tariffs "for 25 years" or "for life" without qualification. The consolidated text has been amended (it now reads "first 30 years" for PV), so check the original text before stating durations.
- The size of the tariff deficit (figures of EUR 26–30 billion circulate). Not verified here.
- Total number of claims or total amount paid by Spain beyond the medium-confidence 2020 figures in fact 14.
- How many investors accepted the RD-law 17/2019 offer. Not verified.
- Spain's ECT withdrawal notification date. Sources differ (April or May 2024); state only the effective date of 17 April 2025.
