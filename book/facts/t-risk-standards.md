# Risk management standards: ISO 31000:2018 and COSO ERM (2017) – process and risk responses

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest documents covered: COSO ERM Executive Summary, June 2017; COSO/WBCSD ESG guidance, October 2018; HM Treasury Orange Book, May 2023)

## Summary

The two reference frameworks for enterprise risk management are ISO 31000:2018 "Risk management – Guidelines" (International Organization for Standardization, second edition) and COSO's "Enterprise Risk Management – Integrating with Strategy and Performance" (June 2017). COSO is the Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission, sponsored by the AAA, AICPA, FEI, IMA and IIA. Both define risk as an effect on objectives, positive or negative, rather than as a pure loss. Both make risk management part of governance and strategy, not a separate department or a checklist. ISO 31000 is a short, generic set of principles, a framework and a process. The process runs scope/context/criteria, then risk assessment (identification, analysis, evaluation), then risk treatment, with communication and consultation, monitoring and review, and recording and reporting around it. COSO 2017 organizes ERM into five components and 20 principles. Its risk responses fall into five categories: accept, avoid, pursue, reduce and share. Project finance practice maps onto both. Contractual allocation, insurance and hedging are "share" responses (ISO: "sharing the risk"). Design changes and contingency are "reduce" responses (ISO: changing likelihood or consequences). Lenders' reserve accounts and covenants are monitoring and treatment tools. The UK government's Orange Book (HM Treasury, May 2023) is an accessible public document that cites BS ISO 31000:2018 and reproduces ISO-style treatment options. ISO's own website blocked access in this research, so ISO text below is verified only at the level of that public restatement and well-established secondary knowledge. Confidence levels are marked accordingly.

## Verified facts

### COSO ERM 2017 (verified from COSO's free Executive Summary and COSO guidance)

1. Title and date. "Enterprise Risk Management – Integrating with Strategy and Performance", Executive Summary dated June 2017, published by COSO. COSO's sponsors are the American Accounting Association, the AICPA, Financial Executives International, the Institute of Management Accountants and The Institute of Internal Auditors. The full framework is sold; the Executive Summary is free. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

2. The 2017 publication updates the original 2004 ERM framework, which the Executive Summary says "was introduced in 2004". It says the update connects ERM more clearly with stakeholder expectations, positions risk in the context of performance, and emphasizes how ERM informs strategy. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

3. COSO's description of ERM in the Executive Summary (paraphrased): ERM is not a function or department. It is the culture, capabilities and practices that organizations integrate with strategy-setting and apply when they carry out that strategy, with the purpose of managing risk in creating, preserving and realizing value. It is also "not a checklist" but a set of principles on which processes can be built. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

4. Definition of risk (COSO 2017, as quoted in COSO/WBCSD 2018 guidance): "the possibility that events will occur and affect the achievement of strategy and business objectives". It covers both negative effects and positive ones (opportunities). [confidence: high] [source: 2]

5. Definition of risk appetite (COSO 2017, as quoted in COSO's risk-appetite thought paper): "The types and amount of risk, on a broad level, an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of value." [confidence: high] [source: 3]

6. The five interrelated components are: (1) Governance and Culture; (2) Strategy and Objective-Setting; (3) Performance; (4) Review and Revision; (5) Information, Communication, and Reporting. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

7. The 20 principles by component:
   - Governance and Culture: 1 Exercises Board Risk Oversight; 2 Establishes Operating Structures; 3 Defines Desired Culture; 4 Demonstrates Commitment to Core Values; 5 Attracts, Develops, and Retains Capable Individuals.
   - Strategy and Objective-Setting: 6 Analyzes Business Context; 7 Defines Risk Appetite; 8 Evaluates Alternative Strategies; 9 Formulates Business Objectives.
   - Performance: 10 Identifies Risk; 11 Assesses Severity of Risk; 12 Prioritizes Risks; 13 Implements Risk Responses; 14 Develops Portfolio View.
   - Review and Revision: 15 Assesses Substantial Change; 16 Reviews Risk and Performance; 17 Pursues Improvement in Enterprise Risk Management.
   - Information, Communication, and Reporting: 18 Leverages Information and Technology (the narrative list in the same document reads "Leverages Information Systems"); 19 Communicates Risk Information; 20 Reports on Risk, Culture, and Performance.
   [confidence: high] [source: 1]

8. The Performance component, as COSO describes it: risks are identified and assessed, prioritized by severity in the context of risk appetite; the organization then selects risk responses, takes a portfolio view of the amount of risk assumed, and reports results to key risk stakeholders. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

9. Risk response categories. "According to the COSO ERM Framework, risk responses fall within the categories of accept, avoid, pursue, reduce and share" (COSO/WBCSD, October 2018). The same guidance describes each:
   - Accept: take no action to change the severity of the risk. Appropriate when the risk is within risk appetite.
   - Avoid: remove the risk, for example by exiting an activity.
   - Pursue: accept increased risk to achieve improved performance, or convert risks into opportunities.
   - Reduce: take action to reduce the severity of the risk, typically when severity exceeds risk appetite.
   - Share: transfer a portion of the risk or collaborate externally. Insurance, outsourcing and hedging are standard examples.
   COSO's 2018 Compendium of Examples uses the same five responses in worked cases. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4]

10. Severity and appetite logic. COSO links response choice to severity relative to risk appetite. Accept applies when risk is within appetite. Reduce applies when severity is higher than appetite, reducing residual risk to within appetite. Accepted risks need monitoring of the assumptions behind the acceptance. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

11. COSO's 2018 ESG guidance (with WBCSD) notes that it can be applied by entities "using other risk management frameworks, such as ISO 31000". [confidence: high] [source: 2]

### ISO 31000:2018 (verified via the HM Treasury Orange Book where stated; otherwise medium)

12. Identity. ISO 31000:2018, "Risk management – Guidelines", is the second edition of ISO 31000 and replaced ISO 31000:2009. The UK adopts it as BS ISO 31000:2018. HM Treasury's Orange Book (May 2023) lists "BS ISO 31000:2018(E) – Risk management – Guidelines" as its first reference and says its principles-based approach is "informed by relevant standards". [confidence: high that the 2018 edition exists and is cited; medium for the February 2018 publication month, which ISO's site could not confirm in this research] [source: 5]

13. Definition of risk. ISO 31000:2018 defines risk as the "effect of uncertainty on objectives". The Orange Book uses the same wording: "Risk is the effect of uncertainty on objectives." It adds that risk is usually expressed in terms of causes, potential events and their consequences. [confidence: high for the Orange Book wording; medium-high that it is ISO's exact clause 3.1 text, consistent with well-established secondary sources] [source: 5]

14. Structure of ISO 31000:2018 (not directly verified from ISO; well established): clause 4 Principles; clause 5 Framework; clause 6 Process. The eight principles are: integrated; structured and comprehensive; customized; inclusive; dynamic; best available information; human and cultural factors; and continual improvement. These surround the purpose "creation and protection of value". The framework is built around leadership and commitment, with integration, design, implementation, evaluation and improvement. [confidence: medium] [source: 5 (consistent restatement), 6]

15. The ISO 31000:2018 process (clause 6; not directly verified from ISO; well established) runs: communication and consultation; scope, context and criteria; risk assessment (risk identification, risk analysis, risk evaluation); risk treatment; monitoring and review; recording and reporting. The Orange Book's own process (Part D) has the same shape: identification and assessment, then selection and implementation of risk treatment options, then monitoring, then reporting. It is surrounded by communication, consultation and continual improvement. [confidence: high for the Orange Book; medium for ISO clause numbering] [source: 5, 6]

16. Risk treatment options. The Orange Book (para D6) lists the options as: avoiding the risk by deciding not to start or continue the activity that gives rise to it; taking or increasing the risk in order to pursue an opportunity; retaining the risk by informed decision; changing the likelihood, where possible; changing the consequences, including planning contingency activities; and sharing the risk, e.g. through commercial contracts. ISO 31000:2018 clause 6.5.2 contains the same options plus "removing the risk source". [confidence: high for the Orange Book list; medium for the exact ISO wording and the additional "removing the risk source" item] [source: 5, 6]

17. The Orange Book (D7) states that selecting treatment options balances the potential benefits against costs, efforts or disadvantages, and that justification is "broader than solely economic considerations". D8 says treatment plans should specify the rationale, actions, accountabilities, resources, performance measures, constraints and timing. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

18. Supporting standards. ISO 31010 (risk assessment techniques) supports ISO 31000. The Orange Book cites "ISO 31010:2009". A 2019 edition, IEC 31010:2019, has since replaced the 2009 edition. ISO Guide 73:2009 provides risk management vocabulary. [confidence: high for the Orange Book citation; medium for the 2019 replacement] [source: 5]

19. Neither ISO 31000 nor COSO ERM is certifiable in the way ISO 9001 is. ISO 31000 is a "guidelines" standard. [confidence: medium (well-established; not verified from ISO site)] [source: 6]

### Mapping (analysis for the book, built from the verified items)

20. Response vocabulary crosswalk:

| COSO 2017 | ISO 31000:2018 / Orange Book | Project finance examples |
|---|---|---|
| Accept | Retaining the risk by informed decision | Residual merchant tail, minor O&M variances held in equity case |
| Avoid | Avoiding the risk (not starting or continuing the activity) | Rejecting unproven technology, exiting a sanctioned jurisdiction |
| Pursue | Taking or increasing the risk to pursue an opportunity | Merchant upside, repowering, sponsor taking development risk |
| Reduce | Changing the likelihood; changing the consequences (and removing the risk source) | Geotech surveys, proven EPC contractor, reserve accounts, spares |
| Share | Sharing the risk (e.g., commercial contracts, insurance) | EPC wrap, offtake contract, insurance, hedges, DFI guarantees, political risk cover |

[confidence: high for the vocabulary from sources 2 and 5; the mapping is the book's analysis]

## What went wrong or right, and why (well-established analysis only, attributed to named sources)

- COSO's own diagnosis (source 1) of the 2004 framework is that it was widely used but often misread: as a department, a risk list, or an internal-control exercise. The 2017 update repositions ERM around strategy and performance.
- The Orange Book (source 5) stresses that treatment choice is not only economic and that accepted risks require monitoring. In project terms, a risk allocated "on paper" to a weak counterparty is not shared in substance (link to Chapter 15's "allocation on paper versus in practice").

## Teaching angles by chapter (for each listed chapter: the transferable lesson and the angle to take, 2–4 sentences)

- **Chapter 15 (Analyzing, allocating and pricing risk):** Present the ISO process (context and criteria, identify, analyze, evaluate, treat, monitor, report) as the generic skeleton. Then show that the project finance method (identify, analyze, quantify, allocate, mitigate, price, monitor) is a specialization of it, with "allocate" made contractual. Use COSO's severity-versus-appetite logic to explain why lenders and sponsors, with different appetites, choose different responses to the same risk.
- **Chapter 16 (The mitigation toolkit):** Use the crosswalk table to classify every instrument in the toolkit. Insurance, hedges, guarantees and contracts are "share"; reserves and design changes are "reduce"; walking away is "avoid". Stress the Orange Book point that treatments have costs and need a plan with owners, resources and monitoring, which is the logic behind covenants and reporting.

## Do not state

- Do not quote ISO 31000:2018 clause text verbatim beyond "effect of uncertainty on objectives". The ISO text was not accessed. Paraphrase it.
- Do not state that ISO 31000 or COSO ERM is certifiable or mandatory for projects or lenders.
- Do not state COSO 2017's principle 18 title with certainty in a single form. The Executive Summary uses both "Leverages Information and Technology" and "Leverages Information Systems". Use the first and note the variant if needed.
- Do not attribute the 2004 COSO "eight components / ERM cube" or the four response categories (avoid, reduce, share, accept) to the 2017 framework. That is the 2004 framework, which was not verified in this research.
- Do not give an exact ISO publication day for ISO 31000:2018 (commonly given as February 2018; not verified here).
