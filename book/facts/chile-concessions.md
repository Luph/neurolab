# Chile's toll-road concession program and the least-present-value-of-revenue (LPVR) auction

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: the January 2008 LPVR auctions reported by Engel, Fischer and Galetovic; renegotiation data to 2007)

## Summary

Chile's Ministry of Public Works (MOP) launched its PPP concession program in 1993 with the El Melón tunnel. Between 1993 and 2006 it awarded 50 concessions: 26 roads, 10 airports, three jails, two water reservoirs, five public-transport infrastructure projects and four others. Roads made up 89 percent of the roughly USD 11.3 billion invested. Early road concessions were fixed-term and awarded on the lowest toll, with minimum revenue guarantees. Eduardo Engel, Ronald Fischer and Alexander Galetovic (EFG) of the Universidad de Chile argued that fixed-term franchises load traffic risk onto the concessionaire, invite lowballing and renegotiation, and need guarantees. They proposed the least-present-value-of-revenue (LPVR, also called PVR) auction. In it, the regulator sets a maximum toll, firms bid the present value of toll revenue they require, and the concession ends when that value has been collected, discounted at a rate fixed in the contract. The term therefore lengthens when traffic is weak and shortens when it is strong. Chile was the first country to run an outright PVR auction, for the Santiago–Valparaíso–Viña del Mar highway in February 1998. It used the method again in 2007–2008 for Route 160, the access road to Santiago's main airport, Melipilla–Camino de la Fruta and Vallenar–Caldera. EFG's later work shows that the program was nevertheless renegotiated heavily. By 2007 there had been 148 renegotiations, raising investment from about USD 8.4–8.5 billion to USD 11.3 billion. Most of the money was agreed bilaterally during construction, and statutory caps were routinely exceeded. EFG argue this let governments get round budget controls.

## Verified facts

**Program scale and history**

1. Chilean PPPs began in 1993 with the El Melón tunnel concession. Between 1993 and 2006 MOP awarded 50 PPPs: 26 roads, 10 airports, three jails, two water reservoirs, five public transportation infrastructure projects and four miscellaneous projects. Roads accounted for 89 percent of the USD 11.3 billion invested. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. EFG (2009) note that Chile's GDP was "currently about $160 billion". At completion of the main projects, the investments represented about 10 percent of GDP. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. EFG (2009, Table 1) give, for each concession, [year of the original concession contract, term in years] and [number of bilateral renegotiations, number of conciliations and arbitrations]:
   - Santiago–Los Vilos [1996, 23] [3, 7];
   - Santiago–Talca [1998, 25] [5, 10];
   - Autopista Santiago–San Antonio [1995, 23] [4, 4];
   - Santiago–Colina–Los Andes [1996, 28] [3, 2];
   - Santiago–Valparaíso–Viña [1998, 25] [6, 3]. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. Under the Chilean concessions law described by EFG, anyone can propose a highway project. If MOP approves it, the project is auctioned and the proponent receives a bid bonus. [confidence: high] [source: 2] See t-unsolicited-proposals.md.
5. Chile's Concessions Law caps concession terms at 50 years. [confidence: per t-ppp-frameworks.md] [source: t-ppp-frameworks.md]

**Fixed-term auctions and their defects (EFG, World Bank Viewpoint No. 112, May 1997)**

6. Two fixed-term designs were in use:
   - "the version now used in Chile": the regulator fixes the term and the franchise goes to the lowest toll bid;
   - the version used in Mexico: tolls are fixed and the franchise goes to the bidder asking for the shortest term. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. EFG argue that fixed-term franchises make the concessionaire bear traffic risk it cannot control. Five consequences follow:
   - a risk premium, which for Chile they estimate at about a third of the investment cost;
   - lender demands for government debt guarantees (Spain) or "generous minimum toll revenue guarantees (as in Chile)";
   - lowballing in the expectation of renegotiation;
   - the winner's curse;
   - inflexibility when tolls or capacity need to change. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
8. The same note cites international precedents. Three of four French franchises of the early 1970s went bankrupt after the oil shock. In Mexico, some toll-road franchise terms more than doubled, and the government spent USD 2 billion to rescue firms and their banks. [confidence: high, as EFG's statements] [source: 3]

**The LPVR mechanism**

9. EFG describe the LPVR auction in four rules:
   - the regulator sets a maximum toll;
   - the franchise goes to the firm bidding the least present value of toll revenue;
   - the franchise ends when the present value of toll revenue equals the winning bid;
   - revenue is discounted at a predetermined rate set in the contract, which should be a good estimate of the franchise holder's loan rate. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
10. EFG's worked example has two bidders. One estimates costs of USD 100 million and bids USD 112 million. The other estimates USD 99 million and bids USD 110 million. The second firm wins and operates until the present value of toll revenue reaches USD 110 million. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
11. EFG claim five advantages for LPVR:
    - the term adjusts automatically to demand, so the concessionaire's risk and required premium fall;
    - the winner's curse is less likely;
    - the winning bid sets fair compensation for early termination: the bid minus the present value of revenue already collected;
    - term extensions become impossible by definition, and a toll increase only shortens the term, so pro-concessionaire renegotiations become explicit wealth transfers;
    - tolls can be adjusted, for example for congestion, without disputes over compensation. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
12. EFG identify the main limitation of LPVR. It weakens incentives for demand-enhancing effort such as road quality, maintenance, toll-booth speed and accident clearance, because extra demand shortens the term. LPVR therefore needs regulators who set and enforce minimum quality standards. The method also needs low-cost verification of revenues, service quality and residual values. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
13. The academic statement is Engel, Fischer and Galetovic, "Least-Present-Value-of-Revenue Auctions and Highway Franchising", *Journal of Political Economy* 109(5): 993–1020, October 2001 (earlier NBER Working Paper 6689, August 1998). An earlier short version is "Highway Franchising: Pitfalls and Opportunities", *American Economic Review* 87(2): 68–72, May 1997. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 3]
14. EFG (2007) report that, depending on risk aversion and revenue uncertainty, their 2001 paper finds welfare gains from moving to the optimal variable-term contract of between 16 and 64 percent of the upfront investment. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

**Use of LPVR in Chile and elsewhere**

15. EFG (2007) state that Chile was the first country to use an outright PVR auction. In February 1998 a franchise to improve the Santiago–Valparaíso–Viña del Mar highway was awarded in a PVR auction. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
16. EFG (2007) report that PVR auctions were "used recently" in Chile for four roads:
    - Route 160 (February 2007);
    - the road to Santiago's main airport (December 2007);
    - the Melipilla–Camino de la Fruta highway (January 2008);
    - the Vallenar–Caldera highway (January 2008). [confidence: high] [source: 5]
17. EFG (2007) give earlier and parallel precedents:
    - The UK's Queen Elizabeth II Bridge (Thames) and Second Severn Crossing used franchises with a variable term, lasting until tolls repaid the debt. EFG call this "probably the first" PVR-like contract.
    - Colombia ran a flexible-term auction a couple of years before 1998, with firms bidding total, undiscounted income.
    - Portugal recently adopted flexible-term contracts for all its highway concessions, after shadow-toll obligations reached a projected EUR 660 million a year from 2008. [confidence: high, as EFG's statements] [source: 5]
18. For low-demand projects, EFG (2007) show that the same PVR logic works as a minimum income guarantee. The government pays the gap between the winning bid and collected revenue, and the concession runs indefinitely. [confidence: high] [source: 5]

**Renegotiations (EFG 2009, data on 50 concessions, 1993–2006, renegotiations to 2007)**

19. By 2007 there had been 148 renegotiations, about three per concession. They raised total investment by USD 2.8 billion, from USD 8.5 billion to USD 11.3 billion. The introduction gives the starting figure as USD 8.4 billion. Urban highways and jails had average increases above 50 percent. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
20. Of the 148 renegotiations, 78 were bilateral and the rest were appeals to arbitration panels. Bilateral deals accounted for 83 percent of the renegotiated amounts, USD 2.3 billion. Of that USD 2.3 billion:
    - 84 percent (USD 1.96 billion) was for additional works;
    - USD 360 million was additional payment for originally contracted works.

    MOP initiated 66 of the 78 bilateral renegotiations. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
21. Of the bilateral renegotiations, 51 of 78, with 78 percent of the amount, took place during construction. Combining both channels, 73 percent of the amount was renegotiated during construction. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
22. Only 35 percent of the USD 2.3 billion awarded bilaterally was paid by the administration that agreed it. For panel awards the figure was 61 percent. Across both channels, about 40 percent of the USD 2.8 billion was paid by the incumbent administration. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
23. Chilean law, as described by EFG (2009), caps additional investments during the concession at 15 percent of the bid cost estimate unless the contract says otherwise. Thirty-nine of 50 contracts set ad hoc caps: 5–15 percent during construction and 10–30 percent over the term. Sixteen concessions exceeded their construction-period caps, where aggregate caps of USD 367 million compared with USD 1.6 billion renegotiated, 4.3 times the limit. Eleven had exceeded their whole-term caps by 2007. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
24. Under the Chilean Concessions Law described by EFG, there is one three-member panel per concession. It conciliates first and then arbitrates. Only the concessionaire, not the government, can bring a dispute to the panel. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
25. EFG (2009) give two examples of renegotiation used to fund works off-budget:
    - After Santiago's 2001 floods, rainwater collectors worth hundreds of millions of dollars were added to three urban highway concessions during construction.
    - An 8 km San Antonio port bypass, valued at about USD 45 million, was added to the Route 78 (Santiago–San Antonio) concession and paid for by toll increases. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
26. EFG (2009) cite Guasch (2004) on nearly 1,000 Latin American concessions from the mid-1980s to 2000:
    - 30 percent were renegotiated, rising to 54.4 percent in transport and 74.4 percent in water;
    - the average time to first renegotiation was 2.2 years. [confidence: high, as cited] [source: 1]

## Financing and structure details

- **Revenue risk.** Under fixed-term contracts, lenders relied on minimum revenue guarantees. EFG cite Chile as giving "generous" ones. [source: 3]
- **Under LPVR, the debt tenor problem moves.** Revenue in present-value terms is more certain, but the maturity date is not. The franchise ends when the bid is reached, so lenders face timing risk rather than amount risk. This is an inference from the mechanism, not an EFG statement on any Chilean financing.
- **Early termination.** The termination payment is defined by formula as the winning bid minus the present value of revenue collected. This gives lenders a clear compensation amount. [source: 3]
- No tranche-level data on Chilean concession financings were verified for this sheet.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right.** Chile built a large interurban network quickly through a rules-based, legally grounded program. EFG call it one of "a handful of well established PPP programs" and cite Hemming (2005) and the IMF (2005). LPVR was put into practice, first in 1998 and then more widely in 2007–2008. [source: 1, 5]
- **Wrong.** EFG (2009) argue that renegotiation became a channel for incumbent governments to add works and push the bill onto future administrations and users. They point to the size of bilateral, MOP-initiated renegotiations during construction and to ineffective caps. Their remedy is to account for PPP investment, including renegotiated additions, as public spending when it is built. [source: 1]
- **Design trade-off.** LPVR reduces demand risk but weakens incentives to boost demand, so it needs quality regulation. [source: 3]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 57 (public-sector case for PPPs).** Use Chile's renegotiation data as evidence that PPPs can escape budget discipline. Three facts carry the argument: 73 percent of renegotiated amounts were agreed during construction, only about 40 percent was paid by the incumbent, and caps were exceeded 4.3-fold. Link this to fiscal accounting and contingent liabilities, and to EFG's argument for on-budget recognition. Contrast the program's reputation with this record.
- **Chapter 58 (procuring and designing PPPs).** Teach the bid variable as a design choice: lowest toll, shortest term, least subsidy, or least present value of revenue. Work the USD 110 million versus USD 112 million example. Show that the LPVR winning bid also fixes the termination compensation, and that a toll change only alters the term. Discuss why LPVR needs quality standards and revenue verification.
- **Chapter 79 (toll roads, bridges and tunnels; Case T ramp-up).** For ramp-up risk, LPVR is the cleanest structural answer: weak early traffic lengthens the concession instead of breaking the financing. Ask students to model the same traffic downside under a fixed-term contract with a minimum revenue guarantee and under LPVR. Compare who bears the loss, the effect on DSCR, and the maturity uncertainty lenders must accept.
