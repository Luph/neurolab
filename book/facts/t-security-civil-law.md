# Security agent structures: parallel debt, security trusts, OHADA security agent (2010), French fiducie and agent des sûretés (2017), UK project finance exception to administrative receivership (Insolvency Act 1986 s.72E), hardening periods

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: French Civil Code arts 2488-6, 2488-10 and 2488-11 as amended with effect from 24 May 2019; French Commercial Code art. L632-1 version in force from 15 May 2022)

## Summary

A syndicate of lenders that changes over time needs one person to hold the security for all of them. Common-law systems use a **security trust**: a security trustee holds the security on trust for the lenders. Many civil-law systems historically did not recognise the trust, and some required the secured creditor to be the creditor of the secured debt. Lenders in those systems relied on a **parallel debt**: a separate, independent debt owed by the borrower directly to the security agent, equal to what it owes the lenders, which the security secures. Several civil-law systems have since legislated a security agent. **OHADA's** Uniform Act on Securities (adopted 15 December 2010, in force 15 May 2011) creates an *agent des sûretés* who takes, registers, manages and enforces security in its own name for the secured creditors (arts 5–11). **France** replaced a weak 2007 provision (former Civil Code art. 2328-1) with a full *agent des sûretés* regime (Civil Code arts 2488-6 to 2488-12), introduced by Ordonnance n° 2017-748 of 4 May 2017 and in force from 1 October 2017. The rights and assets the agent acquires form a ring-fenced estate (*patrimoine affecté*) that is protected from the agent's own insolvency. The official report on the ordinance names English, German and Dutch parallel debt and the Anglo-Saxon security trustee as the tools French practice had been using instead. France also has the *fiducie* (Civil Code art. 2011, Law n° 2007-211 of 19 February 2007), a statutory trust-like transfer that can serve as security. In England and Wales, the Enterprise Act 2002 abolished administrative receivership for qualifying floating charges created on or after 15 September 2003 (Insolvency Act 1986 s.72A). There are exceptions, including **s.72E for project finance**: the project company must have incurred, or be expected to incur, debt of at least £50 million, and the project must include lender step-in rights. **Hardening periods** (suspect periods) decide whether security taken shortly before insolvency survives. In England a floating charge given to an unconnected party within 12 months of the onset of insolvency is invalid except for new value (IA 1986 s.245). In France the court can set the date of cessation of payments up to 18 months before the opening judgment, and security granted in that period for pre-existing debt is void (Commercial Code arts L631-8, L632-1). For restructuring regimes generally, see facts/t-insolvency.md. For LMA documentation, see facts/t-loan-docs.md.

## Verified facts

### OHADA: Uniform Act organising Securities (AUS 2010)

1. The OHADA Uniform Act organising Securities (*Acte uniforme portant organisation des sûretés*, AUS) was adopted on 15 December 2010 at Lomé, Togo. It was published in the OHADA Official Journal on 15 February 2011 and entered into force on 15 May 2011. It repealed and replaced the original Uniform Act of 17 April 1997. According to OHADA's own presentation, a key innovation was the creation of the *agent des sûretés*: a professional responsible for managing other parties' security, from creation to enforcement. Where to find the current rule: ohada.org, "Organisation des sûretés", with the signed text in the OHADA Biblio Numérique. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. AUS art. 5: any security or other guarantee of an obligation may be created, registered, managed and enforced by a financial institution or credit institution, national or foreign, acting in its own name and as *agent des sûretés*, for the benefit of the creditors of the secured obligations who appointed it. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
3. AUS art. 6: on pain of nullity, the appointing instrument must state:
   - the secured obligations (or, for future obligations, enough detail to identify them: debtor, place of payment, amount or estimated amount, maturity);
   - the identity of the secured creditors at the date of appointment;
   - the agent's identity and registered office;
   - the duration of its mission and the extent of its powers of administration and disposal;
   - how it reports to the creditors. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
4. AUS art. 7: the agent must state expressly that it acts in that capacity, and every registration of security made in its mission must show its name and capacity as *agent des sûretés*. Art. 8: unless otherwise agreed, the agent represents the creditors towards debtors, guarantors, security providers and third parties. Within the powers conferred, it may bring any action, including in court, to defend their interests; stating its capacity is enough. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
5. AUS art. 9: where creating or enforcing security transfers ownership to the agent, the transferred assets form a dedicated estate (*patrimoine affecté*) that the agent must keep separate from its own, and the same applies to payments it receives. Except for fraud, and subject to any tracing right (*droit de suite*), those assets can be seized only by holders of claims arising from their preservation and management, including if insolvency proceedings are opened against the agent. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
6. AUS art. 10:
   - The appointing instrument may allow the agent to delegate to a third party under its responsibility.
   - It may also provide for replacement if the agent fails in its duties, endangers the creditors' interests or becomes subject to insolvency proceedings.
   - Absent such provisions, creditors may apply to the competent court for a provisional agent or a replacement.
   - On replacement, all rights and actions pass to the new agent automatically, with no further formality.

   Art. 11: unless the instrument provides otherwise, the agent's liability to the creditors is assessed as that of a paid agent (*mandataire salarié*). [confidence: high] [source: 2]
7. The OHADA Uniform Act on collective insolvency proceedings (AUPC) was adopted on 10 September 2015 at Grand-Bassam, Côte d'Ivoire, published in the OHADA Official Journal on 25 September 2015 and in force from 24 December 2015. [confidence: high] [source: 3] (Its suspect-period rules were not checked against the text for this sheet; see "Do not state".)

### France: agent des sûretés and fiducie

8. Ordonnance n° 2017-748 of 4 May 2017 (*relative à l'agent des sûretés*) entered into force on 1 October 2017. It repealed former Civil Code art. 2328-1 and created a new title, "De l'agent des sûretés", at Civil Code arts 2488-6 to 2488-12. It was made under Law n° 2016-1691 of 9 December 2016 ("Sapin 2"), art. 117, and was ratified by Law n° 2019-486 of 22 May 2019 ("PACTE"), whose art. 206 amended arts 2488-6, 2488-10 and 2488-11 with effect from 24 May 2019. Where to find the current rule: Légifrance, Code civil, Livre IV, Titre III. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5, 6]
9. Civil Code art. 2488-6 (current version):
   - Any security or guarantee may be taken, registered, managed and enforced by an *agent des sûretés*, acting in its own name for the benefit of the creditors of the secured obligation.
   - The agent is the holder of the security and guarantees.
   - The rights and assets it acquires in performing its mission form an estate allocated to that mission (*patrimoine affecté*), separate from its own estate.
   - The qualities required of the security beneficiary are assessed by reference to the creditor of the secured obligation, not the agent. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
10. Arts 2488-7 to 2488-12 (summarised from Légifrance):
    - **2488-7:** the appointment agreement must be in writing on pain of nullity and specify the agent's capacity, the purpose and duration of its mission, and its powers.
    - **2488-8:** the agent must state its capacity expressly.
    - **2488-9:** the agent may take protective measures and file proofs of claim in insolvency proceedings without special authority.
    - **2488-10:** assets in the allocated estate can be seized only by holders of claims arising from their preservation and management, except for fraud, and insolvency proceedings against the agent do not affect that estate.
    - **2488-11:** the creditors may seek court replacement of an agent that fails in its duties or becomes subject to insolvency proceedings, and the estate passes to the successor.
    - **2488-12:** the agent is liable on its own assets for faults committed in its mission. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
11. The official report to the President on the 2017 ordinance said the old art. 2328-1 had several problems:
    - it was uncertain whether the agent was a mandatary, a trustee or a unique institution;
    - it covered only real security;
    - it required the agent to be appointed in the credit agreement itself;
    - replacement and the agent's standing to sue and file claims were unclear.

    As a result, practitioners preferred foreign mechanisms. The report expressly names parallel debt under English, German or Dutch law and the Anglo-Saxon security trustee. [confidence: high (official report, read via Légifrance summary)] [source: 5]
12. The *fiducie* is defined in Civil Code art. 2011, in force since 21 February 2007 and introduced by Law n° 2007-211 of 19 February 2007. It is the operation by which one or more settlors transfer assets, rights or security to one or more fiduciaries who hold them separately from their own estate for a defined purpose, for the benefit of one or more beneficiaries. [confidence: high for article and date; wording paraphrased] [source: 7]
13. French Commercial Code art. L632-1 (version in force from 15 May 2022) lists transactions that are void as of right if made during the suspect period. They include:
    - any conventional real security or conventional retention right granted over the debtor's assets for previously incurred debts;
    - any transfer of assets or rights into a fiduciary estate (*patrimoine fiduciaire*), unless it secured a debt incurred at the same time.

    Gratuitous transactions made in the six months before the date of cessation of payments may also be annulled (L632-1 II). [confidence: high] [source: 8]
14. French Commercial Code art. L631-8 (in force from 1 July 2014, as amended by Ordonnance n° 2014-326): the court sets the date of cessation of payments. It may move that date, but never to more than 18 months before the judgment opening the proceedings. Except in cases of fraud, it cannot be set before a final court approval (*homologation*) of a conciliation agreement under art. L611-8. Applications to change the date must be made within one year of the opening judgment. [confidence: high] [source: 9]

### United Kingdom (England and Wales): administrative receivership and hardening

15. Insolvency Act 1986 s.72A (inserted by the Enterprise Act 2002, s.250): the holder of a qualifying floating charge may not appoint an administrative receiver. This applies to floating charges created on or after 15 September 2003 (S.I. 2003/2095) and overrides any contrary agreement. It is subject to the exceptions in ss.72B–72GA. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
16. **Project finance exception, s.72E:** s.72A does not prevent appointment of an administrative receiver of a project company of a project that (a) is a "financed" project and (b) includes step-in rights. A project is "financed" if, under an agreement relating to the project, a project company incurs (or, when the agreement is entered into, is expected to incur) a debt of at least £50 million for the purposes of carrying out the project. [confidence: high] [source: 11]
17. **Step-in rights (Schedule 2A para 6):** a project has step-in rights if a person who provides finance for it has a conditional entitlement under an agreement to:
    - assume sole or principal responsibility under an agreement for carrying out all or part of the project; or
    - make arrangements for carrying out all or part of the project.

    Providing finance includes providing an indemnity. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
18. **Project company (Schedule 2A para 7):** a company is a project company if it does any of the following:
    - holds property for the project;
    - has sole or principal responsibility under an agreement for carrying out all or part of it;
    - is one of a number of companies that together carry it out;
    - exists to supply finance for it;
    - is the holding company of a company that does any of the above.

    A company that also performs other, unrelated functions is excluded. [confidence: high] [source: 12]
19. **Related exceptions:**
    - **s.72B (capital market):** the debt under the arrangement is at least £50 million and a capital market investment is issued.
    - **s.72C (public-private partnership):** the project is a PPP project that includes step-in rights. No monetary threshold applies.

    [confidence: high] [source: 13, 14]
20. **Preferences and transactions at an undervalue (IA 1986 s.240):** the "relevant time" is:
    - 2 years before the onset of insolvency for a transaction at an undervalue, or for a preference given to a connected person;
    - 6 months for a preference given to anyone else.

    For these periods the company must have been unable to pay its debts at the time, or become unable as a result. That condition is presumed for an undervalue transaction with a connected person. [confidence: high] [source: 15]
21. **Floating charges (IA 1986 s.245, which applies in England, Wales and Scotland):** a floating charge created at a relevant time is invalid except to the extent of new money, goods or services supplied, or debt discharged, at the same time as or after its creation, plus contractual interest. The relevant time is 12 months before the onset of insolvency for an unconnected chargee and 2 years for a connected one. For an unconnected chargee, the 12-month period applies only if the company was then unable to pay its debts or became so as a result. [confidence: high] [source: 16]

## Timeline

- 17 Apr 1997: first OHADA Uniform Act on securities.
- 19 Feb 2007: French Law n° 2007-211 creates the *fiducie* (Civil Code art. 2011, in force 21 Feb 2007). Former art. 2328-1 provides a limited security agent.
- 15 Sep 2003: IA 1986 s.72A–72H (Enterprise Act 2002) in force. Administrative receivership is abolished for new qualifying floating charges, with exceptions including s.72E project finance.
- 15 Dec 2010: OHADA AUS adopted (published 15 Feb 2011; in force 15 May 2011), creating the OHADA *agent des sûretés*.
- 10 Sep 2015: OHADA AUPC (insolvency) adopted; in force 24 Dec 2015.
- 9 Dec 2016: Loi Sapin 2, art. 117, authorises reform of the French security agent.
- 4 May 2017: Ordonnance n° 2017-748; in force 1 Oct 2017 (Civil Code arts 2488-6 to 2488-12).
- 22 May 2019: Loi PACTE ratifies the ordinance and amends arts 2488-6, -10 and -11 (in force 24 May 2019).

## Financing and structure details

- **Security trust (English law and similar):** the security trustee (usually the security agent bank) holds all security on trust for the finance parties from time to time. Transfers of loans do not require security to be re-taken. Proceeds go through the intercreditor waterfall. (Mechanism description; see the LMA forms in facts/t-loan-docs.md.) [confidence: medium (standard practice, not checked against a specific primary source here)]
- **Parallel debt:** the obligors promise to pay the security agent an amount equal to what they owe each finance party (the "corresponding debt"). Payment of either discharges the other. The agent takes security in its own name for its own claim and shares recoveries under the intercreditor agreement. French official materials confirm it was in use under English, German and Dutch law as a substitute where a statutory security agent was unavailable [source: 5]. Its effectiveness depends on the law of the security and the insolvency forum, so legal opinions address it case by case.
- **Statutory security agent (OHADA, France):** the agent holds the security in its own name for the creditors and keeps a segregated estate. Under the statute it can file claims in insolvency and be replaced by contract or by the court, without new security being created. In OHADA, the agent must be a financial or credit institution, national or foreign (AUS art. 5) [source: 2].
- **UK project finance:** a qualifying floating charge plus contractual step-in rights in the direct agreements, with debt of at least £50 million, preserves the lenders' ability to appoint an administrative receiver (s.72E), so lenders can block an administration and take control of the project. Below the threshold, or without step-in rights, the lenders' route is administration.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The French government's own report blamed the uncertain qualification, narrow scope and unclear replacement and standing rules of the 2007 art. 2328-1 for French practice preferring parallel debt and foreign security trustees. The 2017 regime was designed to fix each point: an allocated estate, a power to file claims, and court replacement [source: 5].
- OHADA's 2010 reform created the security agent as part of a broader modernisation of credit security, with simpler formalities and alternative enforcement methods, according to OHADA's own presentation [source: 1].
- English law kept administrative receivership for project finance (s.72E) and PPP (s.72C) so that lenders with step-in rights could continue to control enforcement of complex projects [source: 11, 13]. The policy rationale given in Parliament was not checked for this sheet.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 49 (legal diligence):** Legal DD must confirm, for each jurisdiction, how the security agent holds security: trust, parallel debt, or statutory agent. Opinions must cover whether that structure survives the agent's own insolvency and lender transfers. Use OHADA art. 6 (mandatory contents on pain of nullity) and French art. 2488-7 as checklists. If the appointment document is defective, the security can fail.
- **Ch 52 (security, accounts, waterfall):** Contrast the common-law trust with civil-law parallel debt, then show how OHADA (2010) and France (2017) legislated a statutory agent with a segregated estate. Show where hardening periods bite: security taken late, for example a top-up security package during a waiver, is exposed. In France it can be void if granted for pre-existing debt within the suspect period of up to 18 months. In England a floating charge for old money is vulnerable for 12 months, or 2 years if connected.
- **Ch 64 (distress and enforcement):** Section 72E is why UK project lenders insist on step-in rights and floating charges: it keeps administrative receivership available when debt is at least £50 million. When restructuring, teach the risk of taking new security for existing debt inside hardening periods. Under English s.245 a floating charge is good only for new value. Under French L632-1, security for prior debt in the suspect period is void as of right.

## Do not state

- That parallel debt is "recognised" or "unenforceable" in any particular jurisdiction (Germany, Netherlands, Poland, Luxembourg, OHADA states) without a jurisdiction-specific source. No court decision on parallel debt was verified for this sheet.
- The OHADA AUPC suspect-period length (often cited as up to 18 months before the opening decision) and its list of void transactions. The scanned text was not checked.
- Exact current numbering of French *fiducie-sûreté* articles (commonly cited as Civil Code arts 2372-1 et seq. and 2488-1 et seq.) after the 2021 security law reform (Ordonnance n° 2021-1192). Not checked.
- That the 2017 French ordinance came into force on signature: it applied from 1 October 2017.
- That s.72E requires debt to be drawn: the test is debt incurred **or expected to be incurred** of at least £50 million.
- Any statement that UK administrative receivership is fully abolished; it survives for pre-15 September 2003 floating charges and the s.72B–72GA exceptions.
