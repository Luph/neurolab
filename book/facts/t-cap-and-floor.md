# Ofgem cap-and-floor regimes: electricity interconnectors and long-duration electricity storage (LDES)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest events covered: Ofgem consultation on the Greenlink post-construction review, 18 September 2026; LDES Window 1 minded-to consultation closed 14 August 2026, final decision expected autumn 2026)

Cross-references: facts/t-frontier-data.md (items 20–21) already records the LDES Window 1 headline (16 projects, about 7.6 GW). This sheet adds the regime mechanics, the project list, the interconnector precedent and the Greenlink project-finance worked example. For CfDs and the nuclear RAB, use facts/t-uk-cfd-rab.md.

## Summary

A cap and floor is a regulated revenue band. The regulator sets, for each project, a minimum annual revenue (the floor) and a maximum (the cap) for a fixed regime period. The project trades in the market. If its revenue over an assessment period falls below the floor, consumers top it up through network charges. If revenue rises above the cap, the excess goes back to consumers. Ofgem built the regime for GB electricity interconnectors, using Nemo Link (1 GW to Belgium) as the pilot in 2013 and rolling it out in 2014. Interconnector regimes run for 25 years. By December 2021 Ofgem had awarded regimes in principle to nine interconnectors totalling 10.9 GW across two windows (2014 and 2016). Greenlink (500 MW, Wales–Ireland), which began operating on 29 January 2025, was the first GB cap-and-floor interconnector financed through project finance. Its floor was set from the lenders' debt service and DSCR at financial close. The UK government chose the same model for LDES in October 2024 because LDES has high upfront costs and uncertain merchant revenues, and no new LDES had been built in GB for about 40 years. Ofgem and DESNZ fixed the design in a Technical Decision Document (March 2025): a 25-year regime; a floor that recovers invested capital at a return comparable to the cost of debt; a soft cap that recovers capital at a fair equity return; eligibility of at least 8 hours' duration and 100 MW (stream 1, TRL 9) or 50 MW (stream 2, TRL 8). Window 1 opened in April 2025 and drew 171 applications, of which 77 were found eligible. On 26 June 2026 Ofgem said it was minded to award 16 projects totalling 7,645 MW (about 137 GWh), at the top of NESO's 2.7–7.7 GW advice range. The default rates of return are 4.47% at the floor and 7.48% at the cap, CPIH-real. Final awards are expected in autumn 2026.

## Verified facts

### A. General mechanism (Great Britain; current rules in Ofgem's LDES pages, the LDES Technical Decision Document and each interconnector licence's special conditions)

1. Ofgem's own description: when revenues fall below the floor, consumers top up the difference through a charge on energy bills; when revenues are above the cap, the extra money goes back to consumers through reductions in network charges. Ofgem presents the model as encouraging investment by guaranteeing revenues that offset high upfront costs and market uncertainty. As of 2026-10-03. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. The Energy Minister (Michael Shanks) wrote on 18 December 2025 that under the interconnector cap and floor regime "a floor has never been engaged, though a cap has", so consumers have shared in high revenues. The same letter says the Planning and Infrastructure Act 2025 (Royal Assent that day) directs Ofgem to implement the LDES scheme. [confidence: high] [source: 2]

### B. Interconnector regime

3. Ofgem proposed a cap and floor regime first for Nemo Link (1 GW, GB–Belgium) in 2013. In 2014 it decided to roll it out as an enduring regime for near-term interconnectors. Nemo Link served as the pilot. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. Window 1 opened in August 2014. Ofgem granted regimes in principle (Initial Project Assessment, IPA) to NSL (1,400 MW, Norway; National Grid and Statnett) in March 2015; to FAB Link (1,400 MW, France), IFA2 (1,000 MW, France) and Viking Link (Denmark; 1,000 MW at IPA, later 1,400 MW) in July 2015; and to Greenlink (500 MW, Ireland) in September 2015. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. By its December 2021 policy-review decision Ofgem had held two windows (2014 and 2016) and awarded regimes in principle to nine interconnectors totalling 10.9 GW. Before the regime GB had about 4 GW of interconnection, mostly merchant (for example BritNed, 1 GW, and East West, 0.5 GW). Ofgem judged the regime successful, kept the 25-year regime length, and decided to open a third, locationally targeted window in mid-2022 alongside a pilot for multi-purpose interconnectors (MPIs, which link offshore wind to two markets). [confidence: high] [source: 3]
6. The regime runs for 25 years from the Regime Start Date. A delay to commissioning beyond the approved date shortens the 25-year regime by the length of the delay (Window 1 condition). [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
7. Process stages: Initial Project Assessment (needs case: cost-benefit to consumers), Final Project Assessment (confirms the regime, assesses economic and efficient development, construction, operating, financing and decommissioning costs, sets preliminary cap and floor levels in a Cap and Floor Financial Model), then a Post Construction Review (PCR) that sets final levels from outturn costs. Annual reporting runs between FPA and PCR. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
8. The interconnector cap is a hard cap with an availability adjustment: up 2% if the interconnector meets its target availability, down 2% if not. The floor is "soft". Under the default regime set out in Ofgem's 2021 policy review (for future projects), revenue is assessed against cap and floor over five-year periods (one-year as an option), and a developer loses floor eligibility for any year in which availability is below 80%. An alternative option keeps the floor in low-availability years through a temporary consumer loan capped at four times the floor level, repayable before equity distributions. Cap and floor levels are calculated either on a notional cost of debt and gearing applied to 100% of the regulated asset value (default) or, under a "market approach", to match actual debt service at project-specific cost of debt and gearing, with a provision for a reasonable DSCR, under Ofgem oversight of the debt raise. The LDES regime adds carry-over between assessment periods, which the interconnector regime does not have (see item 17). [confidence: high] [source: 3, 6]
9. Greenlink worked example (project finance under cap and floor):
   - 500 MW HVDC link between Pembrokeshire (Wales) and Great Island, County Wexford (Ireland). Commercial operation began 29 January 2025. It is the fifth operational cap-and-floor interconnector after Nemo Link, IFA2, North Sea Link and Viking Link, and the first financed through project finance.
   - Jointly controlled by Equitix and Baltic Cable AB. Ofgem regulates the GB half (50%); Ireland's CRU regulates the other half.
   - Financial close 16 August 2022. Preliminary levels (2020/21 prices, GB share): cap £31.24 million a year; notional floor £15.58 million; "Actual Floor Level" £18.60 million.
   - The Actual Floor Level came from the project finance model using the final lending terms on the day of financial close. It has two components: (1) opex, replacement capex, decommissioning and tax, indexed to outturn RPI; (2) debt service at an agreed DSCR (redacted), indexed on a fixed RPI schedule so it stays fixed for the regime. It can only move down at PCR (to strip unspent capex, contingency or standby facilities). Gearing must stay at the level set at financial close (redacted).
   - If the Actual Floor exceeds the Notional Floor and consumers pay the higher floor, the licensee must repay the difference before any equity return, shareholder-loan payment or dividend.
   - Ofgem's minded-to PCR (consultation 18 September to 16 October 2026; decision aimed for December 2026): final cap £31.45 million, notional floor £16.16 million, Actual Floor £17.34 million (2020/21 prices). The licence allows one re-opener of opex after at least 10 years.
   [confidence: high] [source: 5]

### C. LDES regime (Great Britain)

10. DESNZ announced on 10 October 2024 (government response to consultation) that it would introduce an LDES cap and floor scheme with Ofgem as regulator and delivery body. The press release noted GB then had 2.8 GW of LDES across four pumped storage schemes, cited analysis that 20 GW of LDES could save the system £24 billion between 2025 and 2050, and noted NESO's estimate of 11.5–15.3 GW needed by 2050. [confidence: high] [source: 7]
11. Ofgem and DESNZ published the Technical Decision Document (TDD) on 11 March 2025. It states the LDES regime builds on but is not the same as the interconnector regime, and does not change approved interconnector regimes. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 8]
12. Cap and floor bases (TDD): the cap allows recovery of invested capital (debt and equity) plus a fair return if the asset performs well; the floor allows recovery of invested capital plus a return comparable to the cost of debt, for both debt and equity investors. Government chose this over a CfD or RAB because the gap between cap and floor keeps an incentive to earn market revenue efficiently. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
13. Floor-setting route: developers may let Ofgem set the floor administratively, or choose an optional "project finance" variation with a competitive debt-raising process, under which the floor is designed to meet debt obligations. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
14. Eligibility (Window 1): minimum 100 MW (stream 1, technology readiness level 9) or 50 MW (stream 2, TRL 8); at least 8 hours' continuous output at full power, maintained over the 25-year regime. The TDD notes only pumped storage hydro and lithium-ion were expected to be TRL 9 at the start of Window 1. The 2024 consultation had proposed 6 hours for both streams; the TDD raised the minimum to 8 hours. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
15. Regime duration is 25 years as standard. Shorter durations must be at least 20 years and are allowed only by exception. Cap and floor levels are fixed in real terms for the regime. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
16. The floor is "soft": a project must meet a minimum availability threshold each year (availability may cover charging and discharging) or lose the floor for that year. The cap is also "soft": revenue above it is shared between licensee and consumers by a sharing factor. This differs from the interconnector hard cap with ±2% availability adjustment. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
17. Assessment: revenue is compared with cap and floor every five years (or annually at the licensee's option) on an NPV-neutral basis, with carry-over between periods and a final true-up over the full regime. Excess revenue over the cap must be paid back the following year. All revenue sources count. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
18. Window 1 timeline: applications opened April 2025; 171 bids assessed for eligibility; 77 found eligible (September 2025, alongside the Financial Framework and Multi-Criteria Assessment Framework); four withdrew, leaving 73 assessed. Data submissions were due 18 November 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 9]
19. Default financial parameters (Financial Framework, as applied in the minded-to decision): 25-year regime, zero residual value, indicative default rates of return of 4.47% at the floor and 7.48% at the cap, both in CPIH-real terms. Projects made 50 "Regime Requests" (2 regime variations, 1 duration, 45 residual value, 2 combined). Ofgem was minded to reject both regime-variation requests, including one that sought an interconnector-style capital structure with an alternative DSCR and a higher inflation-linked floor, for lack of evidence that the default administrative floor ("ACOD floor") would not support financing. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
20. Minded-to decision (26 June 2026; consultation closed 14 August 2026; final decision expected autumn 2026): 16 projects, 7,645 MW. Multiplying each project's MW by its stated duration gives about 136.9 GWh (computed). Ofgem set capacity at the upper end of NESO's advice (2.7–7.7 GW of additional LDES needed by 2035) to allow for attrition. Awards will carry delivery conditions and milestones, and Ofgem may withdraw support if they are not met. A second window is to be decided by 2027. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
21. The 16 minded-to projects (technology, region, MW, hours): Loch Kemp Storage (PSH, N Scotland, 660, 22.3); Coire Glas (PSH, N Scotland, 1,440, 32); TeesCAES (CAES, NE England, 50, 30); Earba PSH (PSH, N Scotland, 1,800, 15); Field Netherton (Li-ion, N Scotland, 400, 16.3); Field New Deer (Li-ion, 400, 18.03); Field Rigifa (Li-ion, 200, 18.03); Field Fyrish (Li-ion, 200, 16.5); Field Long Stratton (Li-ion, E England, 400, 16.05); East Claydon Storage (Li-ion, E England, 500, 12); Ocker Hill BESS (Li-ion, W Midlands, 145, 8); Sundon Storage (Li-ion, E England, 500, 8); Drakelow (Innova) (Li-ion, W Midlands, 385, 8.7); Frontier Legacy (vanadium/zinc flow, N Wales, 65, 8); Springwell (Li-ion, E Midlands, 400, 11.1); Thornton BESS 2 (Li-ion, E Midlands, 100, 11.11). [confidence: high] [source: 9]
22. Project assessment weights (Multi-Criteria Assessment): benefit-cost ratio 40%; security of supply 19%; avoided renewable curtailment 15%; system operability 12%; wider social and economic impacts 8%; real-time flexibility 4%; option value 1%. [confidence: high] [source: 9]

## Financing and structure details

- Revenue stack under a cap and floor: market revenue (for interconnectors, congestion rent from price spreads plus capacity market; for LDES, wholesale arbitrage, balancing, ancillary and capacity revenue) is topped up to the floor or clawed back above the cap. Lenders size debt to the floor; equity is exposed to the band between floor and cap.
- Greenlink shows the floor can be set from the actual debt terms (Actual Floor Level based on debt service at an agreed DSCR), with a covenant that floor payments above the notional floor are repaid before any distributions. [source: 5]
- LDES offers two floor routes: an administratively set floor (rate comparable to cost of debt; 4.47% CPIH-real default) or a project-finance variation with a competitive debt raise. [source: 6, 9]
- Counterparty: there is no contract counterparty. The regime sits in the licence (special conditions), and payments flow through network charges, so the credit is the GB network charging system rather than a government-owned company (contrast LCCC for CfDs).

## What went wrong or right, and why

- Right: Ofgem (2021) credits the regime with taking GB from about 4 GW of interconnection to a further 10.9 GW operational, under construction or approved, and with attracting new entrants and different financing solutions. [source: 3] The minister's 2025 letter states no interconnector floor has been triggered. [source: 2]
- Design change for LDES: government and Ofgem moved from a hard cap with ±2% availability adjustment to a soft cap with sharing, because storage's behaviour in system stress differs from interconnectors and a hard cap removes incentives to stay available once revenue is above the cap (CEPA recommended a soft cap). [source: 6]
- Criticism: NERA (2026) argues Ofgem's multi-criteria assessment is opaque and leaves Ofgem and NESO high discretion, so outcomes may depend on the quality of evidence submitted as much as on the project. [source: 10]
- Attrition risk: Ofgem itself set capacity at the top of NESO's range because some large projects (especially pumped hydro) may not proceed. [source: 9]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Ch 19 (support schemes): place cap and floor between a CfD and a RAB. Like a CfD it is two-sided, but it caps and floors total revenue over a period, not price per MWh, and it leaves a band of merchant exposure on purpose. Use Greenlink to show how a floor set at debt service times DSCR turns a merchant asset into an investment-grade debt story, and the LDES 4.47%/7.48% real band to show what equity is buying.
- Ch 73 (storage, transmission, interconnectors): contrast interconnector (hard cap, five-year periods, no carry-over) with LDES (soft cap, NPV-neutral carry-over, availability-conditioned floor). Walk the reader through how a lender would size debt to the floor and why the availability condition makes O&M and availability guarantees the critical contract. Use the 16-project list to show the duration and technology mix (8 to 32 hours) and the 25-year tenor ceiling.
