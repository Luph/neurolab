# Argentina 2002: pesification of utility tariffs and the treaty claims that followed

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: discontinuance of the Enron resubmission proceeding, 19 July 2018; core coverage runs from the 1992 gas privatization to the US Supreme Court's BG Group decision of 5 March 2014)

## Summary

In the early 1990s Argentina privatized its gas and electricity utilities under licences tied to the 1991 Convertibility Law, which pegged the peso to the US dollar at one to one. For the gas transporters and distributors, the regime calculated tariffs in US dollars, converted them to pesos at billing, and adjusted them every six months by the US Producer Price Index (US PPI). Foreign investors bought in on that basis. From 2000 the government and licensees agreed to defer the PPI adjustments. In the 2001 crisis the government restricted bank withdrawals from 1 December 2001 (the "corralito"). On 6 January 2002 it enacted the Public Emergency and Exchange Regime Reform Law (Law 25,561). Article 8 cancelled dollar-adjustment and foreign-index clauses in public-law contracts, including public works and public services, and fixed the resulting tariffs in pesos at one peso per dollar. Article 9 authorized renegotiation. Article 10 barred the companies from suspending or changing their performance. With the peso soon trading at about three to the dollar, dollar-based returns collapsed. Foreign shareholders brought dozens of investment treaty claims, mostly at ICSID under bilateral investment treaties (BITs). The claims produced landmark and conflicting awards:

- CMS (2005) and Sempra (2007) rejected Argentina's necessity defence.
- LG&E (2006) excused Argentina for the period from 1 December 2001 to 26 April 2003.
- Ad hoc committees annulled the Sempra and Enron awards in 2010 over the tribunals' handling of necessity.
- BG Group's UNCITRAL award of USD 185 million was upheld by the US Supreme Court in 2014.

## Verified facts

1. Law 25,561, the Public Emergency and Exchange Regime Reform Law, was sanctioned and partially promulgated on 6 January 2002. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Article 8 of Law 25,561 provides that, from the law's enactment, dollar or other foreign-currency adjustment clauses, indexation clauses based on other countries' price indices, and any other indexation mechanism are void in contracts entered into by the Public Administration under public law, including public works and public services contracts. The resulting prices and tariffs are fixed in pesos at ARS 1 = USD 1. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Article 9 authorizes the national executive to renegotiate those contracts. For public service contracts it must consider five criteria: [confidence: high] [source: 1]
   - the impact of tariffs on the competitiveness of the economy and on income distribution;
   - service quality and investment plans, where the contract provides for them;
   - the interest of users and access to services;
   - the security of the systems;
   - the companies' profitability.
4. Article 10 provides that Articles 8 and 9 in no case authorize contractors or public service providers to suspend or alter performance of their obligations. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
5. As described in the CMS award, gas transportation company TGN's licence was granted for 35 years, extendable by 10. The claimant's account of the regime was that tariffs were calculated in dollars, converted to pesos at billing and adjusted every six months by the US PPI. Argentina disputed the legal nature and effect of these instruments. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
6. CMS Gas Transmission Company bought into TGN from 1995: 25% at first, then a further 4.42%, for 29.42% in total. CMS said it invested almost USD 175 million in TGN shares. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
7. The CMS award records that in 2000 the licensees agreed to defer the US PPI adjustments, and that these deferral arrangements were then embodied in Decree 669/2000. It also records what the Emergency Law did: [confidence: high] [source: 2]
   - ended the currency board;
   - devalued the peso;
   - ended the right to adjust tariffs by US PPI and to calculate tariffs in dollars;
   - redenominated tariffs in pesos at one to one.
   Decrees 689/2002 and 704/2002 of 2 May 2002 exempted gas exports and their transport tariffs from the conversion.
8. The US Supreme Court described the effect in BG Group: Argentine law changed the basis for calculating gas tariffs from dollars to pesos at one to one, while the market rate was about three pesos to the dollar. This turned MetroGAS's profits into losses. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
9. CMS v Argentina (ICSID Case No. ARB/01/8, US–Argentina BIT of 1991) was registered on 24 August 2001 and the award is dated 12 May 2005. The tribunal: [confidence: high] [source: 2, 3, 4]
   - found breaches of fair and equitable treatment (Article II(2)(a)) and of the umbrella clause (Article II(2)(c));
   - awarded USD 133.2 million, with interest from 18 August 2000;
   - required CMS to transfer its TGN shares if Argentina paid an additional USD 2,148,100 within one year;
   - rejected Argentina's state-of-necessity defence.
10. An ad hoc committee decided Argentina's annulment application in CMS on 25 September 2007. It annulled the umbrella clause finding for insufficient reasons but left the award's fair and equitable treatment finding and the compensation in place. [confidence: high for the date; medium for the summary of the holding] [source: 3, 4]
11. LG&E v Argentina (ICSID Case No. ARB/02/1) concerned gas distribution. The Decision on Liability is dated 3 October 2006, from a tribunal of Tatiana B. de Maekelt (president), Francisco Rezek and Albert Jan van den Berg. It held that Argentina was in a state of necessity from 1 December 2001 until 26 April 2003, and so excused from liability for that period. [confidence: high] [source: 5, 7]
12. The LG&E Award of 25 July 2007 awarded approximately USD 57.4 million plus interest. Annulment applications by both sides were discontinued on 20 February 2015. [confidence: high for the dates; medium for the amount] [source: 5, 7]
13. Sempra Energy International v Argentina (ICSID Case No. ARB/02/16) concerned gas supply and distribution. The award, with a dissent attached, is dated 28 September 2007 and awards Sempra USD 128,250,462. [confidence: high] [source: 8, 9]
14. An ad hoc committee decided Argentina's application to annul the Sempra award on 29 June 2010. A resubmission proceeding started on 12 November 2010 and was discontinued on 3 April 2015. [confidence: high] [source: 8, 10]
15. Enron v Argentina (ICSID Case No. ARB/01/3) concerned gas transportation. The award is dated 22 May 2007. An ad hoc committee decided on annulment on 30 July 2010. The resubmission proceeding started in October 2010 and was discontinued by agreement on 19 July 2018. [confidence: high] [source: 11, 12]
16. BG Group plc held a majority interest in MetroGAS, which held an exclusive gas distribution licence for Buenos Aires. BG Group arbitrated under the UK–Argentina BIT, with the seat in Washington, D.C. In late December 2007 the tribunal awarded USD 185 million. It found no expropriation but a denial of fair and equitable treatment. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 13]
17. The UK–Argentina BIT says that a dispute must first go to the host state's local courts. Arbitration is allowed if the courts have not given a final decision after 18 months. The DC Circuit vacated the BG award because BG had not complied. On 5 March 2014 the US Supreme Court reversed, in an opinion by Justice Breyer. It held that the local-litigation requirement is a procedural precondition to arbitration, mainly for the arbitrators to interpret, and that courts should review the arbitrators' interpretation with considerable deference. Chief Justice Roberts, joined by Justice Kennedy, dissented. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 13]

## Financing and structure details

- The licence regime, as claimed by investors and described in the awards: utility tariffs set in US dollars, converted to pesos at billing, and adjusted every six months by US PPI. Licences ran for 35 years, extendable by 10 (TGN). [source: 2]
- Investment vehicles: foreign strategic investors held minority or majority shareholdings in the Argentine licensees. Examples are CMS with 29.42% of TGN [source: 2] and BG Group with a majority of MetroGAS [source: 6]. These indirect shareholdings were the "investments" protected by the treaties. CMS's jurisdiction decision (17 July 2003) accepted that shareholders could claim in their own right. [source: 3, 4]
- The licensees' own debt is not documented in the verified sources, so do not describe it.
- Dispute resolution routes: ICSID under the US–Argentina BIT (CMS, LG&E, Sempra, Enron) and UNCITRAL rules under the UK–Argentina BIT (BG Group), with enforcement and set-aside litigation in the seat courts (US courts for BG Group).

## What went wrong or right, and why

- The CMS tribunal, as summarized by italaw, rejected necessity because Argentina's own policies had contributed to the crisis and other measures were available. [source: 4]
- The LG&E tribunal reached the opposite conclusion for a defined period of severe crisis. Argentina was excused from 1 December 2001 to 26 April 2003 but liable outside that window. [source: 5, 7]
- The split shows how much the outcome of treaty protection depended on how each tribunal treated necessity, under customary international law and under the BIT's non-precluded-measures clause (Article XI of the US–Argentina BIT). Two awards for claimants (Sempra, Enron) were annulled over this issue. No resubmitted award followed in either case. [source: 8, 10, 11, 12]
- The project-finance lesson is commonly drawn from Article 10 of Law 25,561: the law voided indexation but obliged licensees to keep performing. That left equity and creditors to absorb the currency mismatch while tariffs were renegotiated. [source: 1]
- BG Group shows the practical value of choosing the arbitral seat. Seated in Washington, the award was reviewed under US arbitration law, and the Supreme Court's deference to the arbitrators on procedural preconditions preserved it. [source: 6, 13]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 54 (governing law, disputes and investment protection):** Argentina is the standard case for how BIT protection works when a state rewrites a regulatory bargain. Teach the shareholder-standing point (CMS jurisdiction), FET versus expropriation (CMS, BG Group: no expropriation, FET breached), umbrella clauses (CMS, annulled), the necessity defence and treaty non-precluded-measures clauses (CMS versus LG&E), and annulment risk (Sempra, Enron). Use BG Group to show why the seat and pre-arbitration steps such as waiting periods and local-litigation requirements matter.
- **Chapter 59 (country, currency and payment risk):** Law 25,561 Article 8 shows that a dollar-indexed tariff is only as strong as the host state's willingness to honour it in a crisis. Indexation moved currency risk to consumers, and consumers have votes. Have readers model a gas distributor's dollar debt service when its tariff goes from USD 1 = ARS 1 to an effective USD 0.33. Then discuss what mitigates this: offshore revenue, hard-currency offtake, political risk insurance covering currency convertibility but not devaluation, and sizing debt for a devaluation stress case.
- **Chapter 60 (political risk and its protection):** Contrast what treaty arbitration delivered with what it cost: years of proceedings, mixed outcomes on necessity, annulments, and enforcement against a reluctant sovereign. Use the facts that Argentina could legislate pesification while ordering continued performance (Article 10), and that tribunals disagreed on whether a systemic crisis excused it. Together they show the gap between legal rights and recovering cash. Teach how PRI products split currency inconvertibility from devaluation, and why devaluation is usually not covered.
