# Eurotunnel and the Channel Tunnel: overruns, traffic shortfall, restructurings (1987-2007)

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: 8 April 2008, publication of 2007 results; core coverage ends with the 28 June 2007 implementation of the safeguard plan)

## Summary

The Channel Tunnel is a roughly 50 km fixed rail link between Folkestone (England) and Coquelles (France). Under the Treaty of Canterbury (12 February 1986) and the Concession Agreement (14 March 1986), the British and French governments let Eurotunnel design, finance, build and operate it at its own risk, with no government funds or guarantees. Financing in 1987 combined a GBP 5 billion bank facility, underwritten by about 50 banks and syndicated to more than 200, with staged equity issues. Construction by the ten-company TransManche Link (TML) consortium overran badly: about 80% in real terms for construction and 140% for financing costs (Flyvbjerg). Traffic and revenue came in far below the forecasts, which had overestimated the total cross-Channel market, and fare competition from ferries cut prices further (Anguera). The tunnel opened in 1994 with about GBP 8 billion of debt. Eurotunnel suspended interest payments in September 1995. A first restructuring, agreed in principle in 1996 and completed in April 1998, swapped debt for equity and extended maturities. A second came in 2006-2007 under the new French safeguard procedure (procédure de sauvegarde): it cut financial debt from EUR 9.073 billion to EUR 4.164 billion (about 54%) and refinanced the remainder with a long-dated term loan of GBP 1.5 billion plus EUR 1.965 billion. The tunnel is often cited as a project that succeeded technically and failed financially, at least for its original investors.

## Verified facts

1. The two governments signed the Treaty of Canterbury on 12 February 1986. It authorized private concessionaires to build and operate the Fixed Link. Article 1 says the link is to be financed without recourse to government funds or to government guarantees of a financial or commercial nature. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
2. The Concession Agreement was signed on 14 March 1986 for an initial 55 years. Amendments dated 29 June 1994 and 29 March 1999 extended it by 10 years and then by 34 years, so it now expires in 2086. The two governments approved the extension to 2086 on 19 December 1997. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 6]
3. For 2052 to 2086, the concessionaires must pay the two states a total annual sum equal to 59% of pre-tax profits, including all corporate taxes. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
4. The Railways Usage Contract (1987) required Eurotunnel to make half of the tunnel's capacity available to the British, French and Belgian railways. In return the railways paid a fixed charge, tolls based on traffic volume and a contribution to operating costs. A minimum charge level guaranteed Eurotunnel a level of cash flow for the first 12 years of operation. Grant (1997) says the contract was expected to provide 35% to 40% of revenues. The contract ends in 2052, the minimum usage charge period ended in November 2006, and the contract is governed by French law. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3, 5]
5. The promoters were ten construction companies and five banks. The ten contractors were five British (Translink) and five French (Transmanche) firms, and together they formed TML. The five banks were Midland, NatWest, Crédit Lyonnais, Banque Nationale de Paris and Banque Indosuez. These 15 founder shareholders put up GBP 47 million (Equity 1, September 1986). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
6. Equity was raised in stages. Grant converts at FFr10 = GBP 1. [confidence: high] [source: 1]

   |---|---|---|
   | Private institutional placement (Equity 2) | October 1986 | GBP 206 million |
   | Public issue (Equity 3) | November 1987 | GBP 770 million |
   | Rights issue (Equity 4) | November 1990 | GBP 566 million |
   | Rights issue (Equity 5) | May 1994 | GBP 793 million |
   | Total including smaller items | | approximately GBP 2.4 billion (GBP 2,434 million) |

7. The 1987 financing plan aimed to raise GBP 6 billion: GBP 1 billion of equity and GBP 5 billion of debt. The European Investment Bank (EIB) signed a co-financing agreement in September 1987. The loan agreements were completed in October 1987, conditional on the equity issue. A group of 50 banks underwrote the debt and syndicated it to more than 200 banks. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
8. Grant says the 1987 financing package carried a 25% cushion. Flyvbjerg quotes the public offering as telling investors that 10% "would be a reasonable allowance for the possible impact of unforeseen circumstances on construction costs." [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4]
9. Bank funds became available only after nearly all the equity had been spent. From October 1989 the banks had to waive a number of breaches of the Credit Agreement. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
10. A second financing round closed in November 1990. It comprised a GBP 1.8 billion funding-level bank loan and a GBP 300 million EIB parallel line, which together cleared the GBP 2 billion target, plus the Equity 4 rights issue. A nine-month standby equity underwriting was put in place in May 1990. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. In 1992 TML claimed approximately GBP 1.5 billion in additional construction costs. Until the dispute reached the arbitration tribunal, Eurotunnel had to pay TML GBP 50 million a month as interim funding. The main contractor claim was settled in April 1994. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. The construction contract sent disputes to a panel of experts and then to ICC arbitration in Brussels. When contractors threatened to stop work, the House of Lords held, in Channel Tunnel Group Ltd v Balfour Beatty Construction Ltd [1993] AC 334 (decided 21 January 1993), that the English action should be stayed in favor of the agreed procedure, and it did not grant the injunction. [confidence: high] [source: 9]
13. The service tunnel broke through on 1 December 1990, and the rail tunnels on 22 May and 28 June 1991. TML handed the system to Eurotunnel on 10 December 1993. Queen Elizabeth II and President Mitterrand opened the tunnel on 6 May 1994. Commercial truck shuttles started on 25 July 1994, Eurostar on 14 November 1994 and car shuttles on 22 December 1994. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
14. In 1994 the 1987 cost estimate of about GBP 4.8 billion (1985 prices) became a total cost of about GBP 9.5 billion. Flyvbjerg puts the real-terms overrun at 80% for construction and 140% for financing, with revenues about half of forecast. [confidence: high for overrun percentages; medium for the GBP 9.5 billion total, which depends on cost definitions] [source: 2, 4]
15. Debt at opening was approximately GBP 8 billion. Grant reports 12 loan tranches across currencies and loan types, and a 1994 senior debt layer of GBP 647 million added to GBP 6.8 billion of junior facilities. [confidence: high for tranche structure; medium for the GBP 8 billion round figure] [source: 1, 2]
16. Eurotunnel announced an 18-month suspension of interest payments on 14 September 1995. An independent arbitrator (mediator) was appointed in February 1996. In October 1996 the steering group representing a syndicate of 220 banks agreed in principle to a restructuring plan. Its terms were a debt-for-equity swap at 130p per unit that cut junior debt by GBP 1 billion, conversion of GBP 3.7 billion of junior debt into new instruments, below-market interest fixed for seven years, and existing shareholders keeping approximately 54.5% of the enlarged equity. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
17. The 1997 restructuring was completed in April 1998, and the related instruments (Participating Loan Notes and Stabilisation Notes) are dated 7 April 1998. [confidence: high for the date; the final terms differ in detail from the 1996 outline] [source: 3, 8]
18. On 18 November 1996 a fire in the England-bound tunnel, thought to have started on a truck, suspended freight shuttle traffic and restricted passenger services. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
19. Financial debt of the Eurotunnel group (TNU) stood at EUR 9.073 billion at 30 September 2006. Getlink's own history page rounds this to EUR 9.2 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 6]
20. Eurotunnel filed for the safeguard procedure on 12 July 2006, and the Paris Commercial Court opened it for 17 group companies by judgments dated 2 August 2006. The proposed plan went to creditors on 31 October 2006, with an addendum on 24 November. Creditor committees approved it on 27 November 2006 and noteholders on 14 December 2006, and the court endorsed it on 15 January 2007. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 6]
21. Most of the plan was implemented on 28 June 2007. Holders of 93.04% of TNU units exchanged them for shares in the new holding company, Groupe Eurotunnel SA (GET SA). Financial debt fell to EUR 4.164 billion, about 54% below the historical debt. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
22. The new Term Loan was signed on 20 March 2007 and drawn on 28 June 2007. It totaled GBP 1,500 million plus EUR 1,965 million (about GBP 2.84 billion), arranged by Goldman Sachs and Deutsche Bank, which Citigroup later joined. It had six tranches, all with semi-annual payments. [confidence: high] [source: 3]

   | A1 | GBP 750 million | 3.49%, RPI-linked | 2018-2042 |
   | A2 | EUR 367 million | 3.98%, French CPI-linked | 2018-2041 |
   | B1 | GBP 400 million | 6.63% fixed | 2013-2046 |

   The weighted average cost was estimated at 6.36%. Issue costs were EUR 69 million (1.6% of nominal). Full prepayment is mandatory on a change of control above 50%.
23. Junior creditors (Tier 3 debt and noteholders) received Notes Remboursables en Actions (NRS), notes redeemable in shares. NRS I carried 3% interest and was redeemable only in shares. NRS II carried 6% interest and was redeemable in cash at 140% of par or else in shares. The arrangers underwrote GBP 965 million so that Tier 3 holders could take cash instead of NRS. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
24. Several noteholders opposed the plan in the French courts, among them Elliott International LP and M.D. Sass funds. Their third-party oppositions were rejected on 15 January 2007 and their appeals on 29 November 2007. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
25. Excluding the exceptional restructuring gain, Groupe Eurotunnel reported a 2007 pro forma net profit of EUR 1 million, its first net profit. Its 8 April 2008 press release called it "historic." [confidence: high] [source: 7]

## Financing and structure details

- **Project company.** Twin structure. Eurotunnel PLC (UK) and Eurotunnel SA (France) were stapled as "units." The concessionaires were The Channel Tunnel Group Ltd (CTG) and France-Manche SA (FM), which were jointly and severally liable under the concession. [source: 1, 3]
- **Construction contract.** A design-and-build contract with TML. The widely cited structure has three parts: a target cost with sharing for the tunnelling, a lump sum for terminals and fixed equipment, and procurement items such as rolling stock reimbursed at cost plus a fee. This breakdown appears in teaching cases but was not confirmed against a primary document here (see Do not state). [source: 1]
- **Equity.** About GBP 2.4 billion across five issues plus warrants (fact 6).
- **1987 debt.** GBP 5 billion bank facility; about 50 underwriters syndicated to more than 200 banks; EIB co-financing.
- **1990 debt.** About GBP 2 billion more: a GBP 1.8 billion bank loan plus a GBP 300 million EIB parallel line.
- **1994 debt.** Grant's Table 3 shows:
  - GBP 6.8 billion of junior credit facilities;
  - GBP 647 million of senior facilities;
  - EIB GBP 300 million and ECSC GBP 200 million parallel loans;
  - co-financing from EIB (GBP 1 billion) and Crédit National (FFr 2 billion), secured by letters of credit under the junior facilities.
- **Agent banks in 1990.** Midland, NatWest, Crédit Lyonnais and BNP. [source: 1]
- **2007 capital structure.** Term Loan of GBP 1.5 billion plus EUR 1.965 billion, with tenors to 2050. Sterling was lent to CTG and euros to FM. The loan was securitised on 20 August 2007. NRS sat beneath it, plus warrants for unitholders and noteholders. [source: 3]
- **No state support.** The concession provides no state guarantee or subsidy (fact 1). The minimum usage charge in the Railways Usage Contract was the only floor under revenue.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Forecasts.** Anguera (2006) finds the forecasts "largely and systematically overestimated the total size and growth" of the cross-Channel passenger and freight markets, although the tunnel's market share was predicted accurately. That share was won only through a price war with ferries, so revenue fell far below forecast. He concludes that the British economy would have been better off without the tunnel. [source: 2, 5]
- **Cost and the debt trap.** Flyvbjerg (2014) cites overruns of 80% on construction and 140% on financing, revenues of half the forecast, and a negative internal rate of return of about minus 14.5%. He uses the tunnel to illustrate the debt trap, in which delay and overrun add interest that compounds the debt. [source: 4]
- **Governance at inception.** Grant (1997), Eurotunnel's corporate finance manager and later group treasurer, says the contractors and banks negotiated the construction contract and the credit term sheet among themselves in 1986. No strong representative of the future shareholders negotiated with them at arm's length. He also lists the causes of the overrun as:
  - tunnelling progress slower than expected, partly because the boring machines were imprecisely specified;
  - weak cost controls;
  - changes to the terminals and fixed equipment;
  - rolling stock cost seriously underestimated. [source: 1]
- **Safety-driven changes.** The Global Infrastructure Hub (GIHub) case study attributes part of the overrun to design changes made during construction after safety concerns raised by the Intergovernmental Commission (IGC). It suggests that agreeing the design with the IGC before construction might have avoided some of the difficulties. [source: 2]
- **Lender syndicate size.** Grant says the more than 200-bank syndicate, a by-product of the effort to show broad support ahead of the equity issue, made waivers and restructuring very hard to manage. [source: 1]
- **What went right.** The tunnel was built and has operated continuously since 1994. The GIHub interview attributes investor confidence through the crises mainly to the Railways Usage Contract, together with the Treaty and the concession. The 2006-2007 safeguard restructuring used a then-new French procedure (introduced in 2005) to impose a plan over objecting noteholders. [source: 2, 3]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 3 (How project finance evolved).** Eurotunnel was the defining test of fully private, no-recourse-to-government financing of a megaproject in the late 1980s. Use it to mark the turn from enthusiasm to discipline on demand-risk concessions. Lenders came to demand more conservative traffic cases, larger contingencies and simpler syndicates, and governments came to accept that some demand risk may need public support.
- **Ch 64 (Distress, restructuring and enforcement).** Eurotunnel shows the full sequence: waivers, an interest standstill, a mediated debt-for-equity swap, a second insolvency-law restructuring, and refinancing with very long-dated inflation-linked debt. Teach why a 200-plus-bank syndicate slows consent, how the safeguard procedure let a court bind dissenting noteholders, and how tranching (indexed, fixed and swapped floating) matched debt service to inflation-linked toll revenue. The safeguard procedure of 2006 has since been amended, so present it as historical and point readers to the current French Commercial Code.
- **Ch 79 (Toll roads, bridges and tunnels).** Use the tunnel for ramp-up and demand risk. Anguera's finding that market share was forecast correctly but the total market was overestimated, and that competitors cut prices, is the key lesson for Case T. Also cover the role of a minimum usage charge or anchor-user contract as a partial revenue floor, and the cost of design changes imposed by a regulator after financial close.

## Do not state

- That the 1987 bank facility was "GBP 5 billion from 220 banks at signing." The underwriting group was 50 banks, syndicated to more than 200. The figure of 220 refers to the 1996 syndicate.
- A precise "final cost" without a definition. Figures of GBP 4.65 billion, GBP 4.7 billion and GBP 9.5 billion circulate. They variously mean construction cost or total cost, and nominal or real values. State "approximately GBP 9.5 billion total by completion (GIHub)" only with that attribution.
- Specific traffic forecast numbers (for example "30 million passengers in the first year"). No primary forecast table was verified.
- The 1996 restructuring "junior debt of USD 8.7 billion." Grant prints a dollar sign, but the context suggests sterling. The figure is unconfirmed.
- The exact percentages of TML contract cost under the target, lump-sum and procurement categories, and the "GBP 60 million fee cap" on procurement items. These come from secondary teaching materials only.
- That shareholders were "wiped out" in 2007. Unitholders who tendered received GET SA shares and warrants. Their dilution was severe, but no verified dilution percentage was found.
- Any quotation from Sir Alastair Morton or other executives.
- The GIHub statement that TML "consisted of five banks and ten construction companies." TML was the ten contractors. The five banks were co-promoters of Eurotunnel.
