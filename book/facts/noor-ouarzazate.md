# Noor Ouarzazate CSP (Morocco): concessional finance, MASEN structure

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: KfW ex post evaluation, 2022, reporting Noor I output to 2021; core coverage 2009-2018)

## Summary

Noor Ouarzazate I (Noor I) is a 160 MW gross parabolic-trough concentrated solar power (CSP) plant with about three hours of thermal storage. It sits roughly 10 km north-east of Ouarzazate, Morocco, and is the first phase of the Noor-Ouarzazate complex of about 580 MW (Noor I-IV).

The plant was procured by the Moroccan Agency for Solar Energy (MASEN) as a public-private partnership. A private special purpose company, ACWA Power Ouarzazate (APO), was competitively selected to build, own and operate it. Under the World Bank's appraisal design, MASEN was to hold 25% of the equity.

The structure has three defining features:

- **MASEN as single borrower and on-lender.** MASEN borrowed concessional funds from a group of development finance institutions (DFIs) and on-lent them to the project company as its sole lender. The DFIs were the World Bank (IBRD), the Clean Technology Fund (CTF), the African Development Bank (AfDB), the European Investment Bank (EIB), the Agence Française de Développement (AFD), KfW and the EU Neighbourhood Investment Facility (NIF).
- **MASEN as single buyer.** MASEN buys the plant's output under a 25-year PPA at the bid price and resells it to the national utility ONEE at the lower high-voltage tariff.
- **The State covers the gap.** The Moroccan Government is committed by convention to cover the difference between the two prices.

The World Bank's evaluators say that capital costs bid were about 30% below pre-tender estimates. The plant began operating in early 2016, about a year late. Output has been above target every year, averaging 424 GWh a year in 2016-2021 against a 370 GWh target. The World Bank's appraisal estimated that the State would bear a revenue gap of about USD 60 million a year.

## Verified facts

1. Noor I's project development objective was "to support Morocco in the development of the 500 Megawatt Ouarzazate solar power plant by financing the first phase (160 Megawatt gross) through a public private partnership (PPP)". The words are quoted from the Loan Agreement as reproduced by the World Bank's Independent Evaluation Group (IEG). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
2. Morocco's 2009 energy strategy set a target of 42% renewable share of installed capacity by 2020. The Moroccan Solar Plan (Noor) targeted 2,000 MW of solar by 2020 and 6,000 MW by 2030. MASEN was created in March 2010 under Law 57-09. Its equal shareholders are the State, ONEE, Fonds Hassan II and the Société d'Investissements Energétiques (SIE). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
3. Technology and footprint: [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
   - parabolic trough with about three hours of molten-salt storage, so that it can serve the evening peak;
   - wet cooling, with water drawn from the El Mansour Eddahbi reservoir;
   - about 450 ha of the 3,043 ha acquired for the complex.
4. The World Bank board approved the IBRD loan (USD 200 million) and the CTF loan (USD 97 million) to MASEN, both with a guarantee from the Kingdom of Morocco, on 17 November 2011. The loans became effective in June 2013. IEG attributes the roughly 18-month delay to the "several dozens" of legal agreements and opinions the PPP required. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4]
5. Terms of the two World Bank Group loans: [confidence: high] [source: 4]
   - CTF loan: 40-year maturity, 10-year grace, 0.25% annual service charge and 0.25% one-time management fee;
   - IBRD loan: Fixed Spread Loan, 80% in euros and 20% in US dollars, 30-year maturity and 5.5-year grace.

   MASEN was to pass the CTF terms through to the project company in full, so that the project received the whole CTF subsidy.
6. The appraisal financing plan (PAD, October 2011) totalled about USD 1,427 million, by source: [confidence: high] [source: 4]

   | Source | USD million |
   |---|---|
   | AfDB | 245 (EUR 200 million) |
   | AFD | 123 (EUR 100 million) |
   | KfW | 123 (EUR 100 million) |
   | EIB | 197 |
   | CTF | 197 (97 through IBRD, 100 through AfDB) |
   | IBRD cost-mitigation component | 200 |
   | EC/NIF grant | 37 |
   | PPP partners and MASEN | 379 (private consortium equity 253, MASEN/Government grant from the Fonds de Développement de l'Energie 126) |

   The PAD also states that the CTF has a 75% grant element.
7. The SPC was to be financed 70% debt and 30% equity. IFIs were to fund 100% of the SPC's debt and part of MASEN's equity, with tenors of 20 to 40 years, and MASEN was to on-lend the IFI funds to the SPC. MASEN (through MASEN Capital) was to own 25% of the SPC and the private partner 75%. [confidence: high for the design; actual final shareholdings not independently verified] [source: 4]
8. The PPA and offtake chain works in three steps: [confidence: high] [source: 4, 3]
   - MASEN signs a PPA with the SPC at the bid cost of CSP generation;
   - MASEN signs a power sales agreement (PSA) with ONEE at ONEE's high-voltage tariff;
   - the State compensates MASEN for the "differential cost" under a General Convention of 26 October 2010 and a project-specific Convention.

   The General Convention itself commits the State only to maintaining MASEN's financial equilibrium. ONEE is mandated to take all the power. The PPA term is 25 years.
9. At appraisal, the World Bank estimated the following. The levelised cost of energy (LCOE) would be 2-3 times MASEN's selling price to ONEE. The gap would be covered about 30% by IFI concessional financing and 70% by the Government. The Government's contribution would be about USD 60 million a year over the 25-year PPA, a gross cumulative total above USD 1 billion, or about USD 800 million net of avoided fuel subsidies, taxes and dividends. [confidence: high, as appraisal estimates] [source: 4]
10. The IBRD USD 200 million "Solar Incremental Cost Support" (cost-mitigation) component was designed as a contingent credit line. It would partly cover the gap between MASEN's purchase price and its sale price to ONEE when the Government chose not to use budget funds. It was cancelled in the restructuring of 19 May 2015, after only about USD 0.5 million had been disbursed, and replaced with the same support under the new Noor-Ouarzazate Complex loan (P131256). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
11. In 2018 the World Bank reported two developments. MASEN had used revenue from its equity stakes and fees on common services to help cover the gap. As a result, USD 100 million of the replacement cost-mitigation component was not needed and was reallocated to Noor Midelt. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
12. The project company was ACWA Power Ouarzazate (APO), privately owned and led by Saudi Arabia's ACWA Power. It was selected in a two-stage tender (prequalification completed in December 2010, four consortia shortlisted) using World Bank procurement rules. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4, 5]
13. Cost results: [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 5]
    - capital costs bid were about 30% below estimates at the start of procurement (World Bank 2014);
    - capex was USD 750 million against a pre-bid expectation of USD 931 million (IEG);
    - KfW reports actual cost of EUR 633.6 million against EUR 750 million planned (-16%).
14. CTF support for Noor I was about USD 1,231 per kW (USD 197 million over 160 MW). For Noor II and III the figure fell to about USD 680 per kW. [confidence: high] [source: 5]
15. Private sector investment in Noor I through the PPP was just under USD 126 million, according to KfW. IEG gives USD 126 million, of which 80% was debt and 20% equity. [confidence: medium, since the figure's scope is unclear] [source: 1, 2]
16. Commissioning and output: [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
    - KfW gives initial COD as 20 January 2016 and final COD as 30 November 2018, about a year later than planned;
    - generation was 400 GWh in 2016 and 414 GWh in the second year, against a 370 GWh target;
    - average output in 2016-2021 was 424 GWh a year, with 440 GWh in 2021.
17. The economic rate of return was re-estimated at 3.4% at completion, against 0.9% at appraisal. Those calculations used electricity values of US cents 13.8/kWh at peak and 9.7/kWh off-peak. IEG notes that CSP "was not the least-cost solution". [confidence: high] [source: 1]
18. Local content reached 32% against a 30% commitment. More than 1,900 workers were on site at peak, about 1,500 of them Moroccan. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
19. KfW rates sustainability as limited because water losses at the El Mansour Eddahbi reservoir have increased, and it recommends an emergency concept. Noor II and III switched to dry (air) cooling. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
20. IEG rated the outcome Moderately Satisfactory, against the ICR's own rating of Highly Satisfactory. KfW rated the project 2 (successful). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]

## Financing and structure details

- **Borrower.** MASEN, with a guarantee from the Kingdom of Morocco, borrowed from each IFI and on-lent to APO as APO's sole lender, blending tenors of 20 to 40 years. This "single-borrower" design let IFIs lend on sovereign-backed terms while the plant stayed privately owned and operated.
- **Concessional layers.** These were the CTF (40 years at 0.25%), the NIF grant through the EIB, and the public windows of AfDB, AFD, KfW and the EIB. The CTF was channelled half through IBRD and half through AfDB.
- **Revenue chain.** APO sells to MASEN under the PPA at the bid price. MASEN sells to ONEE under the PSA at the high-voltage tariff. The gap is covered by the State under the Conventions, with an IBRD contingent loan available as a backstop.
- **Risk allocation (Climate Policy Initiative).** The developer bears construction, operating and production risk. The State, through MASEN, bears electricity market (revenue) risk. The IFIs mitigate financing risk.
- **Shareholding.** MASEN was to hold 25% via MASEN Capital and the private consortium 75%, at a gearing of about 70:30.

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right: price discovery through concessional terms announced up front.** The Climate Policy Initiative (Falconer and Frisari) notes that donor coordination let MASEN tell bidders in advance what terms and cost of capital they would get. That competition produced bids in line with or below projections. CPI estimated that concessional finance cut levelised costs by about 25-30%. IEG and the World Bank cite capex 30% below estimates.
- **Right: donor harmonisation.** KfW describes a steering committee of financiers that standardised awarding procedures, so MASEN ran one process rather than one per donor. IEG's lessons stress the State's absorption of technology and financial risk and MASEN's parastatal role.
- **Mixed: cost to the State.** By appraisal estimates, CSP needed a recurring subsidy, and IEG notes it was not the least-cost option. The 2018 World Bank paper reports the gap was smaller than feared at appraisal. The current fiscal cost was not verified (see Do not state).
- **Problems: delay and water.** COD came about 12 months late because of complex awards, permits for heavy transport, and weather (KfW). The wet-cooling design's reliance on a shrinking reservoir is the main sustainability concern.

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Ch 34 (blended, concessional, local-currency finance).** Noor I is the archetype of public-entity on-lending of blended concessional money. Build a simple stack showing CTF at 40 years and 0.25%, IFI public-window loans and an NIF grant passing through MASEN. Show how each layer cuts the tariff the bidder needs. Then show the contingent IBRD cost-mitigation line as a liquidity backstop for the State's gap-funding promise, and why it was barely drawn.
- **Ch 70 (onshore wind and solar).** Use Noor I to teach why CSP with storage was chosen in 2011 for evening-peak value, and how the economics later lost to PV plus storage (KfW and IEG on cost). Use it also to show a single-buyer, government-agency offtake model with a revenue gap the State absorbs. Contrast the subsidy-gap structure with a merchant or corporate PPA solar project. Note the water-use lesson that led to dry cooling at Noor II and III.

## Do not state

- The Noor I tariff. "MAD 1.62/kWh" or "USD 0.19/kWh" is widely repeated but was not verified against a primary source here.
- Total cost figures such as "USD 1.3 billion" or "USD 9 billion" stated as actual cost. USD 1.3-1.4 billion was the appraisal estimate; the actual cost was about EUR 634 million (KfW) or USD 853 million for component 1 (IEG).
- Specific EPC contractor names (for example TSK, Acciona, Sener), consortium partners other than ACWA Power, and the financial close date. None were verified from primary sources here.
- MASEN's annual losses or deficit on Noor, or a Court of Accounts finding. Not verified.
- IEG's disbursement percentages for KfW. Its figures (USD 103 million of USD 136 million described as "over 98 percent") are internally inconsistent.
- That Noor I was "the world's largest" CSP plant. The complex is often described that way, but this was not verified.
