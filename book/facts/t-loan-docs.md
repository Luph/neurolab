# LMA, LSTA and APLMA documentation relevant to project finance

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: LSTA Model Credit Agreement Provisions update of 21 July 2026)

## Summary

Three trade associations produce the template loan documents that most cross-border project financings start from. The Loan Market Association (LMA) was founded in London in December 1996. It covers Europe, the Middle East and Africa and publishes English-law recommended forms. The Loan Syndications and Trading Association (LSTA) was founded in New York in 1995. It publishes New York-law Model Credit Agreement Provisions (MCAPs) and trading documents. The Asia Pacific Loan Market Association (APLMA) was founded in 1998 and is based in Hong Kong. It publishes Asian templates, including Singapore-law and Hong Kong-law forms. None of the three publishes a full recommended-form project finance facility agreement or common terms agreement (CTA) for general use. Project finance documents are bespoke and law-firm drafted. They borrow the "boilerplate" (interest mechanics, tax gross-up, increased costs, agency, transfers, defaulting lender, sanctions) from the LMA or LSTA forms and add project-specific provisions: conditions precedent tied to construction, completion tests, accounts and the waterfall, reserve accounts, insurance, and intercreditor voting.

The closest thing to a standard project finance CTA is the Singapore-law core suite that Infrastructure Asia launched on 31 December 2020 with APLMA support: a term sheet, a mandate letter and a CTA. Roughly half of each is standardized. The three associations jointly publish the market's sustainable lending standards: the Green, Social and Sustainability-Linked Loan Principles, last updated 26 March 2025, and a Guide to Transition Loans issued 16 October 2025. These standards now shape many renewable and transition-project loans. LIBOR ended completely on 30 September 2024, so new project loans reference risk-free rates: compounded or term SOFR for USD, compounded SONIA for GBP, and EURIBOR, which continues, for EUR.

## Verified facts

### The associations

1. **LMA founding and membership.** The LMA was founded in December 1996 by banks active in the European syndicated loan market. Its stated aim is to promote liquidity in the primary and secondary loan markets by developing standard documentation and codes of market practice. In October 2026 its website stated it had "over 880 members across 69 jurisdictions." Members include banks, non-bank investors, law firms, rating agencies and service providers. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. **LSTA founding.** The LSTA was incorporated in New York on 26 December 1995 as the Debt Traders Association, Inc. It changed its name to the Loan Syndications and Trading Association, Inc. on 28 June 1996. Members include banks, funds, law firms and broker-dealers. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4]
3. **APLMA founding.** The APLMA was founded in 1998 by 15 major international banks to promote the syndicated loan market in Asia Pacific. It is based in Hong Kong. It reports more than 400 member institutions and has offices or committees in Australia, Singapore and several other markets. [confidence: medium (association self-description via secondary profiles)] [source: 5]

### What each association publishes (where the current forms are found)

4. **LMA document families.** The LMA's documentation hub, found under "Documents & Guidelines" on lma.eu.com and restricted to members, groups its documents into these categories:
   - Africa (excluding South Africa) and South Africa
   - commodities finance
   - developing markets
   - export finance
   - fund finance
   - infrastructure finance
   - investment grade
   - leveraged
   - real estate finance
   - sustainable finance
   - secondary debt trading
   - restructuring
   - recommended-form intercreditor agreements

   "Project finance" is not a separate document category. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
5. **LMA infrastructure guidance.** The LMA has published a "Guidance Note: Introduction to infrastructure financing – Position Within the Debt Spectrum and Key Features." It covers asset classes, how infrastructure finance relates to leveraged and acquisition finance, common documentation formats, and key covenant, security and intercreditor features. It is guidance, not a recommended-form facility agreement. Its publication date was not confirmed. [confidence: medium] [source: 7]
6. **LMA forms adjacent to project finance.** The LMA has published:
   - a recommended form of single currency term facility agreement for pre-export finance (PXF), announced 20 September 2012;
   - a recommended form of facility agreement for ECA-supported export finance buyer credit transactions, announced 25 April 2018;
   - a real estate finance facility agreement, launched April 2012.

   Project lawyers often draw on the ECA and developing-markets forms for ECA-covered tranches of project financings. [confidence: medium (dates from LMA press-release listings via search)] [source: 8, 9]
7. **LMA Africa multi-jurisdictional exposure draft.** In mid-2024 the LMA published an English-law, USD, Term SOFR facility agreement as an exposure draft, not yet a recommended form. It is built for an African holding company, typically Mauritian, with borrowers or guarantors in Ghana, Kenya, Mauritius, Nigeria, Tanzania, Uganda and Zambia. The LMA said it was the first time in its history that it had included so many jurisdiction-specific provisions in a single document. [confidence: high] [source: 10]
8. **LMA risk-free-rate forms.** The LMA published recommended forms of multicurrency term and revolving facilities agreements referencing compounded RFRs on 30 March 2021, with single-currency compounded-rate forms for sterling, USD and Swiss francs. It published an exposure draft single-currency Term SOFR facilities agreement for developing markets on 27 October 2021. [confidence: high] [source: 11, 12]
9. **End of LIBOR.** The final synthetic 1-, 3- and 6-month USD LIBOR settings were published for the last time on 30 September 2024. That marked the end of all LIBOR settings. Any legacy project loan still referencing LIBOR needed a contractual fallback or amendment. [confidence: high] [source: 13, 14]
10. **LSTA MCAPs.** The LSTA's Model Credit Agreement Provisions are standard New York-law provisions for incorporation into credit agreements, used mainly in leveraged finance. They cover tax, yield protection, agency, assignments, defaulting lenders and disqualified institutions. A revised version in May 2022 introduced the "LSTA DQ Structure." The LSTA's site lists an updated MCAPs release dated 21 July 2026, described as covering core provisions commonly incorporated into leveraged finance credit agreements. Current versions are on lsta.org and restricted to members. [confidence: high for 2022; medium for the July 2026 item] [source: 4, 15]
11. **LSTA LatAm MCAPs.** On 11 February 2025 the LSTA published updated Model Credit Agreement Provisions for Latin American cross-border transactions, including SOFR language. These are relevant to New York-law project and corporate loans into Latin America. [confidence: high] [source: 16]
12. **Infrastructure Asia / APLMA project finance suite.** Infrastructure Asia, a Singapore government platform, led a project to produce standardized core project finance loan documents. Clifford Chance and Allen & Gledhill drafted them with APLMA support, using APLMA's Singapore-law templates as the base. The suite comprises a term sheet, a mandate letter and a common terms agreement. It was completed and launched on 31 December 2020. About 50% of the content (the common clauses) is standardized and the other 50% is left for project-specific negotiation. [confidence: high] [source: 17, 18]
13. **APLMA SOFR and country templates.** APLMA has published SOFR-based syndicated facility templates for Asia Pacific. In September 2021 it launched an English-law facilities agreement template for offshore loans to Indonesian borrowers. [confidence: medium] [source: 19, 20]

### Sustainable lending standards (joint LMA / APLMA / LSTA)

14. **Green Loan Principles (GLP).** The LMA and APLMA launched the GLP on 21 March 2018, with ICMA support. The LSTA joined in an extended version published 11 December 2018. Current texts are on the websites of all three associations. [confidence: high] [source: 21]
15. **2025 update.** On 26 March 2025 the three associations published updated Green Loan Principles, Social Loan Principles and Sustainability-Linked Loan Principles, with guidance. Changes include:
    - clearer "shall" (mandatory) versus "should" (recommended) language, with annual use-of-proceeds reporting to lenders made a requirement;
    - deletion of the grandfathering for transactions completed before 9 March 2023;
    - recognition of "Sustainability Loans" (intentionally mixed green and social projects) and blue loans;
    - a "pure play" definition (90% or more of revenues or assets in green activities), with a statement that pure-play status does not by itself make a loan green;
    - additions to the eligible categories, such as carbon capture and green hydrogen, and confirmation that eligible expenditure includes capex and opex.

    [confidence: high] [source: 22, 23]
16. **Transition loans.** On 16 October 2025 the LMA, APLMA and LSTA published a Guide to Transition Loans. It sets five core components:
    - an entity-level transition strategy;
    - use of proceeds for eligible transition projects;
    - project evaluation and selection;
    - management of proceeds;
    - reporting.

    It targets hard-to-abate sectors. [confidence: high] [source: 24]

### Market norms (indicative; vary by deal)

17. **Majority lender thresholds.** LMA-form agreements traditionally define "Majority Lenders" as lenders with more than 66⅔% of total commitments. Lower thresholds such as 50% appear in some markets and products. New York-law syndicated credit agreements commonly use a "Required Lenders" threshold of more than 50%. Project finance intercreditor agreements frequently set their own thresholds, often with unanimity or super-majority for "entrenched" matters. Typical entrenched matters are economics, maturity, release of security, and changes to the waterfall. [confidence: medium (LMA figure documented; US and project norms are market practice rather than a single verified rule)] [source: 25, 26]
    - Drivers: size and diversity of the lender group, presence of ECAs and DFIs (which often require their own consent rights), the relative power of sponsors, and whether bonds and loans share a common intercreditor.

## Financing and structure details

The typical project finance document set is assembled from the following:

- a mandate letter and term sheet;
- commitment letters and fee letters;
- a common terms agreement, holding the shared representations, conditions precedent, covenants, events of default and project accounts provisions;
- individual facility agreements for each tranche: commercial bank, ECA-covered, DFI, Islamic and others;
- an intercreditor agreement, often merged with the CTA;
- an accounts agreement;
- security documents;
- direct agreements with key counterparties;
- ISDA hedging documents, typically under the 2002 ISDA Master Agreement with a schedule that subjects the hedge counterparties to the intercreditor.

The LMA and LSTA forms provide the facility-agreement "chassis." The CTA and intercreditor are bespoke. The Infrastructure Asia suite is the only published project finance CTA template found. It is Singapore-law and about 50% standardized. (Structure description: [source: 7, 17]. The ISDA reference is general market practice.)

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Standardization gap.** Infrastructure Asia stated that the lack of standard project finance loan documentation raised the time and cost of negotiating bespoke documents and reduced precedent value. That was its rationale for the 2020 suite. [source: 17, 18]
- **Benchmark transition.** The orderly end of LIBOR on 30 September 2024, prepared through synthetic settings, showed why long-tenor project loans need robust benchmark fallback clauses. Project loans often run 15 to 25 years, longer than many benchmarks' assumed lives. [source: 13, 14]
- **Sustainable lending.** The 2025 move from "should" to "shall" responded to market concern about greenwashing and about the integrity of labelled loans, per Slaughter and May's analysis. [source: 22]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 51 (The finance documents).** The template is a chassis, not the car. Teach readers to identify which clauses come from the LMA or LSTA form (largely non-negotiable boilerplate) and which are project-specific, where the negotiation happens: CPs to first drawdown, completion definition, the waterfall, reserve sizing, voting and entrenched rights. Use the Infrastructure Asia "50% standard / 50% bespoke" split as a framing device for the Case P CTA. Compare English-law LMA practice with New York-law LSTA practice, especially majority thresholds, the agency role and transfer restrictions. Explain that the GLP and SLLP are voluntary principles, not law, but are contractually embedded through green and sustainability-linked provisions, and that labelled loans now attract mandatory reporting.

## Do not state

- That the LMA publishes a "recommended form project finance facility agreement" or "LMA project finance CTA." None was found. The LMA has an infrastructure guidance note and adjacent forms (ECA, PXF, developing markets).
- That the LSTA has a project finance template. None was found; MCAPs target leveraged finance.
- Exact current member counts beyond those stated with dates. Figures change; for example, older sources give 845 or 870 for the LMA.
- The exact publication date of the LMA infrastructure guidance note. It was not verified.
- That the LMA Term SOFR forms have become "recommended forms." The status could not be confirmed beyond the exposure drafts.
- The precise day in June or July 2024 of the LMA Africa exposure draft. Sources conflict (24 June vs 24 July 2024); say "mid-2024."
- That a 66⅔% majority is "standard" in project finance intercreditors. Thresholds are negotiated per deal.
