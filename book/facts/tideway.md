# Thames Tideway Tunnel: RAB and government support package

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: Tideway Annual Report 2025/26, published June 2026, covering the year to 31 March 2026 and storm testing to early June 2026)

## Summary

The Thames Tideway Tunnel (TTT) is a 25 km storage-and-transfer sewer under the tidal Thames, from Acton in west London to Abbey Mills in east London. It intercepts combined sewer overflows that the Court of Justice of the European Union had in 2012 found to put the UK in breach of the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive. Rather than having Thames Water build it on its own balance sheet, or the state build it, government chose a separate, specially licensed "Infrastructure Provider": Bazalgette Tunnel Limited (BTL), trading as Tideway. BTL was licensed by Ofwat in August 2015 after a competition run by Thames Water. It earns a regulated revenue, billed to Thames Water's wastewater customers and collected by Thames Water, as a return (a bid weighted average cost of capital of 2.497%, a real-terms, RPI-linked parameter) on a regulatory capital value that grows as it spends, so it is paid during construction rather than only after completion. A contingent Government Support Package (GSP) from Defra covers tail risks that investors said they could not price: cost overruns beyond a threshold, debt-market disruption, uninsurable losses, discontinuation, and special administration. The National Audit Office (NAO) reported that the GSP was key to achieving the low bid cost of capital, and estimated the government's reasonable-worst-case exposure at £6.6 billion. Construction is complete. The last of 21 connections was made in February 2025, King Charles III formally opened the tunnel on 7 May 2025, and as of June 2026 the project was in its final commissioning and storm-testing stage before handover to Thames Water. The cost estimate at completion has risen to £4.7 billion, against a £3.5 billion regulatory baseline. Tideway says the customer bill impact remains within the £20 to £25 a year (2014/15 prices) promised before licence award. The TTT became the reference model for the nuclear RAB used at Sizewell C.

## Verified facts

1. The tunnel is about 25 km long and runs from Acton (west London) to Abbey Mills (east London), intercepting storm sewage overflows that would otherwise discharge into the Thames. It connects to the 6.9 km Lee Tunnel. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
2. In 2012 the Court of Justice of the European Union found the UK in breach of the Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive (1991) because of the frequency of combined sewer overflow spills along the Thames. Defra announced support for a tunnel solution in 2007. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
3. Defra appraised three delivery models: delivery by Thames Water, by an independent "Infrastructure Provider", or by the public sector. Its consultants (Ernst & Young) concluded that a separate Infrastructure Provider was the most cost-effective option that also met the policy preference to minimize calls on government capital and limit customer bills. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
4. Bazalgette Tunnel Limited won Thames Water's Infrastructure Provider procurement competition, and Ofwat issued it a project licence in August 2015. Tideway's 2015 annual report gives the licence date as 24 August 2015. [confidence: high for August 2015; high for 24 August 2015] [source: 1, 4]
5. The winning bid offered a bid weighted average cost of capital (BWACC) of 2.497%. It is fixed, subject to the licence, until the first price review after construction. The NAO describes it as a real-terms regulated parameter used to calculate allowed revenue during construction, not as a measure of investor returns, and notes that the TTT RAB is indexed to RPI. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 4, 5, 9]
6. Tideway's revenue until 2030 is set under its licence, primarily as a percentage return (2.497%) on its Regulatory Capital Value (RCV). From 2030 Tideway expects to be regulated in line with the rest of the water industry. Allowed project spend is added to the RCV during construction, and excluded spend such as financing costs is not. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
7. Thames Water bills and collects Tideway's revenue from its wastewater customers and passes it to Tideway under a charging formula. Thames Water is obliged under its own licence to collect and pass on these revenues, and Ofwat reviews and approves Tideway's charges annually. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
8. Oxera (Peter Hope, September 2015) compared the 2.497% bid WACC with Ofwat's draft guidance of 3.29% and the PR14 wholesale WACC of 3.60%. He argued that the effective return is higher than the headline figure, because during construction BTL earns a return on the following year's capital value, among other regulatory features. [confidence: high (as Oxera's analysis)] [source: 5]
9. At licence award the equity investors were Allianz, Dalmore Capital, International Public Partnerships (INPP, managed by Amber Infrastructure), DIF and Swiss Life. By 2025/26 Tideway's board had shareholder directors from three groups: Amber Infrastructure, Allianz and Dalmore Capital. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
10. At licence award, shareholders committed GBP 1,274.2 million: GBP 509.7 million of equity and GBP 764.5 million of shareholder loans. All of it was injected by 2019, and investment has been debt-financed since. Because Tideway cannot pay dividends before handover, shareholders are paid during construction through shareholder-loan interest and partial repayments. Distributions in 2025/26 were GBP 65 million. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
11. By early 2017 Tideway had arranged GBP 1.27 billion of equity finance and GBP 2.2 billion of debt. The debt comprised a GBP 700 million European Investment Bank loan, a GBP 1 billion committed revolving credit facility from several banks, and GBP 450 million of bond issues. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
12. Tideway has issued 18 green bonds and one blue bond. Green and blue debt totals GBP 2,407 million (GBP 2,082 million of bonds and GBP 325 million of green US private placements). In July 2025 it raised GBP 250 million in a blue-format bond. On 31 March 2026 a new GBP 120 million revolving credit facility, structured as a blue loan, replaced the GBP 160 million facility. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
13. Tideway's credit ratings have been Baa1 (Moody's) and BBB+ (Fitch) since licence award, with stable outlooks as of March 2026. Tideway says these are two notches above the levels required by its Common Terms Agreement (CTA) and licence. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
14. At 31 March 2026, net debt to adjusted RCV (gearing) was 67.9% against a covenant maximum of 70.0%. The interest cover ratio was 4.19x against a minimum of 1.3x. Net debt was GBP 4,137.1 million. Liquidity facilities plus the debt service reserve account are sized to cover twelve months of interest, fees and scheduled amortization. [confidence: high] [source: 6]
15. The Government Support Package (2015) transfers specified risks to taxpayers. As described by the NAO it comprises:
    - a Contingent Equity Support Agreement: if costs exceed a threshold of GBP 4.1 billion (GBP 960 million, or 30%, above the GBP 3.2 billion target price), the government either provides equity or discontinues the project and pays compensation;
    - a Market Disruption Facility: short-term liquidity on commercial terms of between GBP 100 million and GBP 500 million if Tideway cannot access debt markets;
    - a Supplemental Compensation Agreement: an indemnity for claims above GBP 2.26 billion per event for damage to the works and GBP 750 million per event for third-party death, injury or damage, or where insurance is unavailable;
    - compensation if the government discontinues the project, linked to allowed spend and hedge break costs;
    - a Special Administration Offer Agreement: if Tideway is in special administration for 18 months, the government offers to buy it or discontinues and pays compensation.

    [confidence: high] [source: 1]
16. Defra concluded that private delivery would not be financially viable without some form of government support, given the scale of project risks and their effect on financing costs. It considered a call on the GSP highly unlikely, but estimated government exposure in a reasonable-worst-case scenario at up to GBP 6.6 billion. [confidence: high] [source: 1]
17. The NAO (2017) wrote that, by reducing potential risks to investors, the GSP was key to Bazalgette financing the project on a lower regulated return than would otherwise have been possible (2.497%). [confidence: high] [source: 1]
18. In 2017 the GSP was expected to expire when the three-year post-construction testing period ended, then expected in February 2027. [confidence: high for the 2017 expectation; the current expiry date depends on the revised handover and system acceptance timetable] [source: 1]
19. Cost history:
    - 2017: the cost was estimated at GBP 4.2 billion (2016 prices), of which GBP 3.2 billion was Bazalgette's works (including GBP 0.5 billion contingency) and GBP 1.0 billion was Thames Water's enabling works.
    - March 2025: Tideway's cost estimate at completion was GBP 4.6 billion.
    - March 2026: it was GBP 4.7 billion, against a regulatory baseline of GBP 3.5 billion (GBP 3.1 billion in 2014/15 prices).

    The 2025/26 increase was attributed to programme prolongation from river-works consent issues, variable rainfall and below-average inflows during commissioning. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
20. The tunnel's charge on the average Thames Water household bill was GBP 13 in 2016-17. The forecast peak was GBP 20 to 25 (2016-17 prices) in the early 2020s, down from the GBP 70 to 80 announced in 2011, partly because of lower-than-expected financing costs. Tideway states the bill impact remains within the GBP 20 to 25 range (2014/15 prices) estimated before licence award. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 6]
21. The main works were let in three sections to joint ventures: West (BAM Nuttall, Morgan Sindall, Balfour Beatty), Central (Ferrovial Agroman UK, Laing O'Rourke) and East (Costain, Vinci Construction Grands Projets, Bachy Soletanche). [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7]
22. Physical milestones: tunnelling began in 2018; main tunnel construction was completed in spring 2024; the system was first activated in August 2024 (Tideway), with the first connections diverting flows from autumn 2024; the final connection was made on 14 February 2025; King Charles III formally opened the tunnel on 7 May 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 6, 7, 8]
23. Commissioning:
    - Storm tests 1 and 2 were completed on 19 July 2025. On 21 July 2025 Tideway and Thames Water agreed to move from half-tunnel to full-tunnel fill settings.
    - Storm test 3 was achieved in January 2026 under dry-weather contingency measures, with further criteria tested in heavy rain in early June 2026.
    - Storm test 4, a 30-day automated test, was completed in April 2026.
    - Tideway's target was handover by Q2 2025/26. The forecast moved to handover by Q2 2026/27 (that is, by September 2026).
    - Because inflows have been below average, Tideway is discussing an alternative approach to system acceptance with Ofwat, the Environment Agency, Defra and Thames Water.

    [confidence: high] [source: 6]
24. From first activation in August 2024 to 31 March 2026, the tunnel intercepted about 19.6 million tonnes of sewage across more than 1,000 overflow interceptions. [confidence: high (company-reported)] [source: 6]
25. Tideway's 2025/26 report calls Thames Water's risk exposure "high" given Thames Water's evolving financial position. Tideway says its revenues are well protected by water industry legislation and licence obligations. It modelled scenarios in which Thames Water, or a special administrator, withholds revenue for a period, and noted that doing so would breach Thames Water's licence and be expected to lead to enforcement. [confidence: high] [source: 6]

## Financing and structure details

- **Licensed entity**: Bazalgette Tunnel Limited (Tideway), a regulated Infrastructure Provider holding its own Ofwat project licence, separate from Thames Water. Bonds are issued through Bazalgette Finance plc under a multi-currency programme listed on the London Stock Exchange's sustainable bond market. [source: 1, 6]
- **Revenue**: regulated allowed revenue during construction and operation, based on 2.497% bid WACC × RCV, billed by Thames Water to its wastewater customers and passed through. There is a reconciliation mechanism for under-recovery. [source: 6, 4]
- **Equity**: GBP 509.7 million of equity plus GBP 764.5 million of shareholder loans committed at award (GBP 1,274.2 million). The shareholder loan stood at GBP 985.8 million at March 2026 after capitalized interest. [source: 6]
- **Initial debt (2015 to 2017)**: EIB GBP 700 million loan; GBP 1 billion committed RCF; first bonds GBP 450 million. [source: 1]
- **Debt platform**: bankruptcy-remote whole-business-style structure with covenants. Gearing covenant maximum is 70% net debt/adjusted RCV and interest cover minimum is 1.3x. The DSRA plus liquidity facilities (GBP 95 million) cover 12 months of debt service. Debt is largely RPI- and CPI-linked, as a natural hedge against the RPI-indexed RCV. [source: 6]
- **Ratings**: Baa1 (Moody's), BBB+ (Fitch), unchanged since licence award. [source: 6]
- **Construction contracts**: three main works contracts (West, Central, East), a system integrator contractor, and an Alliance Agreement with Thames Water and the programme manager, with pain/gain sharing. Independent assessors report quarterly so Ofwat can disallow unagreed spend and Defra gets early warning of any GSP call. [source: 1, 6]
- **Government Support Package**: see fact 15. Threshold outturn for cost overrun support: GBP 4.1 billion (30% above the GBP 3.2 billion target price). Government reasonable-worst-case exposure: GBP 6.6 billion. [source: 1]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right, financing cost**: The NAO (2017) attributes the low 2.497% regulated return to the GSP and to revenue arrangements that gave investors certainty. Lower-than-expected finance costs helped cut the forecast peak bill impact from GBP 70 to 80 (2011) to GBP 20 to 25. [source: 1]
- **Debate on true returns**: Oxera (2015) argued that the headline 2.497% understates the economic return, because of features such as earning returns on next year's capital value during construction and the banded debt adjustment mechanism. [source: 5]
- **Risk transfer to taxpayers**: The NAO noted that the GSP transfers liability to taxpayers if tail risks materialize, with a reasonable-worst-case exposure of GBP 6.6 billion. It cautioned more broadly that government must make sure such guarantees are genuinely needed. [source: 1]
- **Option appraisal**: The NAO found that more accurate modelling might have produced a smaller, cheaper design of the full tunnel option, and that Thames Water's analysis of alternatives after 2007 was less detailed and its costing of alternatives was not independently scrutinized. Defra disagreed that a smaller-diameter tunnel would have cost significantly less. [source: 1]
- **Delivery**: Construction finished, but the estimate at completion rose from the GBP 3.5 billion regulatory baseline to GBP 4.7 billion (source 6). COVID-19 and, in the commissioning phase, river-consent issues and low rainfall delayed completion. [source: 6]
- **Counterparty concentration**: All revenue flows through Thames Water. Tideway treats Thames Water's financial position as a high risk, mitigated by statutory and licence obligations rather than by any direct contract with customers. [source: 6]
- **Precedent**: The NAO's 2026 Sizewell C report explicitly cites the TTT as the precedent for applying a RAB model to a project under construction, and compares its cost of capital with Sizewell C's. [source: 9]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 19 (FiTs, CfDs and RAB models)**: Use the TTT as the clean worked example of a construction-phase RAB. Revenue starts on day one as a return on a growing RCV. Customers carry demand and inflation risk through the regulator. The state takes only defined tail risks through a contingent package. Contrast it with a CfD, where nothing is paid until generation, and show how payment during construction plus capped tail risk compresses the cost of capital. Have students map each GSP instrument to the risk it removes from the investor's distribution.
- **Chapter 81 (social infrastructure, water and waste)**: Present the TTT as the archetype of a carve-out from an incumbent utility: a separately licensed single-asset company, financed like a whole-business securitization, whose revenue is billed and collected by the incumbent. Teach the counterparty and collection risk this creates, made concrete by Thames Water's later distress, and how licence obligations and reconciliation mechanisms mitigate it. Use the gearing and interest cover covenants, RPI-linked debt, and green and blue bond issuance as a template for regulated water financings.

## Do not state

- Do not state a "final cost of GBP 5 billion" (Wikipedia). The company's latest estimate at completion is GBP 4.7 billion (March 2026). Press figures of GBP 4.5 billion are older.
- Do not state that handover to Thames Water, or system acceptance, has occurred. As of the June 2026 annual report it was forecast for Q2 2026/27, and an alternative approach to system acceptance was under discussion. Completion after that date is unverified.
- Do not state that the licence was awarded on 3 November 2015 (a Wikipedia claim). Ofwat issued it in August 2015 (24 August 2015).
- Do not call 2.497% the investors' equity return or a nominal rate. It is a real-terms regulated WACC parameter.
- Do not state that the GSP has ever been drawn or called. No evidence of a call was found.
- Do not give the exact current GSP expiry date. The 2017 expectation was February 2027, tied to a testing timetable that has since moved.
- Do not attribute specific equity percentages to individual shareholders. These were not verified.
- Avoid stating precise Thames Water restructuring outcomes (2024 to 2026) in this case. They are outside the verified scope of this sheet.
