# Bujagali Hydropower Project (Uganda): DFI-led financing and environmental and social risk

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: 3 March 2025, completion of TotalEnergies' acquisition of Scatec's 51 percent interest in the SN Power hydropower joint venture that holds the largest Bujagali stake; latest official evaluation: World Bank Implementation Completion and Results Report, September 2018)

## Summary

Bujagali is a 250 MW run-of-river hydropower plant on the Victoria Nile about 8 km downstream of the Nalubaale/Kiira dam complex near Jinja, Uganda, developed as Uganda's first large independent power producer (IPP) by Bujagali Energy Limited (BEL). A first attempt, sponsored by AES and approved by the World Bank Board in December 2001, collapsed in 2003 after the sponsor withdrew amid financial distress and corruption investigations, and after a World Bank Inspection Panel investigation found safeguard non-compliance. The Government of Uganda re-tendered the concession and in 2005 selected a consortium of Industrial Promotion Services (Kenya) (an Aga Khan Fund for Economic Development affiliate) and Sithe Global Power (a Blackstone-controlled company), which signed a 30-year take-or-pay style capacity power purchase agreement (PPA) with the state transmission company UETCL in December 2005. Financial close came on 21 December 2007 on a financing plan of about USD 798 million, approximately 78 percent debt, almost all of it from development finance institutions (DFIs): IFC, EIB, AfDB, and the European DFIs (Proparco, AFD, FMO, DEG, KfW), plus USD 115 million of commercial bank debt from Absa and Standard Chartered covered by an IDA partial risk guarantee, with MIGA covering equity. The plant entered commercial operation on 1 August 2012, about 13.6 months late, at a final cost of about USD 891.5 million (12 percent over appraisal); the EPC price rose about 32 percent from the opening bid. A second Inspection Panel investigation (report August 2008) found non-compliance with five World Bank policies, and environmental and social issues (cultural and spiritual values of the Bujagali Falls, land titles and electrification for resettled households, and the Kalagala Falls biodiversity offset later threatened by the government's own Isimba dam) remained open years after completion. In 2018 the DFI debt was refinanced to extend tenor and reduce the tariff, and in 2016 and 2024–25 the Sithe stake changed hands, first to SN Power and then indirectly to TotalEnergies.

## Verified facts

1. Bujagali is a 250 MW run-of-river plant (five 50 MW Kaplan units) with a small daily-storage reservoir, an intake-powerhouse complex and a rock-fill dam about 30 m high, on the Nile about 8 km downstream of the 380 MW Nalubaale/Kiira complex. The reservoir can run the plant for about 10 hours at peak output without inflow. Scatec and TotalEnergies materials from 2024–25 describe the plant as 255 MW. [confidence: high for 250 MW design capacity; medium for the basis of the later 255 MW figure, which is not explained in the sources] [source: 1, 2, 9, 10]
2. The reservoir surface area was estimated at 388 hectares; the project required 238 hectares of land, of which about 80 hectares was newly inundated land and 113 hectares was for temporary and ancillary facilities. About 100 km of transmission line and a substation at Kawanda were needed for evacuation, built as a separate UETCL project financed by AfDB and JICA, with BEL managing procurement and construction on UETCL's behalf. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
3. Context: drought in 2004 cut Nalubaale/Kiira output to about 120 MW; the government contracted emergency diesel-fired thermal plants whose power cost approximately 20–25 US cents/kWh, subsidized from the budget. Technical and non-technical losses were estimated at 44 percent in 2005. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
4. First attempt: AES began discussions with Uganda in 1994. The World Bank Board approved an IDA partial risk guarantee of USD 115 million and IFC loans (USD 60 million A loan and USD 40 million B loan) on 18 December 2001. The sponsor withdrew, and the government terminated the project agreements in September 2003. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4]
5. The World Bank's completion report attributes the sponsor's withdrawal to the continuing deterioration of the sponsor's own financial situation, preceded by investigations of corruption allegations in the United States, Norway, the United Kingdom and Uganda. A 2003 Congressional Research Service report records that Veidekke disclosed an unauthorized payment by its UK subsidiary to a Ugandan official in 1999. [confidence: high as reported] [source: 2, 4]
6. The first Inspection Panel investigation (report June 2002) found the first project did not comply with several safeguard policies: no sectoral environmental assessment, inadequate measures to protect the Kalagala Falls site, an insufficient community development action plan, and economic studies that had not followed a transparent process. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 4]
7. Second attempt: the government ran a competitive tender with prequalification of three sponsors; proposals were assessed on project cost (including proposed return on equity) and financing plan. The selection of BEL was made public by April 2005. The PPA (with UETCL) and Implementation Agreement were signed in December 2005; the World Bank's guarantee brief dates the PPA to 13 December 2005 with a 30-year term. Government advisers were Hunton & Williams (legal) and Scott Wilson Piesold (engineering). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
8. BEL's sponsors at financial close were Industrial Promotion Services (Kenya) Limited, an Aga Khan group affiliate, and SG Bujagali Holdings Ltd, an affiliate of Sithe Global Power LLC, which EIB described as majority-owned by Blackstone; the Government of Uganda also held equity, contributing USD 20 million in kind ("Government Contributed Assets"). [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2, 3]
9. Contract structure: Implementation Agreement (government grants the concession to design, finance, build, own, operate and maintain); PPA under which BEL sells all output exclusively to UETCL, which buys contracted capacity; a Government Guarantee of UETCL's PPA payment obligations and an indemnity for losses if those obligations become void or unenforceable; a fixed-price, date-certain turnkey EPC contract with Salini (Italy), with Alstom Power Hydraulique (France) as key subcontractor; O&M by a Sithe Global affiliate; and lenders' direct agreements, including step-in rights. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
10. The PPA pays BEL on proof of capacity availability rather than energy delivered, so hydrology risk sat with the offtaker. It also passed EPC cost changes through to the capacity payment: if actual EPC cost differed from the base EPC cost, the capacity payment adjusted to offset 70 percent of the difference, capped at a 10 percent change in the capacity payment. The World Bank's completion report concludes the actual risk allocation was not that of a typical IPP project financing, because the public sector shared considerable risks, including construction delays and EPC pricing. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
11. EPC procurement: only two bids were received, on 26 October 2006. The winning bid was USD 467.2 million (approximately USD 1,869/kW); the other bid was approximately 43 percent higher. The bid was 48 percent above the 2001 EPC price for the same project. Bid prices were read publicly under EIB procurement rules. Negotiations ended in May 2007 at USD 557 million (about 20 percent above the bid), and with approved overruns the final EPC cost was USD 616 million (about 32 percent above the bid price). [confidence: high] [source: 2]
12. The government gave BEL a USD 75 million bridge loan during the EPC negotiations so the EPC price could be locked in before bid validity expired and construction could start before financial close; it was to be repaid from an equity injection at full notice to proceed. The government also allowed EPC bidding to proceed before a full geotechnical assessment was complete. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
13. Appraisal financing plan (USD million): IFC 130; EIB 130; commercial banks under the IDA guarantee 115; AfDB 110; European DFIs 142; total debt 627; sponsor equity 151; government equity 20; total equity 171; total 798. The debt-to-equity ratio was approximately 78:22. All senior loans had a 16-year door-to-door maturity. The same World Bank brief also states in its text that EIB lent USD 140 million; its table and the completion report give USD 130 million at appraisal. [confidence: high for the table; the 130 versus 140 inconsistency is in the source] [source: 1, 2]
14. European DFI loans at appraisal were Proparco USD 73 million, DEG/KfW USD 45 million and FMO USD 73 million (per the World Bank brief). Actual amounts reported in the completion report data sheet are FMO USD 82.10 million, Proparco USD 59.18 million, DEG USD 59.02 million and AFD USD 12.80 million. [confidence: medium; the two sources group the institutions differently and do not reconcile] [source: 1, 2]
15. Actual financing at completion (USD thousands): IFC 128,366; EIB 136,000; IDA-guaranteed commercial banks 115,000; AfDB 111,000; European DFIs 213,103; total debt 703,469; sponsor equity 179,761; government equity 20,000; total equity 199,761; total 903,230. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
16. Actual project cost was USD 891.5 million against USD 798.6 million at appraisal (+12 percent): EPC USD 616.5 million (+19 percent against the appraisal EPC estimate of USD 520.1 million), interest during construction and financing fees USD 111.7 million, development costs USD 30.3 million, government-contributed assets USD 20 million, debt service reserve USD 46.0 million, working capital and other costs USD 67.0 million. About USD 11.6 million of spending was disallowed from the PPA cost base. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
17. EIB signed its loan on 7 January 2008: EUR 92 million (USD 136 million equivalent), with a tenor of up to 20 years, as part of a loan package of the equivalent of EUR 462 million. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
18. IFC's financing was an A loan of up to USD 100 million and a C loan of up to USD 30 million. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
19. The IDA partial risk guarantee (Guarantee B0130) of up to USD 115 million was approved on 26 April 2007 and became effective on 22 June 2007, with expiry on 30 November 2023 at the end of the commercial debt repayment period. It covered the commercial lenders (Absa and Standard Chartered at close) against debt service default caused by the government's failure to meet payment obligations under the Implementation Agreement and Government Guarantee arising from political force majeure, change in law or events making the contracts unenforceable, government restrictions on payment, currency conversion or transfer, and failure to meet payment obligations relating to UETCL's power purchases and termination payments. It was non-accelerable. The government counter-guaranteed IDA under an Indemnity Agreement, and BEL signed a Project Agreement with IDA committing to World Bank environmental requirements. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
20. The World Bank stated that the IDA guarantee was about 18 percent of total debt and catalyzed co-financing of over USD 750 million, and that it allowed the commercial loans to match DFI maturities. The guarantee had not been called as of the completion report. [confidence: high as the World Bank's own assessment] [source: 1, 2]
21. MIGA issued breach-of-contract cover to World Power Holdings Luxembourg S.à r.l. (a Sithe Global affiliate) on equity, increased from USD 115 million to USD 120.3 million, for a 20-year term, and USD 9.5 million (11-year term) on the lenders' interest rate swap with Absa and Standard Chartered. Total World Bank Group exposure was up to USD 374.8 million. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
22. Financial close was achieved on 21 December 2007. Construction was to take 44 months. Commercial operation began on 1 August 2012 and the plant was handed over to BEL on 8 October 2012, 13.6 months behind schedule. Main causes were adverse ground conditions under the gated spillway (requiring low-quality rock to be replaced with concrete and steel), problems on the right embankment and a cofferdam, and equipment delivery and commissioning delays, including a ship hijacking incident and electromechanical defects. Under the EPC terms, ground-condition risk lay mostly with the owner, and much of the cost increase passed to UETCL through the PPA. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
23. In the first year of operation the plant generated 1,352.7 GWh against a 1,165 GWh target (16 percent above). The completion report re-estimates the levelized cost of electricity at 9.47 US cents/kWh in its base case (7.69 to 11.37 across hydrology cases) against an appraisal estimate of 5.7 to 9.7, and an actual economic internal rate of return of about 16.7 percent. UETCL had built a record as a punctual payer. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
24. The tariff BEL charges UETCL is set high in the first 11 years to repay project debt and falls once the debt is repaid. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
25. Second Inspection Panel case: a request was received in March 2007 from the National Association of Professional Environmentalists and other local organizations; a parallel request went to AfDB's Compliance Review and Mediation Unit. The Panel's investigation report (Report No. 44977-UG, August 2008) found management not in compliance with five policies: OP/BP 4.01 (Environmental Assessment) together with the disclosure policy, OP/BP 4.04 (Natural Habitats), OP/BP 4.11 (Physical Cultural Resources), OP/BP 4.12 (Involuntary Resettlement), and OP/BP 10.04 (Economic Evaluation). The Board discussed it in December 2008, and a Management Action Plan followed. The seventh and final progress report (February 2018) confirmed all actions complete. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 5]
26. The project triggered seven World Bank safeguard policies (including Safety of Dams and International Waterways) and was Category A. BEL prepared a full Social and Environmental Assessment, an Assessment of Past Resettlement and Action Plan (because most people near the site had already been resettled during the first attempt), a Community Development Action Plan, and consultation and disclosure plans designed to meet the requirements of the World Bank Group, AfDB, EIB, DEG and the government. The government hired the former sponsor's safeguards team to avoid disrupting the resettlement process. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
27. Issues that required special attention or were delayed included blasting impacts on nearby households, the spiritual values of the Bujagali Falls, delays in electrifying the Naminya resettlement area, land titles for resettled households, and protection of the Kalagala Falls site. Two fatalities in the first quarter of 2010 led lenders to require occupational health and safety audits. The April 2016 supervision report, nearly four years after closing, downgraded overall safeguards compliance to Moderately Unsatisfactory (Involuntary Resettlement Moderately Unsatisfactory). [confidence: high] [source: 2]
28. Kalagala offset: the government committed, in the Indemnity Agreement with IDA signed in July 2007, to set aside the Kalagala Falls site to protect its natural habitat, environmental and spiritual values and not to develop power generation that would compromise this without IDA's agreement. That agreement was the only legal instrument protecting the site, and it would end in 2023. The government's Isimba hydropower project (183 MW, financed by the government with China Exim Bank support) threatened part of the site; Inspection Panel requests followed in September 2016. In April 2017 the Board ruled Bujagali itself ineligible for inspection because the project had closed. The Indemnity Agreement was amended on 24 January 2018 to redefine the offset as an approximately 15 km Nile stretch and adjoining forest reserves, and the government committed to give it legal protection beyond 2023. [confidence: high] [source: 2]
29. Refinancing: in 2017 the government asked BEL to refinance its debt to reduce electricity costs; IFC and AfDB were mandated lead arrangers. The package extended the remaining DFI loan tenor from 5 to 15 years. Within the IDA-guaranteed facility, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas were repaid, while Absa and Nedbank stayed in with their original loans. IFC's and MIGA's Boards approved support on 8 March 2018 (MIGA: up to USD 423 million of political risk guarantees for up to 20 years; more than USD 400 million of loans in total), and the refinancing became effective on 20 July 2018. The World Bank expected a tariff reduction of about 5.5 US cents/kWh on average over 2018–2023, helped by an additional tax holiday granted to the sponsor, and an average end-user tariff reduction of about 2.3 US cents/kWh. [confidence: high] [source: 2, 6]
30. Ownership changes: in 2016 SN Power acquired SG Bujagali Holdings' interest in BEL, with the other shareholdings unchanged. On 30 July 2024 Scatec agreed to sell its 51 percent stake in its African hydropower joint venture (SN Power, with Norfund and British International Investment as partners) to TotalEnergies; the sale completed on 3 March 2025 for USD 167 million. TotalEnergies reported this gives it a 28.3 percent stake in Bujagali. BEL lists its shareholders as TotalEnergies, Bujagali Holdings Power Company Ltd (an AKFED affiliate) and the Government of Uganda. [confidence: high for the transactions; medium for the 28.3 percent figure, which is from press reports of TotalEnergies' announcement] [source: 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]
31. The World Bank's guarantee brief reports that the deal was named "Africa Power Deal of the Year 2007" by Euromoney's Project Finance magazine. [confidence: high as reported by the World Bank] [source: 1]

## Financing and structure details

- Project company: Bujagali Energy Limited (Uganda), owned at close by IPS (Kenya) (AKFED group), SG Bujagali Holdings (Sithe Global/Blackstone) and the Government of Uganda (USD 20 million in-kind equity). [source: 1, 2, 3]
- Offtaker: UETCL under a 30-year capacity-based PPA, with payment obligations guaranteed by the Government of Uganda. [source: 1, 2]
- Appraisal sources (USD 798 million): debt USD 627 million (IFC 130, EIB 130, AfDB 110, European DFIs 142, IDA-guaranteed commercial banks 115); equity USD 171 million. All senior loans 16 years door to door. [source: 1]
- Actual sources (USD 903 million): debt USD 703.5 million, equity USD 199.8 million; the overrun was funded by more European DFI debt (+50 percent) and sponsor equity, while World Bank Group amounts stayed unchanged. [source: 2]
- Credit enhancement: IDA partial risk guarantee up to USD 115 million (non-accelerable, government counter-indemnity); MIGA breach-of-contract cover USD 120.3 million on equity (20 years) and USD 9.5 million on the lenders' swap (11 years). [source: 1, 2]
- EIB: EUR 92 million (USD 136 million), tenor up to 20 years. [source: 3]
- 2018 refinancing: DFI tenor extended by 10 years (remaining 5 to 15 years); partial exit of commercial banks; MIGA cover up to USD 423 million for up to 20 years. [source: 2, 6]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- **Right (World Bank completion report):** coordinated use of IDA, IFC and MIGA instruments mobilized long-term private finance in a market where the first attempt had failed; prior sector reform (unbundling, the Umeme distribution concession, an independent regulator) reduced offtaker risk; joint lenders' missions, led in rotation, harmonized the requirements of many DFIs; the plant has outperformed its generation target and UETCL has paid on time. [source: 2]
- **Wrong, procurement and cost (World Bank completion report):** only two EPC bidders, plus public reading of bid prices under DFI rules, gave the winning bidder negotiating leverage; post-award negotiations raised the price about 20 percent, and overruns raised it to about 32 percent above the bid, a cost ultimately borne by government and consumers through the PPA pass-through. The report recommends flexible procurement, including not reading bid prices publicly, when competition is thin. [source: 2]
- **Trade-off, geotechnical risk (World Bank completion report):** bidding the EPC before geotechnical work was complete was a deliberate trade-off against the cost of continuing emergency thermal power; the risk materialized under the spillway. The report calls the decision sensible in an emergency but notes the risk was borne by the public side. [source: 2]
- **Wrong, safeguards (Inspection Panel; World Bank completion report):** the second project did not fully learn from the first Panel's findings, leading to a second finding of non-compliance on five policies. The Kalagala offset was chosen without a rigorous baseline or demarcation, without considering the government's own hydropower plans, and with protection resting only on a contract with IDA, which left it exposed when Isimba was built. [source: 2]
- **Financing tenor and tariff (World Bank completion report):** the tariff was front-loaded because the debt tenor was much shorter than the plant's economic life, which became a political issue once cheaper state-financed hydro (Isimba, Karuma) was coming; the 2018 refinancing extended tenor to lower the tariff. [source: 2]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- **Chapter 50 (Environmental and social risk and standards):** Bujagali shows that E&S failure is a financing and schedule risk. The first project's Inspection Panel findings contributed to a two-year collapse and re-tender, and the second project still drew a five-policy non-compliance finding despite a much stronger assessment. Teach the toolkit as it was used here (Category A ESIA, an assessment of past resettlement inherited from a failed sponsor, a community development action plan, an independent panel of experts, joint lender supervision, a multi-stakeholder monitoring committee) and then the gaps: cultural and spiritual values, land titling that took a decade, and an offset protected only by contract. The Kalagala–Isimba episode is the cleanest example of why an offset needs legal protection and a check against the host government's own pipeline.
- **Chapter 72 (Hydropower and geothermal):** use Bujagali for the risk allocation typical of hydro IPPs in frontier markets. Hydrology risk stays with the offtaker through availability-based capacity payments, ground risk under a "fixed-price" EPC ends up with the owner, and a capped 70 percent cost pass-through shares overruns with the offtaker. Pair the EPC numbers (bid USD 467 million, negotiated USD 557 million, final USD 616 million) with the levelized-cost re-estimate to show how construction outcomes flow into tariffs. Close with the 2018 refinancing as a lesson in tenor mismatch for long-life assets.
- **Chapter 50 and Chapter 72, financing angle:** the deal is a template for DFI-led blended finance. The DFIs provide most of the debt, and the IDA partial risk guarantee lets commercial banks match a 16-year DFI tenor. MIGA covers the equity's breach-of-contract risk.

## Do not state

- Do not state a single "total project cost" without its basis: USD 798 million (appraisal financing requirement), USD 891.5 million (actual cost), USD 903 million (actual financing) and EUR 582 million (an EIB figure) all appear. Earlier figures such as "rose from USD 735 million to USD 860 million in early 2007" come from secondary summaries that were not verified.
- Do not state that EIB lent USD 140 million; the figure appears once in a World Bank brief but conflicts with that brief's own table and with EIB's EUR 92 million (USD 136 million) signing.
- Do not state the consideration or percentage in SN Power's 2016 purchase of Sithe Global's stake (a figure of USD 278 million for 65 percent circulates in search snippets but was not verified from a primary source), or BEL's precise current shareholding percentages, other than TotalEnergies' reported 28.3 percent.
- Do not state the number of people or households resettled for the second project; no verified figure was found (most resettlement had occurred during the first attempt).
- Do not state the AfDB Independent Review Mechanism's specific findings; the 2008 report could not be retrieved.
- Do not quote the original capacity of the AES design as 200 MW; one advocacy source says so, while the World Bank describes the same site, capacity (250 MW) and basic layout.
- Do not state the PPA tariff in cents per kWh; no primary figure was verified.
- Do not attribute the 1999 payment to any named official as established fact; it was reported in 2003 as an allegation and a company disclosure.
- Do not state whether the IDA guarantee was ever called after 2018, or the status of Kalagala's statutory protection after 2018; not verified.
