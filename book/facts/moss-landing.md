# Battery fires (Moss Landing 2025) and storage insurance and safety

As of: 2026-10-03 (latest event covered: second fire in the damaged Moss Landing 300 building on 18 September 2026, per US EPA; latest company disclosure is Vistra Corp.'s Form 10-Q for the quarter ended 30 June 2026, filed 10 August 2026)

## Summary

On 16 January 2025 a fire broke out in Vistra Corp.'s Moss Landing 300 battery energy storage facility, a 300 MW lithium-ion system inside a converted building at the Moss Landing Power Plant site in Monterey County, California. The building held about 100,000 lithium-ion battery modules, and about 55 percent of them were damaged. Around 1,200 nearby residents were evacuated. Nobody was injured. All facilities on the site stopped running until the fire was contained: that meant two other battery facilities (100 MW and 350 MW) and a gas-fired plant. The gas plant restarted in February 2025. Vistra wrote off the approximately USD 400 million net book value of Moss Landing 300. In late 2025 it decided not to restart the 100 MW facility and booked an approximately USD 155 million impairment. By mid-2026 the restart of the 350 MW facility was still uncertain. Vistra's insurance claims ran against policies with combined business-interruption and property limits of USD 500 million net of deductibles, and Vistra had collected the full amount by February 2026. Losses kept growing after that: the EPA-supervised clean-up estimate rose from approximately USD 110 million to approximately USD 175 million by June 2026, and there is lost revenue and litigation on top. The site had already seen two smaller overheating incidents, in September 2021 and February 2022. A second fire in the damaged building on 18 September 2026 burned an estimated 1,300 modules. As of the latest filings the cause of the January 2025 fire had not been published. The case shows where battery storage differs from other generation assets: the indoor design, the risk of thermal runaway spreading from unit to unit, environmental clean-up and decommissioning costs, and the size of insurance limits compared with the true cost of a total loss.

## Verified facts

1. On 16 January 2025 Vistra detected a fire at its Moss Landing 300 MW energy storage facility at the Moss Landing Power Plant site in California. Operations stopped at every facility in the Moss Landing complex until the fire was contained. No injuries occurred. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
2. The Moss Landing complex comprises three battery facilities (Moss Landing 300 MW, Moss Landing 100 MW and Moss Landing 350 MW) plus a gas plant (Moss Landing 1 & 2, a combined-cycle plant of approximately 1,020 MW). The gas plant returned to service in February 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
3. The Moss Landing 300 building held approximately 100,000 lithium-ion battery modules, and about 55 percent of the batteries were damaged in the fire. EPA's response timeline estimates that around 56,000 battery modules burned in January 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 3, 4, 5]
4. EPA's timeline records the fire as going out on 18 January 2025, with a later flare-up inside the building on 18 February 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
5. Approximately 1,200 Moss Landing residents were evacuated on the evening of 16 January 2025. The orders were lifted days later. [confidence: medium; local public-radio reporting] [source: 6]
6. EPA ran air monitoring from 17 to 20 January 2025 and issued a Notice of Federal Response Action to Vistra on 22 January 2025. EPA reported that its air monitoring near the site did not exceed health standards. [confidence: high] [source: 3]
7. On 17 July 2025 Vistra and EPA executed an Administrative Settlement Agreement and Order on Consent (ASAOC). It requires Vistra, under EPA oversight, to remove and dispose of batteries, demolish the building, and monitor air and water at the Moss Landing 300 site. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 3]
8. Workers began removing the first batteries from the Moss Landing 300 building on 29 September 2025. EPA progress figures: 22,984 batteries de-energized by 13 February 2026; more than 27,800 by 10 April 2026; and 33,943 intact batteries from the burned area de-energized and ready for recycling by 24 June 2026. In July 2026 EPA described Phase 1, the removal of accessible and recyclable batteries, as complete. Phase 2, which uses heavy equipment to remove the most damaged batteries while the building is demolished, was in progress. [confidence: high] [source: 4, 5]
9. On 18 September 2026, at approximately 6:00 am, part of the damaged batteries still inside the Moss Landing 300 building caught fire. The fire subsided around 10 am the same day, and no injuries were reported. EPA estimates that about 1,300 battery modules burned. [confidence: high] [source: 4]
10. Vistra wrote off the approximately USD 400 million net book value of Moss Landing 300 to depreciation expense in the quarter ended 31 March 2025 and moved the asset to its Asset Closure segment. It does not plan to return the facility to operation. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
11. Vistra later decided not to return the Moss Landing 100 MW facility to service and recognized an impairment loss of approximately USD 155 million in the quarter ended 31 December 2025. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
12. The Moss Landing 350 MW facility had a net book value of approximately USD 320 million at 31 December 2025 and approximately USD 311 million at 30 June 2026. Vistra said in its 10-Q for the second quarter of 2026 that it was "working towards a return to service" in 2026, but that it remained uncertain when or whether the facility would return to service, pending its restart evaluation after the investigation into the cause of the fire. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
13. Vistra's estimate for the ASAOC clean-up was approximately USD 110 million at 31 December 2025, assuming completion by the end of 2026. By 30 June 2026 the estimate had risen to approximately USD 175 million, including a USD 65 million increase recorded in the second quarter of 2026 that Vistra attributed mainly to an extended remediation timeline and higher cost estimates. USD 90 million had been incurred through 30 June 2026. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
14. Insurance: Vistra filed claims against applicable policies with "combined business interruption and property loss limits of $500 million, net of deductibles". Vistra reports that it collected all of this by February 2026. In 2025 Vistra recorded a property-damage insurance receivable of USD 425 million against incident expenses of USD 502 million: the USD 400 million write-off plus USD 102 million of ASAOC costs. That left net incident expense of USD 77 million for 2025. It also realized USD 71 million of business-interruption proceeds in 2025, recorded in operating revenues. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
15. Vistra says the further impacts include lost revenue while the facilities are offline, litigation costs, negotiated settlements of contracts with counterparties, and possible further non-cash impairments. It says it cannot estimate the full impact. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
16. Several lawsuits have been filed in California federal and state courts against Vistra, LG Energy Solution and others over the incident. The CPUC, EPA and other bodies are investigating. [confidence: high] [source: 1, 2]
17. The CPUC's Safety and Enforcement Division opened an investigation, with an initial site visit on 22 January 2025. On 27 January 2025 the CPUC issued a proposal to modify General Order 167. The proposal implements Senate Bill 1383 by setting maintenance and operation standards for battery energy storage facilities and strengthening oversight of emergency response plans, and it was scheduled for a vote on 13 March 2025. The same CPUC release says California's battery storage capacity grew from 500 MW in 2019 to over 13,300 MW in 2024. [confidence: high for the proposal; adoption outcome not verified here] [source: 7]
18. Earlier incidents: in September 2021 Moss Landing Phase I (the 300 MW facility) had an incident affecting a portion of the battery system, and restoration was completed in June 2022. In February 2022 Phase II (100 MW) had an incident, and restoration was completed in September 2022. Vistra's 10-K said that in both cases a review found the root cause "originated in systems separate from the battery system". Vistra reported in its 2021 10-K that only a small, single-digit percentage of batteries were affected in the 2021 event. [confidence: high] [source: 8, 9]
19. Contract and development history: in June 2018 Vistra announced a 20-year resource adequacy (RA) contract with PG&E for the 300 MW Phase I, which the CPUC approved in November 2018. PG&E pays a fixed monthly RA payment, and Vistra keeps energy revenues and bears dispatch and charging costs. When PG&E went through Chapter 11 (January 2019 to July 2020), the bankruptcy court approved assumption of the contract, subject to a CPUC-approved amendment that the CPUC approved in January 2020. Phase II (100 MW) had a 10-year RA contract with PG&E, approved in August 2020. Phase III (350 MW) had a 15-year RA and energy settlement contract with PG&E, approved in April 2022. [confidence: high] [source: 8, 9, 10]
20. Vistra's 10-K filings say Phase I commenced commercial operations in May 2021, Phase II in July 2021 and Phase III in June 2023. Development costs for Phases I and II together totaled approximately USD 600 million. On reaching commercial operation, Phase III gave rise to USD 154 million of transferable investment tax credits. [confidence: high] [source: 9, 11]
21. Vistra financed these assets on its own corporate balance sheet, not through non-recourse project debt. Its 2023 10-K says the net proceeds of its Series B Preferred Stock were fully allocated to eligible solar and battery projects under its Green Finance Framework. No project-level lenders have been identified. [confidence: medium; no project-level debt identified in filings reviewed] [source: 11]
22. In early 2025 Monterey County residents surveyed by local organizations reported symptoms, and researchers at Moss Landing Marine Laboratories reported elevated nickel, cobalt and manganese in Elkhorn Slough sediments. These are research and community findings as reported by local media. EPA's position is that its own air monitoring did not exceed health standards. [confidence: medium] [source: 3, 6]

## Financing and structure details

- Ownership and financing: Vistra Corp. owns all of it on balance sheet, with no project finance lenders identified (see fact 21). The asset was "contracted merchant": fixed monthly RA capacity payments from PG&E (20-year, 10-year and 15-year contracts for the three phases), with Vistra keeping energy-arbitrage revenue and cost. Phase III's contract also included an energy settlement element. [source: 9, 10]
- Tax equity and credits: Phase III generated USD 154 million of transferable ITCs, the stand-alone storage ITC introduced by the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act. [source: 2, 11]
- Insurance: combined business-interruption and property limits of USD 500 million net of deductibles across the applicable policies, fully collected by February 2026. Accounting treatment: the property recovery was booked as an insurance receivable offsetting incident expense (USD 425 million recognized in 2025), and business-interruption proceeds were booked as operating revenue (USD 71 million in 2025). [source: 1, 2]
- Quantified losses disclosed to mid-2026 (indicative and not additive to a final number): approximately USD 400 million write-off of Moss Landing 300, approximately USD 155 million impairment of Moss Landing 100, and approximately USD 175 million estimated clean-up, giving approximately USD 730 million before lost revenue, litigation, contract settlements, and any impact on the idle 350 MW facility (net book value approximately USD 311 million). Compare the USD 500 million of insurance limits. [source: 1, 2]

## What went wrong or right, and why

- No cause had been published as of the latest Vistra filing (10 August 2026). Vistra and the CPUC were still investigating. Do not attribute a root cause. [source: 2, 6]
- On Vistra's own figures, insurance limits were too small for a total loss of the asset plus remediation. The combined USD 500 million limit was used up within about 13 months, while disclosed write-offs, impairments and clean-up costs already exceeded approximately USD 700 million, with lost revenue and litigation still uncapped. This analysis comes directly from Vistra's 10-K and 10-Q disclosures. [source: 1, 2]
- Remediation cost was hard to estimate. The ASAOC estimate rose by about 59 percent in six months (from approximately USD 110 million to approximately USD 175 million) because of a longer timeline and higher costs, and Vistra warned that hazardous waste other than batteries was not yet included. [source: 1, 2]
- Damaged lithium-ion modules can keep posing a hazard for a long time: a flare-up occurred one month after the fire (February 2025), and a second fire broke out 20 months later (September 2026) during demolition and removal. [source: 4]
- The incident spread to assets that did not burn: shared-site outage, regulatory scrutiny and an uncertain restart cut the value of the 100 MW and 350 MW facilities even though they did not burn. [source: 1, 2]
- Local reporting describes the January 2025 event as the third failure of fire suppression or safety systems at the site after 2021 and 2022. Vistra's filings attribute the earlier incidents to "systems separate from the battery system". [source: 6, 8, 9]

## Teaching angles by chapter

- Chapter 27 (Insurance): use Moss Landing to teach limit adequacy and the gap between the insured and the true economic loss. Have the reader set out the disclosed losses against the USD 500 million combined limit, and show how property damage, business interruption and debris removal or pollution clean-up interact. Show how lenders' insurance consultants should test the maximum foreseeable loss for co-located battery buildings (one fire took out three facilities' operations). It also shows how insurance proceeds are booked: property recoveries as a receivable offsetting the write-off, BI as revenue. Also cover site clean-up cost and regulatory orders (an EPA ASAOC) as exposures that may fall outside or exhaust the property program.
- Chapter 73 (Storage, transmission and interconnectors): use the case for technology and design risk in storage. Contrast indoor, densely packed early-generation designs with containerized layouts spaced to limit propagation, and treat safety-standard compliance and fire-propagation testing as diligence items, without naming a cause. Show the revenue model (long-term fixed RA payments from PG&E plus merchant energy margin) and how a casualty event turns a contracted asset into a stranded one. Also show a counterparty-credit wrinkle: PG&E's Chapter 11 required court and CPUC approval to keep the RA contract. Discuss how lenders to a non-recourse BESS would size reserves, require a decommissioning or remediation security, and treat permitting and community acceptance after Moss Landing.

## Do not state

- Any root cause of the January 2025 fire: none had been published as of 10 August 2026.
- That LG Energy Solution was at fault. It is a named defendant in lawsuits, and Vistra's filings do not state who supplied the Moss Landing 300 cells and modules. Wide press reporting says LG supplied them, but this sheet has not verified that against a primary source.
- The energy capacity of Moss Landing 300 in MWh (often reported as 1,200 MWh) and the total site rating "750 MW/3,000 MWh": not verified here against a primary source. Use MW figures from the filings.
- That Moss Landing was "the world's largest battery fire". This is a media characterization; attribute it if used.
- That Phase I came online in December 2020. Press reports said so, but Vistra's 10-K says commercial operations began in May 2021; use the 10-K date.
- Any figure for total insured or economic loss beyond the disclosed items, and any statement about insurers' identities, deductibles, sub-limits, or premium changes in the BESS insurance market after the fire: not verified.
- That CPUC adopted the GO 167 amendments on 13 March 2025, or the content of any 2025 California legislation following the fire: adoption and later bills not verified here.
- Health effects attributed to the fire: community surveys and academic sediment findings are not established causal findings.
- The outcome or status of the lawsuits.
