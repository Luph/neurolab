## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every PPP starts with a government decision that most private-sector financiers never see: whether to buy an asset and its services through a long-term contract with private finance, or to build it with public money. This chapter puts the reader in the finance ministry. It teaches what a government buys with a PPP and what it pays for it, how to build and attack a public sector comparator and a value-for-money test, how affordability differs from value for money, how statistical and fiscal treatment shapes (and distorts) the choice, how to measure and manage the contingent liabilities a PPP leaves with the state, what PPP units and programs do, and how to handle an unsolicited proposal. The UK Private Finance Initiative supplies the real-world arc from launch to abolition. Case T's Brannock Treasury makes the 2012 decision to procure the Merrick Link, and the reader sees the assumption that will later hurt everyone.

What you will be able to do (for the `\section*{What you will be able to do}` list; binding for the capability map, with the practice items that deliver each):

- Build a public sector comparator and a value-for-money test, and find the one assumption that reverses its sign (Capability 9). Delivered by Examples 57.2 and 57.3, Framework 57.1, Walkthrough 57.9, Exercises 57.8 and 57.11.
- Test a PPP or a whole program against an affordability ceiling, and value the guarantees and termination exposure it leaves with the state (Capability 9). Delivered by Examples 57.4 and 57.5, Exercises 57.9, 57.10 and 57.13.
- Explain to a finance minister, in numbers, when a PPP is the right procurement tool and what the private finance costs (Capability 1). Delivered by Examples 57.1 and 57.7, Framework 57.2 applied to Case T, Exercises 57.6 and 57.12.
- Decide how to handle an unsolicited proposal and defend the decision (Capability 15). Delivered by Example 57.6, the judgment drill, Exercise 57.14.

## 3. Concepts

### 3.1 Concepts owned (taught in full here)

1. The rationale for PPPs from the public side: whole-life costing, risk transfer, private-finance discipline and due diligence, faster delivery, budget smoothing; and the critiques at full strength: higher cost of capital, rigidity, transaction cost, off-balance-sheet bias, illusory risk transfer, accountability.

**57.1 What a government buys with a PPP** (`sec:57.1`)

- 57.1.1 Whole-life cost, risk transfer, and private due diligence (`ssec:57.1.1`). What the public client gets that conventional design-bid-build does not: one party accountable for design, construction and decades of maintenance; payment only for a working asset; lenders who audit the project before they fund it. ## 5. Opening

In January 2018 the UK National Audit Office reported that investors in the six PF2 deals expected after-tax returns of 4.5% to 5%, about double what the government paid to borrow, and that a group of PF2 schools would cost around 40% more in cash terms than a publicly financed comparator (uk-pfi facts 4 and 11 to 12). Ten months later the Chancellor ended PFI and PF2 for new projects (fact 19). The opening poses the question the chapter answers: if private finance always costs more, what was the UK government buying for 26 years, and how would you know, on a given project, whether it was worth the price?

## 6. Worked examples

All figures (Illustrative) were computed in Python by the brief writer.

**Example 57.1 The financing premium on a hospital availability charge (Illustrative)** (`ex:57.1`). In March 2024 the health service of a Spanish autonomous community (fictional: the Servicio de Salud de Valdemora) compares two ways of financing EUR 312.7 million of construction cost for a 384-bed hospital at Valdemora over 27 operating years: regional borrowing at 3.18%, or a DBFM availability contract whose bidder's blended private cost of capital is 6.04%. Simplification stated: the capital element of the charge is a level annuity, ignoring construction-period timing, indexation and operating costs, because the lesson is the rate gap. Compute the annual capital charge at each rate, the difference, and the present value of the difference at the public rate.
Expected results: annual charge EUR 17.43 million at 3.18% and EUR 23.77 million at 6.04%; difference EUR 6.34 million a year; PV of the difference at 3.18% over 27 years EUR 113.7 million, 36.4% of construction cost. The text then states what risk transfer and efficiency must be worth (at least EUR 113.7 million in PV) for the PPP to break even, which motivates the PSC.

**Example 57.2 Building a public sector comparator for a justice center (Illustrative)** (`ex:57.2`). In 2025 the Ministry of the Attorney General of a Canadian province (unnamed) compares conventional delivery of the 14-courtroom Ashcombe Justice Center (fictional) with a 30-year DBFM PPP. Units CAD millions, nominal. Discount rate 6.0%.
Inputs: construction in years 1 to 3 of CAD 61.4, 78.9 and 52.6 million (total 192.9); public operations and lifecycle from year 4 to year 33 of CAD 5.84 million in year 4 escalating 2.5% a year; competitive neutrality adjustment 2.1% of raw PSC present cost; transferable risk = 10.9% of construction PV (from Example 57.3) plus 4.0% of operations PV; retained risk CAD 9.3 million (same under both options); PPP unitary charge CAD 17.62 million in year 4 escalating 2.5% a year for 30 years; contract management CAD 4.6 million (PV).
Expected results at 6.0%: raw PSC 261.2 (construction PV 172.3, operations PV 88.9); competitive neutrality 5.5; transferable risk 22.3; retained 9.3; PSC 298.4. PPP: unitary charge PV 268.3 + retained 9.3 + contract management 4.6 = 282.2. VfM CAD 16.1 million, 5.4% of PSC. Implied private cost of delivering the raw scope (IRR of unitary charge less public operating cost against construction cost) 6.31%. Exhibit 57.2 tabulates the build.
Sensitivity (continued in 57.2.4 and 57.2.5): at 5.0% the PSC is 317.8 and PPP 327.2 (VfM minus 9.5, minus 3.0%); at 4.5% the PSC is 328.9 and PPP 353.5 (VfM minus 24.7, minus 7.5%); at 3.5% PSC 354.4 and PPP 415.5 (minus 61.1, minus 17.2%). Breakeven discount rate 5.34%. With transferable risk cut by 30%, VfM falls to CAD 9.4 million (3.2%). VfM stays positive at 6.0% until transferable risk is cut to 27.7% of its estimate. Breakeven year-4 unitary charge CAD 18.68 million (6.0% above the bid). Lesson: the unitary charge is back-ended, so a lower discount rate penalizes the PPP; and VfM here rests on risk numbers that are themselves estimates.

**Example 57.3 Pricing construction risk as an expected value (Illustrative)** (`ex:57.3`). Same project and province, 2025. The provincial cost estimator's distribution for public-delivery construction overruns on CAD 192.9 million: no overrun with probability 0.40; +8% with 0.30; +20% with 0.20; +45% with 0.10. Expected overrun 10.9%, CAD 21.0 million nominal. Show why a P50 estimate (here the 8% outcome) understates the mean when the distribution is skewed, and contrast with a flat optimism bias uplift (the concept is defined at ssec:9.9.1; this subsection owns only the uplift as a PSC input, R-043). State what is simplified (no delay cost, no correlation with operations risk).

**Example 57.4 Testing a program against an affordability ceiling (Illustrative)** (`ex:57.4`). In the 2025–26 budget round the treasury of an Australian state (unnamed) holds own-source revenue of AUD 46,837 million and a policy cap of 4.0% of revenue on annual PPP service payments: AUD 1,873.5 million. Existing commitments AUD 1,731.6 million; headroom AUD 141.9 million. Three proposed projects (fictional: the Kerrigan Road duplication, a 190-bed regional hospital and a corrections facility) with first-year payments of AUD 38.1, 52.7 and 61.4 million total AUD 152.2 million, so the program breaches the cap by AUD 10.3 million in year 1. Then indexation: payments are indexed at 2.9% a year while revenue grows 2.3% a year, so the breach grows to AUD 21.9 million in year 2 and AUD 60.1 million in year 5 (ceiling 2,051.9 against payments 2,112.0). Deferring the AUD 61.4 million project restores AUD 51.1 million of headroom in year 1 but only AUD 8.7 million by year 5. The reader works out which project to defer or restructure, and why testing first-year payments alone understates the breach once indexation and revenue growth diverge.
Expected results: as stated (cap 1,873.48; headroom 141.88; year-1 breach 10.32; year-5 breach 60.13; headroom after deferral 51.08 in year 1 and 8.70 in year 5).

**Example 57.5 Valuing a minimum revenue guarantee and a debt guarantee (Illustrative)** (`ex:57.5`). In 2026 Peru's transport ministry (the example's terms are invented and imply nothing about Peru's actual guarantee practice) offers a guarantee on a fictional 74 km toll road concession, the Autopista Sur Andina, in USD. Forecast year-1 revenue USD 84.6 million; the state guarantees 80% of forecast (USD 67.68 million) for the first 10 years. ## 7. Walkthrough

**57.9 Walkthrough: reading a value-for-money report.** The artifact is a generic VfM report for an availability-based PPP (structure only; numbers from Example 57.2). Steps:

1. Find the reference project and check that the PSC and the PPP deliver the same output specification and the same term; flag scope differences.
2. Check the discount rate and whether it is applied to nominal or real flows consistently on both sides.
3. Separate the PSC into raw cost, competitive neutrality, transferable risk and retained risk; compute what share of the PSC is risk adjustment (in Example 57.2, 22.3 + 9.3 of 298.4).
4. Trace each transferable risk to its evidence base (historical overrun data, optimism-bias tables, expert elicitation) and ask whether the same optimism is in the bidders' forecasts.
5. Check that retained risks are the same under both options and that contract-management costs are included on the PPP side.
6. Run the flip test (Framework 57.1): discount rate, transferable-risk scale, and any single risk line large enough to reverse the sign.
7. Read the qualitative VfM section for claims that are not costed and decide whether each one is evidence or advocacy.
8. Compare the affordability section with the VfM section: same payment profile, indexation, and budget years.
9. Check the fiscal-treatment section: classification view, contingent liabilities listed and valued (Example 57.5 method).
10. Write the three questions you would put to the PPP unit before Cabinet sees the report.

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 57.1 Value-for-money flip test** (`fw:vfm-flip`), original. Purpose: show decision-makers how fragile a VfM margin is. Steps: (1) list every input that differs between PSC and PPP; (2) for each, find the value at which VfM is zero (breakeven); (3) express each breakeven as a distance from the base value in the input's natural units (percentage points of discount rate, share of risk estimate); (4) rank by distance; (5) state, for the top two, the evidence that would move them. Demonstrated on Example 57.2 (breakeven discount rate 5.34%; transferable risk breakeven at 27.7% of estimate).
- **Framework 57.2 PPP decision gateway** (`fw:ppp-gateway`), original. Purpose: a sequence of tests a finance ministry runs before deciding to procure as a PPP. Steps: (1) need and output specification stable over the contract term; (2) affordability within the envelope including indexation; (3) VfM positive and robust under the flip test; (4) market appetite (bidders, lenders) confirmed by sounding; (5) fiscal treatment and contingent liabilities valued and within ceilings; (6) public legitimacy and contract-management capacity. Each test has a stop rule. Demonstrated on Case T in the installment.
- **Public sector comparator method**, established; credited as the method in national PPP guidance and in the World Bank-led *PPP Reference Guide* (Version 3, 2017; t-ppp-frameworks facts 1 and 2). No sheet verifies the history of the PSC method or names a national PSC manual beyond the NAO material, so the writer credits it generically and names no other guidance.
- **IMF–World Bank PPP Fiscal Risk Assessment Model (PFRAM)**, established (launched April 2016; version 2 guideline 2019); described only at the level of t-ppp-frameworks facts 7 to 9 (no internal tests or version contents, per the sheet).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (for a PPP unit before recommending a PPP):
- Output specification frozen and testable for the full term.
- PSC and PPP built on the same scope, term, indexation and discount convention.
- Each transferable risk traced to data, not to adviser judgment alone.
- Flip test run and the two most fragile inputs named in the Cabinet paper.
- Affordability tested on indexed payments for every budget year, against the program ceiling.
- Contingent liabilities listed, valued (expected and stress) and inside the guarantee ceiling, with a budget line for calls.
- Classification view obtained before bids, not after.
- Contract-management budget and team approved for the life of the contract.

Red flags:
- Risk adjustments larger than the VfM margin many times over.
- A PSC whose traffic or demand forecast comes from the same source as the bidders'.
- A discount rate chosen after the PSC was built.
- Design features justified only by "keeps it off balance sheet".
- A guarantee ceiling with no budget appropriation behind it.
- An unsolicited proposal whose proponent asks for exclusivity before any challenge.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Risk adjustments routinely make up most of the VfM margin; when the margin is smaller than any single risk line, treat VfM as unproven (limit: does not apply when the PPP is cheaper on raw cost, which is rare).
- A one-percentage-point fall in the discount rate swings VfM on a 30-year back-ended payment profile by several percent of PSC (Example 57.2: from 5.4% to minus 7.5% for 1.5 points); limit: shorter or front-loaded payment profiles are less sensitive.
- Expected-value guarantee costs understate fiscal risk because bad years cluster; always report a stress value.

Common mistakes: comparing nominal PPP payments with a real PSC; counting risk once in the cash flows and again in the discount rate; omitting contract-management cost; treating a guarantee cap as a budget; testing affordability only in year 1.

Questions experts ask: Which single number would reverse this recommendation? Who wrote the risk register and were they paid by the outcome? What does the state pay if the private party walks away in year 3? Is there a budget line for a guarantee call? Would the project be done at all without the PPP, and if not, is the comparator real?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** It is March 2026 and you head the fiscal risks unit of the treasury of a Brazilian state (fictional: the state of Serra Alta). The PPP unit recommends procuring a 22 km user-pay toll road as a DBFOM concession. The VfM report shows a margin of 4.1% of PSC; transferable traffic risk is 38% of the PSC's risk adjustments. The sponsoring ministry wants a minimum revenue guarantee at 75% of forecast to attract bidders, and the Cabinet paper is due in nine days. (Illustrative.)

**Reasoning it through.** The expert separates three questions: whether VfM survives without the traffic-risk line (run the flip test; with a 4.1% margin it almost certainly does not if traffic risk is overstated); whether the guarantee takes back the very risk that produces the margin (an MRG at 75% returns most of the downside to the state, so the transferable traffic risk in the PSC must be reduced accordingly and VfM recomputed); and what the guarantee costs in expected and stress terms (Example 57.5 method). The answer a top practitioner gives: do not recommend the PPP with the MRG on the current numbers; either recommend procurement without a guarantee and test market appetite through sounding, or switch to availability payments with demand risk retained and recompute VfM honestly. Put the stress cost of the MRG in the Cabinet paper.

**What would change the answer.** An independent traffic study that narrows the forecast range (supports user-pay without a guarantee). A bidder market that refuses demand risk at any price (moves toward availability payments). A guarantee priced and charged to the concessionaire (reduces the net fiscal cost). A fiscal rule that counts the MRG at expected value in the deficit (makes the cost visible and changes the politics).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A PPP contract is written by the government before any bidder sees it, and most of what later goes right or wrong was decided in that draft and in the tender rules. This chapter teaches the reader to design both: choosing the delivery model and who carries demand risk; running a procurement from market sounding to financial close; designing evaluation rules that pick the best bid rather than the boldest; drafting a payment mechanism with deductions that bite without bankrupting the project company; handling change, benchmarking and market testing over 30 years; setting termination compensation by ground; sharing refinancing gains; securing handback; and using standard contract guidance. Real cases (Purple Line, Port of Miami Tunnel, UK PFI refinancings, REIPPPP, Carillion, Sydney's tunnels) supply the lessons. Case T runs from shortlist to the May 27, 2015 financial close.

What you will be able to do (binding for the capability map, with the practice items that deliver each):

- Design a PPP or IPP procurement and an evaluation method, and recognize a bid that is too low to be safe (Capability 9). Delivered by Example 58.1, Walkthrough 58.12, the judgment drill, Exercises 58.6 and 58.16.

**Example 58.1 Scoring three bids under two price formulas (Illustrative)** (`ex:58.1`). In May 2025 a Dutch provincial authority (fictional: Provincie Lindeveld) evaluates three bids for a 25-year DBFM contract to widen and maintain its N-road network. Weight 60 price, 40 quality. Bid NPVs (EUR millions) and quality scores out of 40 from three fictional consortia: A (Polderweg Infra) 418.3 and 31.5; B (Kanaalzone Partners) 396.4 and 27.0; C (Lindeveld Mobiliteit) 441.9 and 37.2.
Formula 1 (price score = 60 × lowest / bid): A 56.86, B 60.00, C 53.82; totals A 88.36, B 87.00, C 91.02; C wins.
Formula 2 (60 minus 1.5 points per 1% above lowest): A 51.71, B 60.00, C 42.78; totals A 83.21, B 87.00, C 79.98; B wins.
Lesson: the formula, not just the weights, decides how much quality is worth in money; under Formula 1 the authority implicitly pays EUR 45.5 million in NPV (441.9 − 396.4) for 10.2 quality points. The writer states that EUR 45.5 million figure and asks whether the authority would have paid it knowingly.

**Example 58.2 Letting traffic set the concession term (Illustrative)** (`ex:58.2`). In 2025 Chile's Ministry of Public Works (the example's terms are invented; no real Chilean contract terms are implied) awards a fictional 148 km coastal toll motorway, the Ruta Costera Norte, on the least present value of revenue (LPVR) in real terms at 6.5%, values in USD for illustration. Winning LPVR bid USD 1,284.6 million (2025 prices) from a fictional consortium, Autopistas del Pacífico Norte. The concession ends when the PV of collected revenue reaches the LPVR, with a 40-year maximum. Scenarios (year-1 revenue, real growth): high 112.4 and 3.5%; base 96.8 and 3.0%; low 74.2 and 2.0%.
Results: term 14.7 years (high), 18.7 years (base), 35.0 years (low). Under a fixed 30-year term the PV of revenue would have been 2,156.8, 1,750.8 and 1,197.3, so the low case would leave the concessionaire USD 87.3 million short of its LPVR in PV terms while the high case would deliver a USD 872.2 million windfall. Lesson: the LPVR design transfers most demand risk back to the state through term, lowers the cost of capital, and makes early termination compensation simple (remaining LPVR less collected PV). Equation 58.1. Real-world LPVR use is stated from chile-concessions (summary and facts 9 to 12): first outright PVR auction in February 1998, further auctions in 2007–2008; the termination payment equals the bid less the PV of revenue collected; LPVR weakens incentives for demand-enhancing effort and needs quality standards and revenue verification.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (authority, before issuing the RFP): bid variable chosen and justified; evaluation formula tested on dummy bids for perverse rankings; committed-finance requirement and bid security set; payment mechanism tested with the deduction calibration test; termination compensation for every ground drafted and costed; refinancing gain share and debt cap in the compensation formula; change protocol and benchmarking timetable; handback requirements with survey dates and security; standard form departures listed.

Red flags: winning bid far below the reference and the next bid; a bidder's revenue forecast above every independent forecast; deductions so large that one bad month breaches lock-up, or so small that ignoring a failure is cheaper than fixing it; termination rights triggered by elapsed time alone; a refinancing clause without a debt cap in compensation; FM pass-down caps that leave the project company holding most deductions.

Rules of thumb and their limits: deductions should cost more than prompt rectification (limit: does not hold for failures the project company cannot fix, which belong in relief events); preferred-bidder phases longer than about six months erode price certainty (limit: depends on rate volatility and on whether a funding competition is run); a debt floor in contractor-default compensation lowers the cost of debt and raises the state's contingent liability by the same logic.

Common mistakes: a price formula that rewards quality beyond its value; deduction weightings that do not sum to 100%; indexation applied to the debt-service portion of the charge; compensation formulas that count debt drawn after an unapproved refinancing; handback surveys too late to fund the works.

Questions experts ask: What does the state pay if this contract ends on day 400 for each ground? Which bid variable makes bidders reveal their real view of risk? How much deduction can the debt absorb? Who funds a capital change and at what rate? What happens if the contractor is solvent but walks?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** It is June 2026 and you are the director of the PPP unit of a fictional Australian state, Carrick. The BAFO for a 38-year user-pay toll road concession, the Weld River Crossing, gives you a preferred bidder whose required state contribution is 26.7% below your reference and 17.9% below the second bidder. The bidder's traffic case is its own adviser's; your adviser's case is 9% lower in the mature year. The bidder's banks have issued committed letters subject to their own traffic adviser's review. The minister wants an announcement in two weeks. (Illustrative; the percentages deliberately differ from Case T's so the drill cannot be answered from the installment.)

**Reasoning it through.** The expert weighs three things in order: whether the state carries any of the traffic risk the low bid relies on (under full user-pay with no debt floor, the immediate fiscal exposure is small, but distress later carries implicit costs: political pressure, renegotiation, relief demands); whether the low bid is a symptom of mispricing that will produce a failed project and a renegotiation (abnormally low bid process: demand the bidder's explanation, sensitivity of its DSCR, and confirmation that the lenders sized on their own adviser's lower case); and the cost of delay versus the second bid. A top practitioner appoints the preferred bidder subject to conditions: lenders' sizing on their own adviser's case with a minimum equity amount, a sponsor support commitment until traffic is proven, and no relief-event expansion in final negotiations. Choosing the second bid is defensible only if the first fails those conditions.

**What would change the answer.** A debt floor in contractor-default compensation (state exposure rises; be stricter). A government plan that depends on the road's success for related development (implicit liability rises; be stricter). Lenders already sized on the lower case (accept with fewer conditions). A thin market where re-tendering adds two years (accept with conditions).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A contracted power plant in a weak economy fails less often because the plant breaks than because the country runs short of dollars or the offtaker runs short of cash. This chapter teaches the reader to analyze country and sovereign risk, to separate the three currency risks (devaluation, convertibility, transfer) and the liquidity problem of a foreign-exchange queue, to choose among the solutions (indexed or hard-currency tariffs, local-currency debt, convertibility undertakings, offshore accounts, devaluation stress sizing), and to design and size a payment security package for a weak offtaker: letters of credit, escrow, government guarantees, liquidity facilities and netting. Paiton, Dabhol, Argentina 2002 and Azura-Edo supply the real record. Case P lives through SEKA's 2022 to 2024 crisis: the arrears, the FX queue, the LC drawing, the guarantee demands, the gas netting agreement and the settlement.

Breakeven with no indexation: DSCR 1.00x at a 40.0% rise in LCY per USD (the currency losing 28.6% of its value). Lesson: full indexation protects the DSCR (and even improves it because local opex falls in USD), but only by moving the whole shock to the utility, which is the next example's problem.

**Example 59.3 The cost of a conversion lag (Illustrative)** (`ex:59.3`). A monthly USD 4.86 million invoice is paid in local currency at the rate on the day before payment, but the central bank's allocation queue delays conversion by 47 days while the currency depreciates 3.1% a month. Loss = 4.86 × (1 − 1/1.031^(47/30.4)) = USD 0.224 million, 4.61% of the invoice. Equation 59.4. Over twelve monthly invoices the loss is 12 × 0.224 = USD 2.69 million a year. Case P's 47-day average lag is an input; its FX losses are inputs (Case Bible 1.9), not computed here.

**Example 59.4 Sizing a standby LC and counting the months of runway (Illustrative)** (`ex:59.4`). A 300 MW gas plant with a capacity charge of USD 13.92/kW-month (USD 4.176 million a month) and estimated energy charges of USD 6.37 million a month. LC sized at two months of capacity plus one month of energy: USD 14.72 million; at three months of capacity and energy: USD 31.64 million. Monthly cash needs if the offtaker pays nothing: operating costs USD 1.94 million, gas USD 6.37 million (the gas seller is paid even if the offtaker is not), debt service USD 23.6 million semiannual (USD 3.933 million a month): total USD 12.243 million. Resources: LC 14.72, DSRA 23.6, cash 8.4: USD 46.72 million; runway 3.82 months. Equation 59.3. With the larger LC the runway is (31.64 + 23.6 + 8.4) / 12.243 = 63.64 / 12.243 = 5.20 months. Lesson: an LC sized in months of billing buys fewer months of survival because fuel and debt service continue.

**Example 59.5 PLN's May 1999 payment (Real case: Paiton I, 1994–2003)** (`ex:59.5`). **Example 59.6 Pesification arithmetic (Real case: Argentina, 2002; Illustrative distributor figures)** (`ex:59.6`). ## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 59.1 Country risk scorecard** (`fw:country-scorecard`), original. Purpose: a structured, weighted reading of a country for a long-tenor project loan. Steps: (1) score five areas on 1 to 5 with stated indicators; (2) weight by the deal's exposure (USD-indexed PPAs weight the FX regime more heavily); (3) compute the composite; (4) read the worst-scoring line against the deal's own structure; (5) name the instrument that mitigates that line or decline. Demonstrated in Example 59.1 and on Kessara in the installment.
- **Framework 59.2 Payment security stack** (`fw:payment-stack`), original. Purpose: design payment protection in layers that answer different questions. Layers: liquidity (LC, escrow, liquidity facility: does the project get paid this month?), credit (guarantee, PRG: does someone solvent stand behind the offtaker?), termination (put option, termination compensation, guarantee of termination sum: if the contract ends, who pays the debt?). Steps: map each instrument to a layer; check that no two layers rest on the same fiscal capacity without an external backstop; size the liquidity layer in months of runway; test the cure and replenishment provisions. Demonstrated on Azura (Example 59.7) and Case P.
- **Months-of-runway test** (part of Framework 59.2), Equation 59.3.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (payment and currency package at term sheet): currency of revenue, cost and debt mapped; devaluation case run and breakeven stated; indexation share, reference rate and lag defined; convertibility and transfer undertaking and exchange-control authorization in hand as CPs; offshore accounts and sweep frequency agreed; LC size, confirmer rating, replenishment period and cure provisions drafted; guarantee demand mechanics and budget appropriation verified; DFI liquidity support or PRG in place where the LC bank needs it; months of runway computed with fuel and debt service included.

Red flags: an offtaker whose retail tariff has not moved in two years while its purchase costs are FX-indexed; an LC replenishment obligation with a regulatory cure that has no deadline; a guarantee without a budget line; central bank reserves below three months of imports; a queue or "prioritization list" for FX; every support layer issued by the same ministry.

Rules of thumb and their limits: payment-security LCs in emerging-market IPPs are commonly sized at two to three months of billing (indicative; give market and period only from t-market-norms); limit: months of billing overstate months of survival when fuel is a pass-through cost the project still pays. Full USD indexation protects the DSCR but transfers the shock to a party that may not survive it; partial indexation plus a liquidity facility often protects lenders better.

Common mistakes: treating a USD-indexed tariff as USD revenue; sizing the LC on capacity charges only; forgetting the conversion lag; assuming a capped guarantee will be paid on time; counting the DSRA as payment security without reducing the distributions it protects.

Questions experts ask: In what currency and from what account does the offtaker actually pay? Who allocates FX in a shortage and in what order? How many months does the project survive if the offtaker pays nothing? Who confirms the LC, and is that bank's claim on the state backed by a DFI? If the guarantee is called, which budget pays it?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are the facility agent's credit officer for a 450 MW thermal IPP in a West African country (Illustrative; not Case P). The offtaker's arrears reached 3.4 months of billing; the LC was drawn last month and not replenished; the offtaker offers a regulator-approved payment plan with 12 monthly installments and asks for a 90-day standstill on the guarantee demand. The central bank has an FX queue averaging 52 days. The sponsors want to keep distributions open from the cash already offshore. You must recommend a position to the lenders within five days.

**Reasoning it through.** The expert separates liquidity from credit: the immediate question is runway (compute months of resources against needs, including fuel), the second is whether the payment plan cures or defers a breach, and the third is whether delaying the guarantee demand weakens the termination layer. The likely answer: accept the payment plan only with a replenishment schedule for the LC, interest on arrears, a netting arrangement with the state fuel supplier if one exists, and a reservation of rights on the guarantee demand (serve it, agree to defer payment, preserve the date); block distributions until the LC is restored and arrears fall below a set level; engage the DFI behind the PRG early.

**What would change the answer.** An IMF-style stabilization program with FX allocation for IPP debt service (softer). A second offtaker or export revenue (softer). A fuel supplier that stops delivering (harder: runway shortens). A guarantee claim period that expires if not served now (serve immediately). Political risk insurance whose waiting period starts only on a formal demand (serve demand; see Ch 60).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Political risk is the risk that a state, the host or the sponsor's own, changes the rules after the money is sunk. This chapter teaches the reader to name the perils precisely, to understand why bargaining power shifts to the host once a project is built (the obsolescing bargain) and how to structure against it, to manage host-government relations and local content, to buy and claim on political risk insurance from MIGA, ECAs and the private market, to judge what the multilateral halo does and does not deter, to comply with anti-bribery laws (FCPA, UK Bribery Act) and sanctions regimes, and to run a dispute or treaty claim as a business decision. Real cases span Mozambique LNG, Oyu Tolgoi, Cobre Panamá, Chad–Cameroon, Hub Power, Dabhol, Argentina, Spain and the 2022 sanctions. Case P buys private PRI in 2018, faces a minister attacking its "dollar tariff" in 2022, and decides in 2023 not to claim.

What you will be able to do:

- Map a project's political risks to the instruments that respond, and know which perils no instrument covers (Capability 8).
- Read a political risk insurance policy and decide whether, when and how to claim (Capabilities 3 and 8).
- Structure a project so the host's incentive to honor the deal survives after construction (Capabilities 8 and 15).
- Identify bribery and sanctions exposure in a deal's chain of parties and draft the protections lenders require (Capability 6).

## 3. Concepts

### 3.1 Concepts owned

1. The political perils: direct and creeping expropriation, political violence (war, terrorism, civil disturbance), breach of contract by a state or state entity, non-honoring of sovereign financial obligations, currency inconvertibility and transfer restriction (as a PRI peril; analysis in Ch 59), adverse regulatory change, sanctions imposed by home states.
2. The obsolescing bargain: mechanism, quantification of extractable value, and structuring responses (local and DFI equity, phased investment, affordable tariffs, stakeholder alignment, export structures); the obsolescing bargain clock (original framework).
3. Host-government relations: mapping power and interests, relationship management through government changes, the role of the local partner.
4. Local content: employment, procurement, equity and training obligations; costs, benefits and drafting.
5. Political risk insurance: providers (MIGA, ECAs and bilateral insurers, the private market), covers and triggers, waiting periods, exclusions (including devaluation and non-discriminatory regulatory change), pricing, tenor, claims and subrogation; comparison with ECA comprehensive cover and DFI guarantees.
6. The multilateral halo: deterrence through DFI participation and preferred creditor status (mechanics owned by Ch 29), its evidence and its limits.

## 6. Worked examples

All (Illustrative) figures computed in Python by the brief writer.

**Example 60.1 How much a host can take after the plant is built (Illustrative)** (`ex:60.1`). A 450 MW gas plant in a Southeast Asian country, capex USD 486.0 million sunk. Annual revenue USD 118.4 million; operating costs and fuel USD 41.7 million; senior debt service USD 52.3 million; equity cash USD 24.4 million; DSCR 1.47x. A tariff cut of 10% leaves DSCR at 1.24x. A cut of 20.6% wipes out equity cash and leaves DSCR at 1.00x. The plant still covers operating costs until the cut reaches 64.8%, so a sponsor that maximizes going-forward cash keeps running through any cut below that level. Equation 60.1: extractable share before shutdown = (Rev − Opex) / Rev. Lesson: after construction, the host can extract up to 64.8% of revenue before the plant rationally stops; equity bears the first 20.6 points and lenders the next 44.2. The structuring response is to raise the host's cost of extracting it (Framework 60.2).

**Example 60.2 What PRI costs and what it saves (Illustrative)** (`ex:60.2`). A USD 186.0 million uncovered commercial tranche for a 450 MW plant; PRI covering 90% of principal plus six months of interest (insured amount 0.9 × (186.0 + 13.9 / 2) = USD 173.66 million) at 1.15% a year: premium USD 1.997 million a year, USD 0.37/kW-month equivalent. Lenders price the covered tranche at a margin of 2.95% instead of 4.40%; saving 1.45% on USD 186.0 million = USD 2.697 million a year; net saving to the project company USD 0.700 million a year. Lesson: PRI pays for itself only when it changes the lenders' pricing or capital treatment; the sponsor should ask for the margin with and without cover.

**Example 60.3 The loss probability a PRI premium implies (Illustrative)** (`ex:60.3`). Premium 1.15% a year. If a covered event causes a total loss of the insured amount, the break-even annual probability is 1.15%. If the insurer expects to recover 40% through salvage and subrogation (loss given claim 60%), the implied annual probability is 1.15% / 0.60 = 1.92%. Equation 60.2. The reader compares this with their own view of the country (Ch 59 scorecard) and decides whether cover is cheap or dear. Simplification stated: ignores insurer expenses and capital cost, which means the true implied probability is lower.

**Example 60.4 Screening an intermediary (Illustrative)** (`ex:60.4`). During a 2025 tender for a 120 MW solar IPP in a North African country, a consultant introduced by a ministry official offers "permitting facilitation" for a 3.0% success fee on project cost (USD 4.6 million on USD 153.4 million), payable to an offshore account in a third country, with no deliverables schedule. The example runs the red flags (referral by an official, success fee, offshore payment, vague services), the legal exposure under the FCPA and the UK Bribery Act (as stated in t-anticorruption-sanctions, planned), the lenders' representation and covenant (Clause 60.1), and the right response (decline, document, report as policy requires). No real persons or countries' officials are named.

**Example 60.5 Applying the 50 percent rule (Illustrative)** (`ex:60.5`). A shipping company proposed for an LNG charter is owned 30% by designated person A and 25% by designated person B; together 55%, so it is treated as blocked under the 50 percent aggregation rule; a subsidiary 60% owned by that company is also blocked. A second shipowner owned 45% by designated person C and controlled through a board seat is not blocked by ownership but is a red flag under control-based EU and UK tests. The rule's exact wording and the EU and UK control tests are stated only from t-anticorruption-sanctions (planned).

**Example 60.6 The expected value of a treaty claim (Illustrative)** (`ex:60.6`). A claim of USD 300.0 million after a tariff repudiation. Probability of winning 55%; expected award 60% of the claim; annulment risk 15%; collection probability 70%; six years to cash; costs USD 18.0 million spread evenly over six years; discount rate 12%. Expected nominal recovery 300.0 × 0.55 × 0.60 × 0.85 × 0.70 = USD 58.9 million; PV at 12% over six years USD 29.84 million; PV of costs 3.0 × 4.1114 = USD 12.33 million; net PV USD 17.51 million, or 5.8% of the claim. Equation 60.3. Lesson: a treaty claim is a negotiating asset worth far less than its face, which is why settlements such as Hub's and talks such as Cobre Panamá's happen.

**Example 60.7 Hub Power's 2000 settlement (Real case: Hub Power, 1998–2000)** (`ex:60.7`). Facts (hub-power summary and teaching angle): allegations from 1998 that 1993 to 1994 PPA amendments were corruptly obtained (none proven); local courts blocked ICC arbitration; Supreme Court upheld the block 3–2 on June 14, 2000; settlement December 17, 2000 cut the levelized tariff from 6.5 to 5.6 US cents/kWh (a 13.8% cut); lenders' debt service protected, equity return cut from 18% to about 12% to 14%. Lesson: under pressure the host cut equity, not debt; the World Bank PRG and multilateral presence protected lenders but could not keep the original bargain. Do-not-state list applies (no named individuals, no convictions).

**Example 60.8 When creeping pressure misses the policy trigger (Real case: Oyu Tolgoi, 2009–2023)** (`ex:60.8`). Facts (oyu-tolgoi summary): USD 4.4 billion project financing December 15, 2015 with IFC and EBRD A and B loans, ECAs, 15 commercial banks and MIGA cover; tax assessment of about USD 155 million taken to UNCITRAL arbitration in 2020; December 2019 parliamentary resolution; court challenge to the 2015 plan; January 2022 agreement including the waiver of the USD 2.4 billion carry account loan; underground production from March 2023. ## 7. Walkthrough

**60.9 Walkthrough: reading a political risk insurance policy.** The artifact is an original illustrative lenders' PRI policy (private market style) covering a commercial tranche. Steps:

1. Insured, insured loan, insured percentage (for example 90%) and the insured amount definition (principal plus how much interest).
2. Covered perils and their definitions: expropriation (including creeping), political violence, inconvertibility and transfer, non-honoring of a government guarantee; compare with the deal's actual exposures (Framework 60.1).
3. Exclusions: devaluation, losses caused by the insured's own breach, non-discriminatory regulatory measures, sanctions-related losses, losses arising from corruption by the insured.
4. Waiting periods for each peril and when they start (event, demand, award).
5. Conditions and warranties: duty of disclosure, sanctions and anti-corruption warranties, notification of circumstances within a fixed period, cooperation in mitigation.
6. Claims procedure: proof of loss, assignment of rights, subrogation, salvage sharing.
7. Premium, payment, cancellation rights, and what happens on refinancing or prepayment.
8. Governing law and dispute resolution with the insurer.
9. The assignment to the security agent and the loss payee arrangements (cross-reference Ch 27).
10. A one-page note to lenders: what the policy covers in this deal, what it does not, and the two scenarios in which a claim would actually pay.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (political risk at term sheet): instrument map completed and every uncovered peril has an owner; PRI or ECA cover tenor matches the loan; waiting periods and claim triggers aligned with guarantee and PPA timelines; anti-corruption and sanctions representations, covenants and policies agreed; intermediaries screened and contracts audited; local content obligations measurable and achievable; treaty-protected holding structure reviewed with tax (Ch 54, Ch 67); host relationship plan names who meets whom after an election.

Red flags: a project whose benefits to the host fall sharply after commissioning; tariffs far above the system's alternatives; a minister attacking the contract in public; parliamentary resolutions or court challenges to the concession's approval; success fees to intermediaries introduced by officials; any counterparty with designated owners near 50%; an ECA whose government is under domestic pressure on the sector.

Rules of thumb and their limits: PRI is worth buying when it changes lender pricing, capital treatment or appetite, not as general comfort (limit: equity investors may buy it for board or covenant reasons); the halo protects debt better than equity (limit: deliberate state action only; useless against violence or sanctions); treaty claims recover a fraction of face value after years (limit: settlement value can be much higher than expected award value).

Common mistakes: assuming PRI covers devaluation; letting a waiting period lapse by not serving a formal demand; buying cover whose perils do not match the actual exposure (expropriation cover when the real risk is non-payment by a state utility); treating sanctions as a compliance item rather than a credit risk; local content commitments without a training plan.

Questions experts ask: What would the host gain by breaking this deal in year 5, and what would it lose? Which of our perils has no instrument? When does each waiting period start, and who must do what to start it? Who introduced this intermediary? Which of our counterparties, owners or vessels could be designated next year?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You lead the lenders' insurance and political risk workstream on a USD 640 million gas-to-power project in a lower-middle-income country (Illustrative). The commercial banks' credit committees will lend uncovered at 4.40% or with 90% private PRI at 2.95%; the premium quoted is 1.20% a year on the insured amount, with a 180-day waiting period on non-honoring and an exclusion for losses "arising from any change in law of general application". The sponsor argues the IA's stabilization clause and a DFI A/B loan make PRI unnecessary. Credit committee meets Thursday.

**Reasoning it through.** The expert runs the instrument map: the stabilization clause gives a claim, not cash; the A/B loan protects the DFI's participants, not the uncovered banks; the PRI's change-in-law exclusion removes the most likely creeping peril (tariff or tax change), so the cover protects mainly against expropriation, violence and non-honoring of the guarantee. Economics: the margin saving (1.45% on the covered tranche) against premium (1.20% on 90% plus interest) leaves a small net saving; the cover's value is mainly the banks' willingness to lend at the tenor needed. The likely recommendation: buy the PRI, negotiate the change-in-law exclusion down to non-discriminatory measures that do not target the sector or the project, align the non-honoring waiting period with the guarantee's payment period, and keep the DFI in the structure for the halo.

**What would change the answer.** A DFI partial risk guarantee available for the same perils at lower cost (replace PRI). A host with an investment-grade rating (drop PRI). An insurer refusing to narrow the exclusion (reconsider: the cover may not respond to the realistic peril). A sanctions exposure in the gas supply chain (cover will exclude it; structure the supply instead).
