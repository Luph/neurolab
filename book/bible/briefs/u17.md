# Chapter 85. Screening deals and reading data rooms

## 85-1. Header

- Chapter: 85
- Title: Screening deals and reading data rooms
- File: `chapters/85-screening-deals-and-reading-data-rooms.tex`
- Target length: 10,500 words (craft chapter with heavy calculation and a full walkthrough)
- Dominant expert lens: a lead arranger and a sponsor-side investment director who between them screen several hundred opportunities a year and take perhaps one in twenty to a mandate. The independent engineer and the PPP unit official appear in the data-room sections; the contractor's seat appears in the "build" block of the screen.

## 85-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Every financier spends most of their working life saying no, quickly and for the right reason, and the rest of it deciding where to spend a scarce diligence budget. This chapter turns the whole book into a fast, repeatable instrument: a one-hour screen that answers one question (is this worth the next USD 250,000 of time and advisors, and on what conditions?), a short set of questions that work on any deal in any sector, and a disciplined way to read a data room so that the reader finds the deciding facts, and the missing documents, before the sponsor's narrative sets the frame. The chapter is calculation-heavy in a narrow way: three-line CFADS, an annuity shortcut for debt capacity, two or three breakevens and an affordability ratio, each done by hand or on one spreadsheet tab, each with its error stated.

What you will be able to do (for the `\section*`):

- Screen any project teaser or information package in under an hour and name the four to six risks that will decide whether it is bankable, with a proceed, proceed-with-conditions or decline verdict (Capability 2).
- Estimate debt capacity, equity return and the breakevens that matter from a handful of inputs, and say how far each estimate could be wrong (Capabilities 2 and 5).
- Read a data room in the order that exposes the deciding facts fastest, trace one number through every document, and list what is missing (Capability 7).
- Ask the questions that expose a deal's weak points from the sponsor's, lender's, government's and contractor's seats (Capability 15).

**Example 85.1. Screening a contracted solar plant in an hour (Illustrative)** (`ex:85.1`)
- Scenario: a 148.6 MWac single-axis tracking solar plant in Andalusia, Spain, offered by a developer with a 12-year pay-as-produced corporate PPA at EUR 41.70/MWh flat nominal with an unnamed industrial buyer, then merchant to year 30.
- Inputs: P50 net capacity factor 24.3% (AC); one-year P90 92.1% of P50; one-year P99 87.4% of P50; operating cost EUR 2.38 million a year (flat for the screen); total project cost EUR 0.612 million per MWac; lenders' sizing at 1.30x on P50 and 1.00x on one-year P99; all-in cost of debt 5.15%; tenor 11 years (one-year tail inside the PPA); gearing cap 80%; merchant capture price after the PPA EUR 33.20/MWh flat (screen assumption, stated as such); tax ignored; no degradation (stated simplification).
- Computation and key results: P50 energy 148.6 x 8,760 x 0.243 = 316,321.8 MWh (316.3 GWh). Revenue EUR 13.191 million (P50), 12.149 (P90), 11.529 (P99). CFADS EUR 10.811 million (P50), 9.769 (P90), 9.149 (P99). Debt service allowed: 10.811 / 1.30 = 8.316 (P50 test); 9.149 / 1.00 = 9.149 (P99 test); P50 binds. Annuity factor at 5.15% for 11 years = 8.2414. Debt capacity = 8.316 x 8.2414 = EUR 68.53 million (P99 test would allow 75.40). Total cost 148.6 x 0.612 = EUR 90.94 million; gearing 75.4%, below the 80% cap (EUR 72.75 million), so the DSCR test binds. DSCR at the chosen debt service: P50 1.30x, P90 1.17x (1.175), P99 1.10x. Equity EUR 22.41 million; distributions EUR 2.495 million a year for 11 years, then merchant CFADS EUR 8.122 million a year for 19 years. Equity IRR 15.7% with the merchant tail; 3.5% if the plant earned nothing after year 11; project IRR 10.4%.
- Teaching point: the screen shows that the equity return lives almost entirely in the merchant tail, so the deciding risks for the sponsor are long-run capture prices and curtailment (Chapter 20 and Chapter 70), while the lender's deciding risk is the P90 year inside the PPA. Write Equation 85.1 here:
  - `eq:85.1`: $D_{\max} = \min\left(\frac{\mathrm{CFADS}^{\mathrm{P50}}}{\mathrm{DSCR}^{*}_{\mathrm{P50}}}, \frac{\mathrm{CFADS}^{\mathrm{P99}}}{\mathrm{DSCR}^{*}_{\mathrm{P99}}}\right) \times \frac{1-(1+i)^{-n}}{i}$, then $\min(D_{\max}, G_{\max} \times \text{total cost})$. Excel on a single screening tab: `=MIN(C12/C6, C13/C7)*PV(C8, C9, -1)` and `=MIN(C15, C10*C11)`, with cell layout stated in the text.
- Common error to show: using the PPA price on P50 at 1.00x ("the debt is paid if the plant runs as expected") gives 10.811 x 8.2414 = EUR 89.10 million, 98% of cost; the screen that does so is not a screen.

**Example 85.2. How far can traffic fall? (Illustrative)** (`ex:85.2`)
- Scenario: a 31.8 km greenfield toll motorway in northern Portugal (no concession grantor named; illustrative terms), operating year 3 forecast.
- Inputs: forecast AADT 31,420 vehicles; average toll per vehicle EUR 4.85; fixed operating cost EUR 9.40 million a year; variable cost 6.0% of revenue; scheduled debt service EUR 29.65 million; lock-up 1.20x.
- Results: revenue 31,420 x 365 x 4.85 = EUR 55.621 million; CFADS 55.621 x 0.94 - 9.40 = EUR 42.884 million; DSCR 1.45x (1.446). Required revenue for 1.00x: (29.65 + 9.40) / 0.94 = EUR 41.543 million, a 25.3% fall in traffic (AADT 23,467). For lock-up at 1.20x: (35.58 + 9.40) / 0.94 = EUR 47.851 million, a 14.0% fall (AADT 27,031).
- Equation `eq:85.2`: $\mathrm{Rev}^{\mathrm{BE}} = \frac{\mathrm{DSCR}^{\mathrm{test}} \times \mathrm{DS}_t + \mathrm{Opex}^{\mathrm{fixed}}_t}{1 - v}$, where $v$ is variable cost as a share of revenue; the percentage decline is $1 - \mathrm{Rev}^{\mathrm{BE}}/\mathrm{Rev}_t$. Excel: `=(C5*C4+C6)/(1-C7)` and `=1-C9/C3`.
- Teaching point: a 14% traffic shortfall in year 3 of a greenfield toll road is well inside ordinary forecast error (cross-reference Chapter 48 on traffic forecast error; no statistics are stated here because none is in a fact sheet); the screen's finding is that distributions are fragile even if the debt is not.

**Example 85.3. Can the utility pay? An affordability ratio (Illustrative)** (`ex:85.3`)
- Scenario: a state-owned single-buyer utility, deliberately unnamed and placed in no real country because any real utility would carry invented figures; the text says so in one clause and gives the numbers below.
- Inputs: utility revenue USD 1,412.6 million a year; existing IPP fixed (capacity) commitments USD 386.0 million a year; new 340 MW plant with a capacity charge of USD 13.85/kW-month.
- Results: new capacity payments 340,000 kW x 13.85 x 12 = USD 56.508 million; total fixed commitments USD 442.51 million; ratio rises from 27.3% to 31.3% of utility revenue.
- Teaching point: the ratio is a screen flag, not a test with a market threshold (no threshold is in a fact sheet, so none is stated); the question it forces is what happens to the 31.3% if the currency falls, retail tariffs freeze, or dispatch falls while capacity payments stay fixed. Cross-reference Chapter 59 for payment security.

**Example 85.4. One EPC price, three documents (Illustrative)** (`ex:85.4`)
- Scenario: a data room for a 340 MW gas plant (same illustrative plant as Example 85.3) contains three different EPC numbers.
- Inputs: EPC contract Schedule 7 price USD 312.45 million; Variation Order No. 1 (signed after the model was frozen) USD 4.18 million; owner-furnished spares included in the EPC price but modeled in owner's costs USD 2.65 million; a provisional sum in the EPC contract not yet instructed USD 1.37 million; the model's EPC line USD 309.80 million; the information memorandum states "EPC price USD 318.0 million".
- Results: 312.45 - 2.65 = 309.80 (the model, correctly excluding spares it carries elsewhere); 312.45 + 4.18 = 316.63 (contract plus signed variation); 316.63 + 1.37 = 318.00 (the IM, which adds an uninstructed provisional sum). Findings: the model omits the signed variation of USD 4.18 million, so total cost is understated by that amount unless contingency absorbs it; the IM includes an amount that is not yet a liability. Exhibit 85.4 shows the trace table: document, figure, what it includes, reconciling item, finding.
- Teaching point: the number trace takes ten minutes and finds the two most common data-room defects (stale model inputs and presentation numbers that mix commitments with allowances).

## 85-7. Walkthrough

**85.8 Walkthrough: screening a teaser in sixty minutes.** Artifact: an Illustrative four-page teaser (reproduced as Exhibit 85.5, text drafted originally) for a 220 MW onshore wind farm in north-eastern Brazil: 70% of P50 output sold under a 15-year corporate PPA in BRL to an unnamed mining company, 30% merchant; turbines from a supplier with a 4.5 MW platform; a 38 km connection line to be built by the project; a USD-denominated bank loan proposed; the sponsor a first-time developer backed by a fund. The writer sets illustrative numbers in the teaser (capacity factor, PPA price in BRL/MWh with its USD equivalent at a stated rate, capex) and verifies the screen arithmetic with code.

Steps, matching Framework 85.1's blocks:
1. Fill the one-page deal summary; mark the gaps (no PPA termination terms, no curtailment history, no grid-connection agreement status).
2. Revenue block: who pays (a mining company's credit); currency mismatch between BRL revenue and USD debt (pointer to Chapter 59); the 30% merchant share against the merchant share lenders tolerate (use only `t-market-norms` early-2026 US panel evidence that 25% to 30% merchant is a practical maximum, stated as US evidence, or omit).
3. Build block: turbine platform track record question; the 38 km line as the interface with no contract yet.
4. Killers: land titles, E&S category, integrity check on the first-time developer.
5. Numbers: Equation 85.1 on the contracted 70% only; breakeven price for the merchant 30%.

## 85-8. Frameworks

- **Framework 85.1, the one-hour deal screen** (`fw:one-hour-deal-screen`; original). Purpose: decide in one hour whether to spend the next tranche of diligence money, and on what conditions. Steps: (1) 0 to 10 minutes: the deal on one page; (2) 10 to 25: revenue; (3) 25 to 35: build and operate; (4) 35 to 45: killers (country, law and security, E&S and land, integrity); (5) 45 to 55: numbers (CFADS, Equation 85.1, equity return, two breakevens, affordability where relevant); (6) 55 to 60: verdict (proceed, proceed with conditions, decline), the deciding risks ranked, the information requests, the cost and time of the next step. Demonstrated in Example 85.1, the walkthrough, and the Case P installment.
- **Framework 85.2, the questions to ask on any deal** (`fw:deal-questions`; original). As many questions as the content needs (the writer must not force a round number; the brief's list has nine): Who pays, and why will they still pay in year 12? Can they, in the currency the debt is in? What has to go right in construction, and who pays if it does not? Which interface has no contract? What is the single largest number in the model, and who certified it? Who loses first, and do they control the decisions? What does the host government gain from the deal, and when does that gain run out (cross-reference the obsolescing bargain, Chapter 60)? What does each party do if it wants out? What is not in the data room? Each question is followed in the text by its reason and the seat that asks it hardest.
- **Framework 85.3, data room reading order** (`fw:data-room-reading-order`; original). Steps as in 85.5.2, with time budgets for a first pass of four hours.
- Frameworks used, not owned: risk matrix and bankability ladder (Chapter 15), contract gap scan and "Who pays if...?" trace (Chapter 28; `fw:who-pays-if`), the DD request lists (Chapters 48 to 50).

## 85-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (each item a full sentence with its reason):
- Fill the one-page summary before reading any narrative section of the teaser.
- Find the termination schedule of the revenue contract before the tariff schedule.
- Identify every asset the project needs that someone else builds or owns.
- Count reference plants for the technology at this scale and in this climate.
- Run Equation 85.1 on the lender's downside volume, not only on P50.
- Compute the breakeven to lock-up as well as to 1.00x.
- Read the Q&A log and the upload dates before the reports.
- Run one number trace on the largest cost line and one on the revenue line.
- Write the absence index before requesting documents.
- Record every answer with its source in the screen note.

Red flags:
- A teaser that gives an equity IRR but not a total project cost.
- A revenue contract "in final form" that is unsigned or unseen.
- The sponsor's traffic, resource or price forecast is the only one in the room.
- Reports in the data room are drafts with the reservations section missing.
- Recently uploaded folders named "supplemental" or "historical" (late disclosures).
- A model that does not reconcile to the IM's sources and uses.
- Guarantees from the same public balance sheet as the offtaker presented as separate credit.
- An intermediary whose role nobody can explain (cross-reference Section 87.5).

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- The annuity shortcut lands within a few percent of a sculpted answer when CFADS is flat; it overstates capacity when CFADS falls over the tenor and understates it when CFADS rises.
- If most of the equity value arrives after the contract expires, the deal is a merchant deal with a contracted head.
- A breakeven below 15% volume or price decline on a greenfield demand-risk asset means distributions are fragile; the 15% is the brief writer's illustrative screen trigger, labeled as a rule of thumb with its limit (it says nothing about debt default).
- Each rule stated with what breaks it.

Common mistakes:
- Screening the sponsor's case instead of the lender's case.
- Treating a sovereign guarantee as additional credit when the sovereign is the offtaker's owner and funder.
- Reading the model before the contracts.
- Spending the hour on a risk that is priced instead of the one that is fatal.
- Using information received outside the process.

Questions experts ask: drawn from Framework 85.2 plus seat-specific ones (the PPP official: "what does this cost the budget in a bad year?"; the contractor: "who certifies completion, and who pays us if the offtaker fails before then?").

## 85-12. Judgment drill

The situation (second person, specific): You are a director at an infrastructure debt fund. A developer offers you exclusivity, for a fee of USD 0.6 million payable by the fund, on a USD 285.0 million senior loan to a 410 MW, two-hour battery in Australia's National Electricity Market, contracted for 60% of capacity under a seven-year virtual toll with an unnamed retailer and merchant for the rest; the developer wants a signed exclusivity letter by Friday noon; your screen finds two unresolved items (the retailer's credit support is a parent letter of comfort, and the grid connection agreement's performance standards are not yet approved). (The writer keeps all terms illustrative and asserts no fact about NEM rules beyond what Chapter 11 teaches.)

Reasoning it through: the fee buys a time option; the two items are curable conditions, not fatal flaws, but one (connection approval) is outside both parties' control; the expert proceeds only with conditions written into the exclusivity letter (fee refundable or credited if the connection approval is not obtained by a date; credit support upgraded to a guarantee or LC sized to the toll payments for a defined period); compares the fee with the cost of the diligence it would trigger; and states what the screen note must say to the fund's IC.

What would change the answer: retailer investment-grade with a parent guarantee (proceed without condition on credit); connection standards approved (remove the date condition); merchant share above the fund's mandate limit (decline); a competing lender offering a lower all-in cost (shorten exclusivity rather than pay).

## 86-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. A credit paper is the document on which an institution commits its capital and against which it will judge its people when the deal goes wrong. This chapter teaches the reader to write one that a committee can approve quickly and for the right reasons: recommendation first, the deal in two pages, every material risk stated with its mitigant and the residual risk that remains, the numbers on the lender's cases rather than the sponsor's, conditions that bite, and a profitability case the bank can defend. It then teaches the equity counterpart, the investment committee memo, whose questions are value, returns, exit and portfolio fit; and how to present either to a committee and handle its questions. The centerpiece is Castellan Bank's complete credit paper for Bélanou in May 2018, followed by a reading of that paper with eight years of hindsight: which risks it weighted correctly, which it underweighted, and what a better paper would have said.

What you will be able to do:

- Write a credit paper or investment committee memo whose recommendation, risk analysis, numbers and conditions would get it approved for the right reasons (Capability 14).
- Build a risk–mitigant–residual table and a returns bridge from the sponsor's case to your institution's case, and present the sensitivities that matter in order of impact (Capabilities 14 and 4).
- Present a transaction to a committee and answer its questions with the number, the source and the consequence (Capabilities 14 and 15).
- Draft conditions of approval and monitoring triggers that a deal team can satisfy and a portfolio manager can test (Capabilities 14 and 10).

**Example 86.2. The sponsor's case and the bank's case (Illustrative)** (`ex:86.2`)
- Scenario: the 96.4 MW run-of-river hydro plant of Example 86.1; first full operating year.
- Inputs: sponsor case generation 412.6 GWh at a tariff of USD 64.80/MWh; operating cost USD 6.94 million; tax nil (holiday). Bank case adjustments: generation 3.2% lower (lenders' hydrologist uses the longer flow record); availability 97.0% against the sponsor's 98.5%, applied as a factor 0.970/0.985; operating cost 8.5% higher (IE view of staffing and insurance). Sizing at 1.35x on the bank case; debt 14 years at 6.45% all-in, level debt service for the illustration.
- Results: sponsor revenue USD 26.736 million, CFADS USD 19.796 million. Bank generation 393.3 GWh, revenue USD 25.487 million, operating cost USD 7.530 million, CFADS USD 17.957 million. Debt service at 1.35x on the bank case USD 13.301 million; the same debt service shows 1.49x (1.488) on the sponsor's case. Sized on the sponsor's case, debt service would be USD 14.664 million (1.363 more) and the bank case DSCR would be 1.23x (1.225). Debt at 6.45% over 14 years: USD 120.26 million on the bank case against USD 132.58 million on the sponsor's (USD 12.32 million difference).
- Exhibit 86.2a (case table): each adjustment, its source, and its effect on CFADS. Teaching point: the paper shows both cases and attributes each adjustment to a named report; a committee that sees only the bank case cannot judge how conservative it is.

**Example 86.3. Sensitivities in order of impact (Illustrative)** (`ex:86.3`)
- Same plant; debt service fixed at USD 13.301 million; base bank-case DSCR 1.35x.
- Sensitivities and results (DSCR, change from base): generation minus 10%: 1.16x (1.158; minus 0.19); operating cost plus 10%: 1.29x (1.293; minus 0.06); availability minus 2 points (95.0% against 97.0%): 1.31x (1.310; minus 0.04); interest plus 100 bps on the 20% unhedged share of USD 120.26 million debt (USD 0.241 million more interest): 1.33x (1.326; minus 0.02).
- Exhibit 86.2: tornado chart and table, ordered by impact. Teaching point: hydrology dominates; the paper spends its words on hydrology, and the rate sensitivity goes in an appendix.

**Example 86.4. The profitability box (Illustrative)** (`ex:86.4`)
- Inputs: final hold USD 60.0 million; weighted average life 6.8 years; margin 2.75%; upfront fee 1.75% amortized over the WAL (0.2574% a year); liquidity and funding premium 0.55%; expected loss PD 1.10% times LGD 30% = 0.33% a year; operating cost 0.18%; tax 25%; capital 12.0% of exposure at a 100% risk-weight density (the capital inputs are given as the bank's methodology output; Chapter 68 explains how they arise).
- Equation `eq:86.1`: $\mathrm{RORAC} = \frac{(m + f/\mathrm{WAL} - c_{\mathrm{fund}} - \mathrm{PD}\times\mathrm{LGD} - c_{\mathrm{op}})\times(1-\tau)}{k \times \rho}$, with $m$ margin, $f$ upfront fee, $c$ costs, $\tau$ tax rate, $k$ capital ratio, $\rho$ risk-weight density. Excel: `=(C4+C5/C6-C7-C8*C9-C10)*(1-C11)/(C12*C13)`.
- Results: pre-tax spread 1.9474%; post-tax 1.4605%; RORAC 12.2% (12.17%). Annual post-tax income on the hold USD 0.876 million against capital of USD 7.2 million. Downgrade case (PD 2.20%, risk-weight density 115%): pre-tax 1.6174%; RORAC 8.8% (8.79%).
- Teaching point: a deal that clears a 12% hurdle at origination falls well below it after one notch of credit deterioration; the paper should show both and say why the bank still wants the asset (relationship, ancillary hedging and agency income), with those cross-sell revenues stated separately and never netted into the base RORAC. Add the statement that the hurdle level is illustrative.

**Example 86.5. The returns bridge from sponsor case to IC case (Illustrative)** (`ex:86.5`)
- Scenario: a fund buys into a 30-year contracted solar plant; equity outlay USD 38.42 million at financial close; year-1 CFADS USD 13.85 million growing 1.0% a year; level senior debt service USD 9.42 million for 18 years.
- Sponsor case equity IRR 15.2% (15.23%); year-1 cash yield 11.5%.
- IC adjustments, cumulative: (1) degradation view lowers CFADS growth from 1.0% to 0.6%: 14.5% (14.46%); (2) operating cost plus USD 0.205 million a year escalating at 2.0%: 14.0% (13.98%); (3) useful life 25 years instead of 30: 13.3% (13.31%); (4) a 3% capex overrun funded by equity, USD 1.56 million more equity: 12.8% (12.84%). NPV at a 10% hurdle: USD 26.32 million (sponsor case) and USD 12.14 million (IC case).
- Exhibit 86.5: the bridge as a bar chart and table. Teaching point: the bridge is the IC memo's most useful page because it turns the argument with the sponsor into four named, sourced assumptions; the largest single step is the useful-life assumption, which no model audit would flag.

## 86-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist:
- State the request with amount, facility, role, tenor and hold in the first sentence.
- Show the sponsor's case and the institution's case side by side with each adjustment sourced.
- Order sensitivities by impact and explain the top two in words.
- State every residual risk as a number or a scenario, and say who bears it.
- Table every available forecast for demand-risk assets.
- Run the pre-mortem before submission.
- Show profitability on the hold without cross-sell, and cross-sell separately.
- Draft conditions that name a document, a deadline and who confirms satisfaction.
- Check every number in the paper against the model output file and the term sheet.

Red flags:
- Recommendation at the end.
- Residuals all "low" or "mitigated".
- A financial section that shows only the sponsor's case.
- A sensitivity set that omits the deal's deciding variable.
- Conditions such as "satisfactory documentation" with no named test.
- Profitability that depends on ancillary business not yet mandated.
- The paper's numbers differ from the model's by rounding drift across versions.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- If the committee can understand the deal from the first two pages, the paper is long enough; the attachments carry the rest. Limit: complex intercreditor or sovereign support structures need a diagram on page two.
- One sensitivity usually explains most of the downside; find it and write about it. Limit: correlated risks (currency and offtaker liquidity) must be run together.
- Profitability one notch after a downgrade tells you whether the bank wants the asset or the fee. Limit: illustrative hurdle rates; each bank sets its own.

Common mistakes:
- Copying the sponsor's information memorandum into the paper.
- Arguing with the committee instead of answering.
- Defending a number someone else produced without checking it.
- Promising a sponsor terms before approval (Pieter's 2036 maturity, Case Bible 4.1).
- Treating the paper as finished at approval rather than the baseline for every later review.

Questions experts ask (committee members): what is the one thing that kills this loan; what do we lose and when if it happens; who else has looked at this and declined; what did the IE refuse to sign; where does our case sit against every other forecast; what do we do on day one of a payment default; if we could not sell down, would we still approve the full underwrite?

## 86-12. Judgment drill

The situation: You are the second-line credit officer reviewing a deal team's paper for a USD 212.0 million underwriting of a 300 MWac solar plant with a 15-year PPA (illustrative; unnamed country with a BB-rated sovereign and a state-owned offtaker). The team asks for approval within 48 hours because the sponsor has a competing bank's offer. The IE's report is in draft with one open reservation on module supply; the sizing is at 1.25x on P50; the bank's hold after sell-down is USD 40.0 million; the RORAC on the hold is 11.6% against a 12% hurdle, rising to 14.1% "including expected hedging income".

Reasoning it through: separate what is approvable now from what is conditional; the draft IE reservation is a condition, not a reason to decline; the RORAC presentation is wrong (hedging income is not yet mandated and must be shown separately), so the true return is below hurdle; the 48-hour deadline is a sponsor tactic and a bank that approves under a deadline it did not set takes the risk it has not read; the expert approves the underwriting only with conditions (final IE report without new reservations; underwrite reduced or priced to clear the hurdle on the hold alone; sell-down plan with a backstop), or recommends a counter-offer on price and hold.

What would change the answer: the IE reservation closed (removes a condition); sizing on P90 at 1.20x rather than P50 at 1.25x showing more headroom (strengthens approval); sovereign downgraded or offtaker payment arrears (moves toward decline or a smaller hold); the bank's strategy explicitly values the client relationship at a stated return (allows below-hurdle approval, recorded as such).
