## 2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. A sponsor who can raise USD 600 million for a gas plant in a B-rated country is not choosing "debt"; it is choosing among six different businesses that happen to lend money, each with its own funding, regulator, mandate, tenor appetite, currency, documentation habits and behavior in a crisis. This chapter teaches the reader how each lender type makes money, what rulebook bounds it, what it will and will not do, and how a lead arranger assembles several of them into one lender group. It owns the commercial bank's business model (capital-rule detail is Chapter 68), the export credit agency (OECD Arrangement, cover types, content rules, premia, repayment-profile limits), the development finance institution (mandates, additionality, preferred creditor status, A/B and parallel loans, the existence of DFI guarantees), public development lenders, institutional investors as lenders, and infrastructure debt funds and private credit. Bonds as an instrument belong to Chapter 30; the guarantee instruments to Chapter 34.

What you will be able to do (the chapter's `\section*` list; two to four items):

- Explain how a commercial bank, an ECA, a DFI, an institutional investor and a private credit fund each earn their return on a project loan, and predict how each will behave when the project gets into trouble (Capabilities 1 and 5).
- Build an ECA-covered tranche from an export contract under the OECD Arrangement, including content limits, local costs, the premium and the repayment-profile tests (Capabilities 5 and 8).
- Structure an A/B loan or parallel loan with a DFI and explain what preferred creditor status does and does not protect (Capability 8).
- Compare competing lender offers on cost, tenor, refinancing risk and flexibility, and recommend a lender group for a given project (Capabilities 5 and 15).

**Example 29.1 A bank's return on a project loan (Illustrative)** (`ex:29.1`). Scenario: in February 2026 the New York branch of Veldhaven Bank NV (a fictional Dutch bank) is offered a USD 62.5 million share of a 15-year senior term loan to Red Shale Wind Holdings LLC (fictional), owner of three operating wind farms in Oklahoma and Kansas with 15-year PPAs with two investment-grade utilities. The ticket is a round number because arrangers allocate round tickets; the text says so. Inputs: margin 1.65% a year; upfront fee 1.40% of the commitment, spread over an expected average life of 8.3 years; liquidity premium on the bank's funding 0.42% a year over the reference rate; operating cost 0.18% a year of the balance; probability of default 0.65% a year and loss given default 30.0% (each given one sentence of plain meaning and a forward reference to ssec:38.1.1), so expected loss 0.195% a year (ssec:15.3.1); allocated capital 9.1% of the loan (given; Chapter 68 explains its source); return earned on the capital itself at a reference rate of 3.10%; tax 25%; hurdle return on allocated capital 11.0%. Computation (Equation 29.1):
RAROC = [(margin + fee/average life − funding premium − operating cost − expected loss) × (1 − tax) + capital × reference rate × (1 − tax)] / capital.
Key results: revenue 1.8187% a year (1.65% + 0.1687%); pre-tax risk-adjusted income 1.0237%; after tax 0.7678%; capital benefit 0.2116% (9.1% × 3.10% × 0.75); RAROC 10.76%, below the 11.0% hurdle. Adding hedging income of 0.07% a year (the bank's swap execution margin, of the same kind as Case P's 7.5 bps credit and execution charge) lifts RAROC to 11.34%. The margin needed to reach 11.0% without ancillary income is 1.68% (1.6789%). On USD 62.5 million: annual revenue USD 1.14 million, after-tax risk-adjusted income USD 0.48 million against allocated capital of USD 5.69 million. Excel: the RAROC formula contains percentages, so it is specified as an `excel` block with the inputs in cells (margin F5, fee F6, average life F7, funding premium F8, operating cost F9, PD F10, LGD F11, capital F12, reference rate F13, tax F14): `=((F5+F6/F7-F8-F9-F10*F11)*(1-F14)+F12*F13*(1-F14))/F12`. Lesson: banks bid for ancillary business (swaps, accounts, agency) because the loan alone often misses the hurdle, which is why Pieter's insistence that Castellan price the swaps (Case Bible 4.1) is economics, not greed alone.

**Example 29.3 Testing a sculpted repayment profile against Arrangement limits (Illustrative)** (`ex:29.3`). Scenario: the Example 29.2 loan repays over 12 years in 24 semiannual installments, the first six months after the starting point of credit, each installment 3.1% larger than the one before (a sculpted, back-weighted profile). Computation: installments run from 2.87% to 5.79% of principal; the largest six-month repayment is 5.79%, far below 30%; first repayment at six months, within 24 months; weighted average life of the repayment period, computed with eq:6.2 (cited, not re-derived; t in years from the starting point of credit) = 6.97 years (6.975) against the Equation 29.3 limit of the longer of 65% × 12 = 7.80 years and 6 years, so the profile passes. ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 29.1 Lender-fit map** (`fw:lender-fit-map`), original. Purpose: decide which lender types belong in a given financing before approaching any of them. Steps: (1) write the project's financing needs as six lines: amount, currency of revenue, tenor the cash flow needs, construction risk retained, country risk, need for post-close flexibility; (2) for each lender type (commercial bank, local bank, ECA, DFI, public development lender, institutional investor, debt fund) mark each line as fits, fits with a condition, or does not fit, using Exhibit 29.1; (3) strike any lender type with a "does not fit" on currency or country risk; (4) rank the survivors by cost and time to commit; (5) check concentration: no lender type should hold a veto the sponsor cannot live with (ECA consent, DFI policy covenants); (6) write the target lender group as tranches with indicative shares and the conditions each lender type will impose. Demonstrated on Example 29.5 and then on Case P in Section 29.11. When to use: at the financing-strategy stage, before mandating an arranger (Chapter 55 takes over from there).

**Framework 29.2 ECA cover build** (`fw:eca-cover-build`), original procedure applying the OECD Arrangement. Steps: (1) list export contracts by country of origin; (2) apply each ECA's national content rule; (3) take 85% of eligible export contract value; (4) add supportable local costs up to the cap and check notification thresholds; (5) choose pure cover or direct lending and the cover percentage; (6) add the premium and decide whether it is financed (Equation 29.2); (7) test tenor and repayment profile (Equation 29.3); (8) list the conditions (E&S category, integrity, content certificates). Demonstrated in Example 29.2 and the walkthrough. Credit the Arrangement as the source of the limits and say the procedure is the book's.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist (lender-group design): currency of each tranche matches revenue or is hedged; tenor of each tranche checked against the ECA and bank limits; country and single-obligor limits of each bank cleared before mandate; ECA content certificates obtainable from the EPC contractor; DFI E&S category and action plan timeline mapped onto the financial-close timetable; B-loan participants eligible (not resident in the host country; rated); intercreditor voting map drafted before term sheets are final; hedge providers drawn from the lender group.
- Red flags: an ECA tranche sized to the cap with no room for overruns; a B loan whose participants are also the commercial banks and gain nothing; a DFI asked to join late, after the term sheet is set (its policies reopen it); private credit pricing quoted without a call-protection schedule; a local bank asked for a tenor beyond its regulatory limit; an ECA starting point of credit tied to a date the project cannot control.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: ECA cover lowers the price of covered debt but adds premium and time (often several months of diligence); applies to deals where export content is large. DFI participation reduces political-risk loss but slows close; applies most in frontier countries. Banks match tenor to their funding, so long tenor costs more or comes as a mini-perm; limit: in 2025 to 2026 some bank markets offered long amortizing tenors for contracted renewables (dated, FS: t-market-norms).
- Common mistakes: treating ECA cover as cover for all lenders; forgetting that the uncovered 5% sits with the bank; assuming preferred creditor status protects against project failure; comparing lender offers on margin rather than on all-in cost and balloon; ignoring the voting power that a large ECA tranche or B-loan bloc acquires.
- Questions experts ask: Which lender here has a mandate that will make it behave differently from the others in a crisis? What is each lender's hold level and sell-down plan? Which tranche sets the tenor, and which sets the covenants? What does the ECA need from the EPC contractor, and has the contractor agreed to provide it? Where will the local-currency need be met?

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the lead arranger for a 238 MW wind farm in a B-rated, lower-middle-income country, with a 20-year USD-indexed PPA with a state utility. The sponsor needs USD 310.0 million of senior debt and wants financial close in seven months. Three routes are on the table: (a) an ECA-covered tranche of USD 150.0 million tied to the turbine supplier's home country plus USD 160.0 million of uncovered commercial debt with private PRI; (b) a DFI A loan of USD 110.0 million and a B loan of USD 90.0 million plus USD 110.0 million of commercial debt; (c) a single DFI-led parallel-loan group of three DFIs for the full amount at a higher all-in cost. The sponsor asks for your recommendation by Friday.

Reasoning to outline: tenor first (wind is CCSU-eligible, so the ECA tranche can reach long tenors with sculpting under the 35%/36-month/70% limits; banks alone will not go long in this country); country risk second (PCS through the B loan protects against transfer restrictions, PRI covers named political perils but is a claim, not priority access); time third (DFI E&S diligence and board approvals compete with the seven-month target; the ECA's own E&S review runs in parallel); cost fourth (route c is dearest but simplest to document); concentration and voting fifth (route a gives the ECA consent rights; route b gives the DFI policy covenants and B-participant voting). Expert answer: a combination: ECA-covered tranche for the turbine export content plus a DFI A/B loan for the balance, with the uncovered commercial piece minimized; documented under one common terms agreement; with an explicit plan to run ECA and DFI E&S diligence on one action plan.

What would change the answer: a sovereign upgrade to BB (commercial banks with PRI become competitive and route a without the DFI wins on time); turbines from a country whose ECA lacks appetite for the host country (drop the ECA); a requirement for local-currency debt (adds a DFI local-currency tranche or local banks; Chapter 34); a seven-month deadline that is political, not contractual (route c's simplicity gains weight).

## 2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. A bond turns project debt into a security that thousands of investors can own, rate and trade. That changes who lends, at what rate, for how long, and how hard it is to change anything after close. This chapter teaches what a project bond is and how it differs from a loan; the markets in which project bonds are sold (Rule 144A and Regulation S, US private placements, domestic and local-currency markets, labeled bonds, and sukuk at overview level); project-bond conventions and arithmetic (bond-equivalent yield, accrued interest, issue discount, amortizing bonds and sinking funds, negative carry, make-whole), building on the bond basics of Chapter 6; the bond-or-loan comparison; and how rating agencies analyze projects in construction and operation, taught through the actual published frameworks (S&P's two-phase stand-alone credit profile method, Fitch's master criteria architecture, and Moody's scorecard in its labeled 2022 version), including the sovereign ceiling and the structures that pierce it. Green and sustainability standards are taught in Chapter 84 and sukuk structures in Chapter 33; this chapter covers only what they change in a bond deal.

What you will be able to do:

- Decide whether a project should be financed or refinanced with bonds, loans or both, and explain the decision to a sponsor's board (Capabilities 5 and 1).
- Price an amortizing project bond on a bond-equivalent basis, compute its settlement price, average life and make-whole, and size the negative carry of a pre-funded bond (Capability 5).
- Run a rating-style analysis of a project the way the agencies do: map a minimum DSCR and a business assessment to a preliminary outcome, test resiliency in a downside, find the breakeven stresses, and explain where the sovereign or offtaker cap binds (Capabilities 5 and 8).
- Read a project bond offering memorandum and an indenture's key covenants and say what a bondholder can and cannot do when the project needs a waiver (Capability 6).

**Example 30.2 The cost of negative carry on a pre-funded bond (Illustrative)** (`ex:30.2`). Scenario: in 2026 Brazos Bend Power LLC (fictional) finances a 640 MW combined-cycle plant in Texas with a 36-month construction period and USD 476.3 million of uses before financing costs, spent on a sine-shaped monthly S-curve (month m share proportional to sin(π(m − 0.5)/36)); option A is a bond at 5.375% issued in full at financial close with proceeds held in escrow earning 3.10%, coupons during construction paid from the escrow; option B is a bank facility at the same 5.375% all-in drawn as needed with interest capitalized (fees ignored to isolate carry). Computation (monthly accrual): the bond must be USD 536.7 million (536.74) to fund the uses plus its own construction coupons (coupons USD 86.6 million less escrow earnings USD 26.1 million); the drawn-as-needed facility totals USD 515.4 million (515.36) including capitalized interest. The negative carry costs USD 21.4 million (21.37) of additional debt, about 4.5% of the uses. The general carrying cost of a funded reserve is eq:16.1 (ssec:16.5.2); the writer cites it and shows the bond-specific case. The circularity (bond size depends on coupons that depend on bond size) is linear in the bond size, so the writer solves it in closed form: every coupon is proportional to B and the escrow earns a fixed rate, so the escrow balance at the end of construction is linear in B (E(B) = a × B − b, with a and b computed from the spend profile in two helper rows), and setting E(B) = 0 gives B = b / a with no circular reference; the writer notes that a workbook could instead use iterative calculation with a circuit breaker (Chapter 13 teaches both; Chapter 40 fixes the house method). **Example 30.3 A make-whole on a private placement prepayment (Illustrative)** (`ex:30.3`). Scenario: in March 2022 Lone Mesa Wind LLC (fictional), owner of a contracted 280 MW wind farm in West Texas, issued USD 153.6 million of 12-year 5.94% senior notes to five US insurers, semiannual, equal principal installments of USD 6.40 million; in March 2026, after eight installments, USD 102.4 million is outstanding and the issuer prepays to refinance. Make-whole: present value of the 16 remaining payments discounted at the comparable Treasury yield of 4.12% plus 50 bps (4.62%, semiannual; the 50 bps is this note's own term, not a market norm) = USD 107.43 million, so the make-whole premium is USD 5.03 million (4.9% of the amount prepaid). **Example 30.4 Rating a contracted plant in operation (Illustrative)** (`ex:30.4`). Scenario: in 2026 an analyst rates the senior secured notes of Gila Bend Peaking LLC (fictional), a 340 MW gas-fired peaking plant in Arizona with a 20-year tolling agreement with a fictional investment-grade utility. Capacity revenue at 100% availability is USD 118.4 million a year, the net energy margin is USD 6.3 million at a base availability of 92% and scales with availability, fixed operating costs are USD 31.7 million and annual debt service is USD 55.9 million. Step 1 (base case): cash available for debt service USD 83.5 million (83.528), ratio 1.49x (the intuitive DSCR from Chapter 1; Chapter 35 is the home of the definition). Step 2 (preliminary operations SACP from S&P's published table, Exhibit 30.5): assume the analyst assesses an OPBA of 5 (an assumption stated for the example: availability-based revenue, low market exposure, a simple-cycle technology with good operating history); at OPBA 5 to 6 a minimum DSCR of at least 1.30x and below 1.75x maps to the 'bbb' category, so 1.49x indicates 'bbb' (the plus or minus modifier depends on position in the range and committee judgment, so the example stops at the category). Step 3 (resiliency): a downside of 85% availability and fixed costs 10% higher gives cash available of USD 71.6 million and a ratio of 1.28x, which maps to the 'bb' category at OPBA 5 and stays above 1.00x; the writer explains that downside ratios staying above 1.0x and mapping to at least 'bb' in most periods is S&P's "high" resiliency, worth one notch up from a 'bbb' base under S&P's table, and that the liquidity, debt-structure and refinancing modifiers then apply (named, not scored). Step 4 (breakevens, the ladder of Framework 30.2): breakeven availability (ratio 1.00x) = (55.9 + 31.7) / (118.4 + 6.3/0.92) = 69.9%; with fixed costs 15% higher the breakeven is 73.7% and the base ratio falls to 1.41x. Step 5 (caps): the result cannot rise above the offtaker's credit for a fully contracted plant, which the writer states as the binding constraint to test. ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 30.1 Bond-or-loan test** (`fw:bond-or-loan-test`), original. Steps: (1) size: is the debt large enough to justify bond execution costs and index eligibility; (2) construction: how much completion risk remains for lenders, and can it be wrapped by completion support or a bank construction tranche; (3) tenor and rate: does the cash flow need a fixed-rate tenor beyond what banks offer; (4) rating: can the project achieve the rating the target investors need, given sovereign and offtaker ceilings; (5) flexibility: how many waivers, amendments or additional debt incurrences are likely in the first five years; (6) prepayment: is a refinancing or sale likely before maturity, and what will call protection cost; (7) disclosure and timetable: can the sponsor live with an offering memorandum and market windows. Score each line bond-favorable, neutral or loan-favorable and write the decision with the two lines that decided it. Demonstrated in 30.4.4 and Section 30.8.

**Framework 30.2 Rating-case stress ladder** (`fw:rating-stress-ladder`), original, modeled on how analysts reason. Steps: (1) restate the sponsor base case; (2) build a rating case with conservative but plausible assumptions for each revenue and cost driver; (3) run single-variable breakevens to a 1.00x ratio; (4) compare each breakeven with the historical worst for the technology, market or counterparty; (5) run combined stresses; (6) apply caps (sovereign, offtaker, construction) and uplifts (guarantees, PCS, offshore accounts); (7) state the rating outcome with the binding constraint. Demonstrated on Example 30.4 and Comber Wind.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist (bond readiness): rating feedback obtained on the target structure; escrow or delayed-draw mechanics chosen and negative carry funded in the sources and uses; call protection schedule agreed and priced into the refinancing plan; consent thresholds and the trustee's discretion mapped against expected waivers; reporting covenants matched to what the project company can produce on time; investor eligibility (144A, Reg S, NAIC, ERISA or local regulations) confirmed; labeled-bond framework and external review lined up if a label is used; intercreditor position of bondholders against ECA, DFI and hedge creditors settled.
- Red flags: a construction-phase bond with weak contractor security; a bond maturity beyond the revenue contract; a make-whole that blocks the refinancing the sponsor's plan assumes; bond covenants copied from a bank loan that will need monthly waivers; a rating that depends entirely on a guarantee from a counterparty whose own rating is under pressure.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: bonds suit large, long, stable, rated cash flows after construction; limit: with completion support and liquidity, construction-phase bonds have closed (Case T's bonds funded at close; Pacífico 3). Negative carry costs scale with the gap between coupon and reinvestment rate and with construction length. A make-whole is cheap when rates rise and dear when they fall.
- Common mistakes: comparing a bond coupon with a loan margin instead of all-in cost (Chapter 38); forgetting negative carry in the sources and uses; assuming bondholders will respond to a waiver request in a week; treating a rating as a forecast of loss rather than an opinion.
- Questions experts ask: What rating can this achieve without a guarantee, and what caps it? How many consents will the project need in the next five years? What does the call protection cost in the sponsor's own refinancing case? Who holds the bonds, and do they have a single decision-maker?

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the financial advisor to the sponsor of an operating 300 MWac contracted solar portfolio in the US, two years after COD. Its USD 330.0 million bank term loan, a mini-perm maturing in five years, carries SOFR plus 1.875%, 85% swapped. A placement agent offers a 17-year USPP at an indicative 6.05% fixed, rated BBB- by one agency, with a make-whole at Treasuries plus 50 bps. The sponsor's investment committee meets in ten days and expects to sell the portfolio within six years. Recommend.

Reasoning to outline: compare all-in costs (the swap rate plus margin against the fixed coupon, plus fees and swap unwind); remove the refinancing risk of the mini-perm against adding make-whole cost if the portfolio is sold (a buyer may assume the notes, or the sponsor pays the make-whole: run Example 30.3's logic at plausible rates); flexibility needs (repowering or augmentation may need consents); rating stability. Expert answer: if the sale plan is firm, keep the bank loan and negotiate an extension or portability; if the hold is likely beyond the mini-perm, take the USPP but negotiate assignability to a buyer and a par call in the last years.

What would change the answer: falling rates expected (make-whole cost rises, favoring a shorter or callable structure); a buyer universe of insurers who prefer to inherit long fixed-rate debt (USPP gains); a planned repowering (loan flexibility wins); a downgrade risk on the offtaker (rating fragility argues for bank debt with negotiated waivers).

## 2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. The senior loan or bond is rarely the only debt in a project. Between it and the sponsors' equity sit mezzanine loans and holding-company debt that raise returns and take more risk; beside it sit ancillary facilities that solve timing problems the senior debt cannot: bridging equity to COD, funding VAT until refunds arrive, carrying receivables, posting letters of credit, and standing by for overruns. This chapter teaches each instrument: what problem it solves, who provides it, how it ranks and is secured, how it is priced, what it can enforce, and what goes wrong. Holdco leverage executed in refinancings and sales is Chapter 63; intercreditor mechanics in general are Chapter 53; DSRA letter-of-credit substitution rules are Chapter 37.

What you will be able to do:

- Explain the difference between contractual and structural subordination and trace a bad year through an opco and holdco cascade to see who gets paid (Capability 5).
- Price a mezzanine loan with cash interest, PIK and warrants, and judge whether it is cheaper than the equity it replaces (Capabilities 5 and 12).
- Size and structure the ancillary facilities a project needs: equity bridge, VAT, working capital, letters of credit, standby and cost-overrun facilities (Capabilities 5 and 10).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 31.1 A mezzanine lender's return (Illustrative)** (`ex:31.1`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional London mezzanine fund lends USD 43.8 million to Douro Solar Holdings SA (fictional), a contracted 290 MW solar portfolio in Portugal with USD-indexed corporate PPAs (USD terms are an Illustrative simplification), 8-year bullet, cash interest 9.50% a year on the accreted balance, PIK interest 3.00% a year added to principal, upfront fee 2.00%, and warrants worth USD 4.5 million (estimated) at maturity. Annual periods. Computations: accreted balance at maturity USD 55.49 million (43.8 × 1.03^8); cash interest rising from USD 4.16 million in year 1 to USD 4.97 million in year 7 and USD 5.12 million in year 8; lender cash flows −42.92, 4.161, 4.286, 4.414, 4.547, 4.683, 4.824, 4.968, 65.102; IRR 13.61% with warrants, 12.88% without. Equation 31.1: B_t = B_{t−1} × (1 + PIK). Discussion: compare with a sponsor's 14% to 16% equity hurdle (Illustrative) and the dilution of upside the warrants represent.

**Example 31.2 Following one bad year through an opco and a holdco (Illustrative)** (`ex:31.2`). Scenario: in 2026 Cimarron Plains Renewables OpCo LLC (fictional), a contracted wind and solar portfolio in Texas and Oklahoma, has senior debt service of USD 62.7 million a year and a distribution lock-up when cash available for debt service divided by debt service falls below 1.20x (lock-up mechanics are defined in sec:37.4; the ratio in sec:35.2); its parent, Cimarron Plains HoldCo LLC, owes USD 12.4 million a year on a holdco loan from a fictional private credit fund, secured by the opco shares. Base year: cash available USD 84.6 million, ratio 1.35x (1.349), distribution USD 21.9 million, holdco cover 1.77x (21.9/12.4). Downside year: cash available USD 73.1 million (13.6% lower), ratio 1.17x (1.166), below lock-up, so distributions are zero and the holdco has no cash at all; a 6-month holdco reserve of USD 6.2 million pays half a year. The lock-up bites at cash available of USD 75.24 million, an 11.1% fall from base. Lesson: a 13.6% fall in opco cash cuts holdco cash by 100%. Exhibit 31.2 tabulates both years.

**Example 31.3 An equity bridge loan and the sponsors' IRR (Illustrative)** (`ex:31.3`). Scenario: in 2026 the sponsors of a 30-month, 410 MW combined-cycle project in Jalisco, Mexico (fictional sponsors and project company; USD-denominated PPA) must provide USD 118.8 million of equity, USD 3.96 million a month pro rata with debt; COD at month 30; distributions of USD 16.6 million a year for 20 years, the first 12 months after COD. Option 1: sponsors fund each month. Option 2: a bank EBL at 5.85% a year funds the equity draws, and the sponsors repay principal and accrued interest at COD. Computations (monthly compounding of the annual rate): option 1 equity IRR 10.71%; option 2 sponsors pay USD 127.36 million at COD (interest USD 8.56 million) and earn 11.58%. The EBL rate at which the IRR gain disappears equals the option 1 IRR, 10.71%. Lesson: the EBL raises IRR by 0.86 percentage points because the sponsors' cost of capital exceeds the EBL rate; the lenders treat it as sponsor credit and the senior lenders require the bridge to be repaid from committed equity, not project cash (forward reference Chapter 40 for modeling the bridge).

**Example 31.4 Sizing a VAT facility (Illustrative)** (`ex:31.4`). Scenario: a 2026 wind farm in Kenya's Rift Valley (fictional project company; the 16% VAT rate and three-quarter refund lag are assumptions for the example, not statements of Kenyan law) pays VAT on local construction spend and receives refunds three quarters after payment; local spend over nine quarters of USD 19.4, 38.8, 58.8, 73.8, 77.5, 65.6, 47.5, 26.3 and 11.3 million (total USD 419.0 million, shown in USD equivalent of local currency); a local-currency VAT facility from a fictional Nairobi bank at 14.25% a year with interest paid quarterly by the project company as a construction cost. Computations: VAT paid USD 67.04 million in total; quarterly VAT 3.10, 6.21, 9.41, 11.81, 12.40, 10.50, 7.60, 4.21, 1.81; facility balance by quarter 3.10, 9.31, 18.72, 27.42, 33.62, 34.70 (peak), 30.50, 22.30, 13.62, 6.02, 1.81, 0.00; total interest USD 7.16 million (interest on the opening balance each quarter). Sensitivity for the exercises: a six-quarter refund lag (peak USD 57.92 million; interest USD 14.33 million). Lesson: the facility is sized to the peak, its interest belongs in the construction budget, and a refund delay converts it into term debt; the cost of the lag as a tax matter is ssec:67.7.2.

**Example 31.5 Letters of credit or cash (Illustrative)** (`ex:31.5`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional 180 MW battery storage project in California must post USD 24.6 million of security (a debt service reserve requirement that the lenders allow to be met by an LC, plus a hedge counterparty's credit support), either as cash funded at close or as an LC from a bank at a fee of 1.85% a year. Computations: cash funded from equity costs (13.5% − 3.5% deposit rate) × 24.6 = USD 2.46 million a year in foregone return; cash funded from senior debt at 6.40% costs (6.40% − 3.50%) × 24.6 = USD 0.71 million; the LC costs USD 0.46 million a year, but its reimbursement obligation is a claim that must rank somewhere (senior, pari passu with senior debt, or sponsor-guaranteed). Lesson: the LC is cheap because someone else carries the reimbursement risk; the question is who.

**Example 31.6 Funding an overrun through the standby layers (Illustrative)** (`ex:31.6`). Scenario: in 2027 a fictional 260 MW hydro project in Peru faces a hard cost overrun of USD 61.8 million; contingency USD 38.0 million; delay LDs received USD 9.2 million; standby facility USD 31.5 million and contingent equity USD 10.5 million drawn 75:25. Computations: remainder after contingency and LDs USD 14.6 million, funded USD 10.95 million standby and USD 3.65 million contingent equity. Second case: overrun USD 92.4 million leaves USD 45.2 million after contingency and LDs; the standby (capped at 31.5) and contingent equity (capped at 10.5) together fund USD 42.0 million and leave a USD 3.2 million funding gap, which the sponsors must cover or the lenders must waive. ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 31.1 Subordination stack test** (`fw:subordination-stack`), original. For any facility that is not plain senior debt, answer seven questions in order: (1) Whose debt is it: which legal entity borrows? (2) What cash pays it, and which lock-ups, blockages or sweeps stand between that cash and the lender? (3) What security does it hold, and over which entity's assets or shares? (4) What can it enforce, and after what standstill? (5) What does it rank behind, including trade creditors and hedges? (6) What happens to it in the senior lenders' downside case? (7) What does it cost, all in, compared with the equity it replaces? Write a two-line verdict: who bears the first loss in a bad year, and whether the sponsor is buying cheap capital or expensive leverage. Demonstrated on Azura-Edo's mezzanine (sec:31.9) and Case R's TLB (ssec:31.11.1).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist (junior and ancillary facilities): for each facility, the borrowing entity, source of repayment, ranking, security, enforcement standstill, cross-default reach and cost (Framework 31.1); holdco distribution cover tested on the opco lenders' downside and lock-up, not only on the base case; EBL repayment funded by committed, LC-backed equity; VAT facility sized to peak outstanding with a refund-delay sensitivity; LC reimbursement claims ranked in the intercreditor agreement; standby and contingent equity caps tested against the independent engineer's overrun scenarios.
- Red flags: holdco debt sized on base-case distributions with no reserve; mezzanine whose standstill lets it accelerate before senior lenders can act; a PIK toggle that hides cash stress; an EBL guaranteed by a sponsor whose credit is weaker than the project's; a VAT refund regime with a history of arrears; LCs issued by a bank outside the lender group with an unranked reimbursement claim.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: holdco lenders should model the opco's lock-up as their default trigger; limit: a holdco reserve or sponsor support can bridge a single bad year. Mezzanine is cheaper than equity only if its all-in IRR, including warrants, is below the sponsors' required return; limit: warrants are hard to value. Ancillary facilities are cheap because they are short, so their real risk is extension.
- Common mistakes: counting holdco debt as non-recourse to the project when the holdco's only asset is the project; ignoring hedge counterparties in the stack; forgetting the interest on a VAT facility in the budget; treating a standby facility as contingency.
- Questions experts ask: Which covenant at opco cuts off this lender's cash, and how far is it from today's ratio? Who pays if the VAT refunds stop? What happens to the EBL if one sponsor defaults on its equity commitment? Who reimburses the LC bank if the LC is drawn?

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are head of a private credit fund's infrastructure team. A sponsor asks for a USD 140.0 million, 7-year holdco loan to finance 45% of its equity in a newly operating 520 MW contracted and partly merchant solar-plus-storage portfolio, priced at SOFR plus 4.50% with a 1% OID. Opco senior debt is a 7-year mini-perm with a 1.20x lock-up and a 50% cash sweep from year 5. Base-case distributions cover the holdco debt service 1.82x; in the opco lenders' downside, opco ratio falls to 1.16x in two of the first five years. Your IC meets on Thursday.

Reasoning to outline: Framework 31.1; the lock-up is the real default trigger, so the 1.82x base cover is not the risk measure; the year-5 sweep coincides with the holdco amortization profile; size the holdco reserve to the two downside years; consider a PIK toggle with a cap; require a sponsor equity cure right and limits on opco amendments; price for the structural subordination and the refinancing risk of two stacked mini-perms. Expert answer: lend, but smaller (sized on downside distributions with a reserve), with a 12-month holdco DSRA, a sweep that steps up when opco cover falls, and a maturity before the opco mini-perm.

What would change the answer: opco revenues fully contracted with a strong offtaker (downside improves; larger loan); opco lenders refusing any information covenant to the holdco lender (decline); sponsor rated investment grade and willing to guarantee (it becomes corporate credit, priced lower).

**Example 32.1 Share capital or shareholder loan (Illustrative)** (`ex:32.1`). Scenario: in 2026 two fictional sponsors, a Spanish developer and a Singapore infrastructure fund, provide USD 96.4 million of equity to a fictional project company owning a contracted 220 MW solar plant in Vietnam; the tax rates are assumptions for the example, not Vietnamese law: 28% corporate tax, 10% withholding on interest and 10% on dividends at domestic rates; in an operating year the project company has USD 17.8 million of pre-tax cash after senior debt service, assumed equal to taxable profit before shareholder-loan interest (simplification stated). The withholding and deductibility mechanics are those of ssec:7.6.4, cited. Case A: all share capital. Tax USD 4.98 million; dividend USD 12.82 million; net to sponsors after withholding USD 11.53 million. Case B: 20% share capital (USD 19.28 million) and 80% shareholder loan (USD 77.12 million) at 10.0% (interest USD 7.71 million). Taxable profit USD 10.09 million; tax USD 2.82 million; dividend USD 7.26 million; net to sponsors USD 13.48 million, a gain of USD 1.94 million a year. With Illustrative treaty rates of 5% on interest and 7.5% on dividends (not any real treaty's rates; treaty structuring is Chapter 67): case A USD 11.86 million, case B USD 14.05 million, gain USD 2.19 million. Writer adds two limits, each one sentence citing ssec:7.6.4: thin capitalization or earnings-based interest limitation can disallow the deduction (the Case P rule of three times equity is annex P 4.15 and is modeled in Chapter 41), and accounting losses can block dividends while shareholder-loan interest still flows (ssec:42.5.2).

**Example 32.2 When the equity goes in (Illustrative)** (`ex:32.2`). Scenario: in 2026 the sponsors of a USD 386.4 million, 30-month toll bridge project in Ontario, Canada (fictional sponsors and project company; USD equivalent for consistency) at 75:25 debt to equity need USD 96.6 million of equity; distributions of USD 14.1 million a year for 20 years, the first 12 months after COD. Three timings (monthly model, IRR annualized; LC fees accrue monthly on the undrawn balance at the start of each month): upfront at month 1: equity IRR 9.86%; pro rata at USD 3.22 million a month with an equity LC on the undrawn balance at 0.95% a year: IRR 11.11%, LC fees USD 1.19 million; back-ended at month 30 with an LC on the full USD 96.6 million at 0.95% a year: IRR 12.96%, LC fees USD 2.29 million. Exhibit 32.2 sets the three IRRs against the lenders' exposure (with back-ended equity, lenders fund 100% of costs until COD and rely on the LC). Lesson: back-ending raises IRR by about 3.1 percentage points against upfront, and lenders accept it only against LCs from banks they rate, which is why Case P's sponsors chose pro rata with LCs.

**Example 32.3 Pricing a development premium in a farm-down at financial close (Illustrative)** (`ex:32.3`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional Dutch developer has spent USD 18.6 million over the year before financial close on a contracted 330 MW wind project in Colombia's La Guajira (fictional project company; USD-indexed PPA); at close the project needs USD 112.0 million of equity (which includes reimbursing the development costs from the project budget), and the equity is expected to receive USD 19.6 million a year from year 3 to year 27. Project equity IRR on these flows: 13.05%. A fictional Canadian pension-backed infrastructure fund buys 49% at close and requires 11.5%. Computations: present value of distributions at 11.5% USD 128.07 million; equity NPV at the buyer's rate USD 16.07 million; development premium for 49% = USD 7.88 million (Equation 32.1); the buyer pays its 49% of contributions (USD 54.88 million) plus the premium (total USD 62.76 million) and earns exactly 11.5%; at a 13.0% buyer rate the premium falls to USD 0.25 million. The developer, keeping 51%, funding its share and receiving the premium and reimbursement, earns about 14.2% on its cash flows from the development spend (timing simplification stated). Lesson: the premium is the buyer paying today for value the developer created, at the buyer's discount rate; the gap between the two parties' rates sets the negotiation.

**Example 32.4 A yieldco's cost of equity and its incentive distribution rights (Illustrative)** (`ex:32.4`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional NYSE-listed yieldco owning contracted US wind and solar plants pays an annual dividend of USD 1.28 per share out of cash available for distribution of USD 1.42 per share (payout 90.1%), shares trade at USD 21.40 and investors expect 8% dividend growth. Dividend yield 5.98%; cost of equity by the dividend-growth model k = D1/P + g = 14.46%. IDR tiers on quarterly distributions per unit: up to USD 0.2600 none; USD 0.2600 to 0.3000, 15% of the incremental total to the IDR holder; 0.3000 to 0.3500, 25%; above 0.3500, 50% (the IDR holder's share is of total distributions in each tier, so for every incremental USD 1.00 to unitholders it receives s/(1 − s)). At USD 0.3200 per unit per quarter the IDR holder receives USD 0.0137 per unit (4.1% of the total distributed); at USD 0.4000, USD 0.0737 (15.6%). Lesson: growth funds the sponsor's IDRs, so a yieldco must keep growing to deliver its public holders' return, and a falling share price raises its cost of equity and halts acquisitions. (TerraForm's real Minimum Quarterly Distribution was USD 0.2257; the tiers here are Illustrative.)

## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 32.1 Equity commitment ladder** (`fw:equity-commitment-ladder`), original. Rung by rung, from most to least certain for lenders: (1) equity paid in before first drawdown; (2) equity paid pro rata, undrawn balance backed by LCs from banks rated at a stated minimum; (3) pro rata with a sponsor guarantee from an investment-grade parent; (4) back-ended with LCs; (5) back-ended against an equity bridge loan with sponsor guarantees; (6) back-ended with an unrated sponsor's undertaking only. For each rung the reader writes the sponsor's IRR gain, the lenders' exposure, the cost of support, and the trigger for moving up a rung (downgrade, default, change of control). Used to negotiate the equity plan: sponsors climb down the ladder for return; lenders charge for every rung.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist (equity plan): commitment amount tested against the maximum funding requirement including overrun cases; share capital meets local minimum-capital and distribution-law needs; shareholder-loan terms within thin-cap and interest-limitation limits and with treaty withholding confirmed; contribution timing and LC requirements agreed with each sponsor's treasury; LC bank rating and replacement mechanics; contingent equity sized against the IE's overrun case; development fee conditions and lender treatment; transfer restrictions and lock-ins compatible with planned farm-downs; for US projects, tax-credit qualification evidence (beginning of construction, prevailing wage and apprenticeship, PFE tests) in the CP list.
- Red flags: equity commitments backed by a sponsor whose own rating is near the LC trigger; shareholder-loan interest that cannot be deducted; a development fee paid at close that leaves the developer with no capital at risk; a farm-down agreed before financial close that breaches the lenders' lock-in; a yieldco sponsor holding voting control without economic exposure; tax-credit transfers with no recapture protection.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: lenders want equity in before or alongside debt; limit: back-ended equity is accepted against strong LCs. Shareholder loans usually beat share capital on after-tax cash; limit: interest limitation rules and distribution-law constraints. Development premia shrink as the buyer's required return rises toward the project's equity IRR.
- Common mistakes: counting LC fees as free; forgetting that capitalized shareholder-loan interest grows the sponsors' claim but not the lenders' cushion; valuing a farm-down at the seller's discount rate; describing tax-credit rules without a date.
- Questions experts ask: What happens to the equity commitment if a sponsor is downgraded mid-construction? Which sponsor would fund an overrun, and is that written down? What is the equity's real cost after LC fees and bridge interest? Who controls the vote, and does it match who owns the cash?

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the CFO of a strategic developer with a BBB- rating. You hold 100% of a 410 MW wind project that reaches financial close in eight weeks; the equity requirement is USD 168.5 million. A pension-backed infrastructure fund offers to buy 49% at close at a development premium of USD 11.2 million, pro rata funding, and wants joint control of reserved matters. Your bank group offers an equity bridge loan that would let you defer all equity to COD at 5.6%, guaranteed by your parent. Your board wants to recycle capital into two other projects and to keep control. Recommend.

Reasoning to outline: compare IRR and capital recycled under (a) farm-down at close with pro rata funding, (b) EBL with 100% ownership and a farm-down after COD; price the premium against the fund's likely discount rate (Example 32.3 logic) and the post-COD de-risked price; weigh parent-guarantee capacity under the BBB- rating; governance cost of joint control; lenders' lock-in and consent. Expert answer: farm down at close only if the premium is close to what a post-COD sale would fetch net of construction risk; otherwise use the EBL, keep control through construction, and sell after COD, subject to the parent's guarantee capacity.

What would change the answer: a downgrade risk for the parent (EBL guarantee becomes costly; take the farm-down); construction risk the IE rates high (the post-COD premium rises, favoring a later sale); a lender lock-in requiring 51% until two years after COD (forces timing).

## 2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. A large share of the capital available to projects in the Gulf, Malaysia and parts of Africa and Asia comes from banks and investors that may not lend at interest. They finance projects instead by buying, building, leasing and selling assets, or by partnering. This chapter teaches the principles behind those constraints, the contracts project finance uses (istisna'a, ijara and forward ijara, wakala, murabaha, musharaka), how an Islamic tranche and a project sukuk are built and priced to match a conventional loan, where the economic equivalence breaks, and how Islamic and conventional tranches share one financing (with the intercreditor interface itself left to Chapter 53). Sadara is the reference case.

What you will be able to do:

- Structure an Islamic tranche for a project's construction and operating phases and show that its rentals match a conventional loan's debt service (Capabilities 5 and 6).

## 3. Concepts owned and concepts assumed

### Concepts owned

1. The prohibitions: riba (interest), gharar (excessive uncertainty), maysir (speculation), and prohibited sectors; the requirement that returns be tied to assets, trade or partnership.
2. Sharia supervision: Sharia boards, fatwas (pronouncements) on a transaction's documents, the role of standard-setting bodies such as AAOIFI, and variation in interpretation across markets. From FS: t-islamic: the jurisdictions that make AAOIFI's Shari'ah standards mandatory and the fact that Malaysia and Saudi Arabia, the two largest sukuk markets, are not on that list; the standards most used in project finance (SS 9 Ijarah, SS 11 Istisna' and Parallel Istisna', SS 12 Sharikah/Musharaka, SS 23 Agency, SS 8 Murabaha, SS 17 Investment Sukuk, the last listed as under redrafting); draft Shari'ah Standard No. 62 "Sukuk" (exposure draft approved September 2023, released November 6, 2023, consultation extended to July 31, 2024; AAOIFI confirmed on April 28, 2025 that it was still a draft being amended) and its central tension: genuine ownership and risk-bearing by sukuk holders against fixed-price purchase undertakings. The sheet's "Do not state" list binds: never say SS 62 has been issued or give an effective date; never quantify non-compliant sukuk; never quote the Arabic text; never say Malaysia or Saudi Arabia mandates AAOIFI standards.
3. Istisna'a: an order to manufacture or build; the financier commissions the asset and the project company (or its contractor) builds it under a parallel istisna'a; staged payments; use in construction financing.
4. Ijara: a lease of an asset owned by the financier; fixed and variable rent; the lessor's ownership responsibilities (major maintenance, insurance, total loss); purchase undertakings and sale undertakings at the end.
5. Forward ijara (ijara mawsufa fi al-dhimma): a lease agreed before the asset exists, with advance rentals during construction credited against later rent or refunded if the asset is never delivered.
6. Wakala: an agency in which the financier appoints the project company (or a manager) as agent to procure assets or invest funds on its behalf; wakala-ijara combinations (as at Sadara).
7. Murabaha and commodity murabaha: cost-plus sale with deferred payment; use for working-capital lines and for short-dated liquidity; tawarruq-type structures and their scholarly debate in outline (one paragraph, neutral; no ruling stated as fact; no claim from FS: t-islamic's "Do not state" list, such as the 2007 "85% non-compliant" remark).
8. Musharaka and diminishing musharaka: partnership financing; the Sadara sukuk's musharaka between Sadara and the sukuk issuer.
9. The istisna'a–ijara project structure: asset transfer or co-ownership, parallel istisna'a with the EPC contractor or project company, forward lease, service agency agreement under which the project company performs the lessor's ownership duties, total loss and dissolution events, purchase undertaking at maturity or on default.
10. Pricing: rentals benchmarked to SOFR or SAIBOR plus a margin (using a benchmark to set a rent is accepted by the market; the writer states this as practice and attributes any scholarly position to a fact sheet), advance rentals, fixed rent as the principal equivalent.
11. Late payment and default: no default interest; late-payment amounts paid to charity or limited to actual cost under some structures; consequences for parity with conventional lenders' default interest.
12. Prepayment and termination: purchase price under the purchase undertaking; break costs as actual cost.
13. Hedging: Islamic profit-rate swaps built on wa'd (unilateral promise) structures in outline; ranking with conventional swaps (Chapter 53).
14. Project sukuk: certificates of ownership in assets or a venture, issued by an SPV; asset-based versus asset-backed; tradability and tangible-asset requirements in outline; certificate-holder decision-making (the 2021 Sadara vote); market context from FS: t-islamic (IIFM Sukuk Report 2026: global issuance USD 262.9 billion in 2025, outstanding about USD 1.02 trillion at December 31, 2025, with the source named and not mixed with S&P's figure); Dana Gas as the lesson that Sharia-compliance risk is also enforcement risk, without its settlement terms or the UAE outcome.
15. Islamic and conventional co-financing: common terms agreement, pro rata sharing of payments and enforcement proceeds, title held by the Islamic financier or its agent, total loss treatment, insurance proceeds, and economic parity; the voting and intercreditor architecture is Chapter 53 (conventional–Islamic interface).
16. Framework 33.1, the Sharia structure selector (original).
17. Framework 33.2, the Islamic and conventional parity check (original).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 33.1 Rentals under an istisna'a and forward ijara (Illustrative)** (`ex:33.1`). Scenario: in 2026 four fictional Gulf banks provide a USD 176.4 million Islamic tranche for part of the construction of a 300,000 m3/day reverse-osmosis desalination plant at Ras Mohaisen, Saudi Arabia (fictional project company), under an istisna'a, paying the contractor USD 29.3, 53.8, 58.6 and 34.7 million at the start of four semiannual construction periods; the project company pays advance rentals during construction equal to six-month SOFR plus 1.95% on the cumulative amount paid; six-month SOFR (Illustrative path) 4.12%, 3.98%, 3.85%, 3.71% in construction and 3.66%, 3.62% in the first two operating periods; semiannual rates taken as half the annual rate (simplification stated). Advance rentals: USD 0.89, 2.46, 4.11 and 4.99 million (total USD 12.46 million), identical to the interest a conventional loan with the same draws would accrue. After delivery, rent has two parts: fixed rent of USD 7.35 million each period (the acquisition cost repaid over 24 periods) and variable rent on the outstanding acquisition cost: period 1 variable USD 4.95 million, total USD 12.30 million; period 2 variable USD 4.71 million, total USD 12.06 million. Equation 33.1. Lesson: the cash flows equal a conventional amortizing loan's; the difference is that the financier owns the plant and the rent is for its use, so total loss, maintenance and late payment are handled differently (33.3.2, 33.3.3).

**Example 33.2 A commodity murabaha working-capital line (Illustrative)** (`ex:33.2`). Scenario: in March 2026 a fictional 1,200 MW combined-cycle IPP in Oman needs USD 24.7 million for 180 days; a fictional Omani Islamic bank buys metals on an exchange for USD 24.7 million and sells them to the project company for deferred payment at cost plus a profit of 6.10% a year on an actual/360 basis; the project company sells the metals spot for cash. Markup USD 0.7534 million; deferred price USD 25.4534 million. The writer explains why the deferred price is fixed at signing (no later change) and how a rollover works, and states the scholarly debate over commodity-based liquidity structures in neutral terms without naming a ruling (FS: t-islamic "Do not state" applies).

**Example 33.3 Periodic distributions on a project sukuk (Illustrative)** (`ex:33.3`). Scenario: in 2027 a fictional issuer for a contracted 1,500 MW solar plant in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province issues a SAR-denominated amortizing project sukuk of SAR 737.5 million (sized to the outstanding acquisition cost of the plant assets transferred to the issuer) with periodic distributions at six-month SAIBOR plus 0.95%, SAIBOR at 5.62% for the period; semiannual distribution SAR 24.23 million (737.5 × 6.57% / 2, simplification on day count stated). The writer shows where the distribution comes from (rentals under the forward lease flowing through the issuer), what the certificate holders own, and what happens to distributions before completion when the sponsors' completion guarantees back them (Sadara's structure, FS: sadara).

**Example 33.4 Sharing an enforcement loss between Islamic and conventional tranches (Illustrative)** (`ex:33.4`). Scenario: a fictional USD 893.6 million senior financing closed in 2025 for a 1,050 MW gas-fired IPP in Kuwait has a USD 634.2 million conventional tranche and a USD 259.4 million istisna'a–ijara tranche on pari passu terms; after a default in 2029 the lenders sell the project as a going concern for net proceeds of USD 537.9 million. Under pro rata sharing each tranche recovers 60.2%: conventional USD 381.75 million, Islamic USD 156.15 million. The writer then shows why the outcome requires drafting: the Islamic financiers' claim is a purchase price under the purchase undertaking, not a loan balance; the asset sold includes assets the Islamic financiers own; and accrued "rent" after termination cannot be claimed as interest. The parity mechanics (a sharing clause based on "outstanding acquisition cost" equated with principal; excluding default interest from the conventional share or redirecting it) are introduced here and left to Chapter 53 (sec:53.8) for the intercreditor drafting.

## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 33.1 Sharia structure selector** (`fw:sharia-structure-selector`), original. Questions: (1) Does the asset exist yet? If not, istisna'a or forward ijara (with wakala for procurement). (2) Will the financier own a tangible asset for the life of the financing? If yes, ijara; if the need is short and assetless, murabaha. (3) Is the financing to be traded as a security? If yes, a sukuk over assets that satisfy tradability rules. (4) Do the parties want profit-sharing rather than fixed returns? Musharaka or diminishing musharaka. (5) Can title or a usufruct be transferred under local law at acceptable cost (registration, stamp duty, foreign ownership limits)? If not, choose a structure that does not move title, or drop the Islamic tranche. Output: a recommended structure per financing phase with the local-law precondition flagged.

**Framework 33.2 Islamic and conventional parity check** (`fw:islamic-parity-check`), original. For each of ten items write the conventional term, the Islamic equivalent, and the drafting that equalizes them: (1) principal and acquisition cost; (2) interest and variable rent; (3) fees; (4) default interest and late payment; (5) prepayment and break costs; (6) total loss and insurance proceeds; (7) major maintenance and service agency costs; (8) hedging; (9) voting weight; (10) enforcement and sale of assets the Islamic financiers own. Any item without an equalizing term goes to the intercreditor negotiation (Chapter 53).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist (adding an Islamic tranche): Sharia board engaged early with the term sheet; local-law analysis of asset transfer or usufruct, registration and stamp costs; service agency scope matched to the O&M and LTSA arrangements; total-loss and insurance provisions aligned with the insurance program and loss payee clauses; purchase and sale undertakings priced to equal conventional prepayment and acceleration amounts; late-payment mechanism agreed; hedging route agreed; parity check (Framework 33.2) completed before the intercreditor term sheet.
- Red flags: an asset transfer that conflicts with a concession's transfer-back obligation or a government's land rights; title held in a jurisdiction where the financiers' ownership exposes them to environmental or third-party liability; an Islamic tranche whose rent cannot follow the conventional tranche's base-rate fallback; a sukuk structure whose tradability depends on assets that will be depreciated or transferred.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: economics can be matched almost exactly; limit: late payment and default interest cannot. Istisna'a–ijara fits greenfield construction; limit: brownfield refinancings may need a sale-and-leaseback of existing assets, which local law may tax. Islamic tranches add documents and time; limit: in Gulf programs the documents are standardized and the extra time is small.
- Common mistakes: calling rent "interest" in drafts; forgetting that the financier as owner must bear certain risks that cannot simply be passed to the lessee; assuming one Sharia board's approval binds another bank's board.
- Questions experts ask: Who owns what, and when does title move? What happens on total loss before the insurance pays? Does the purchase price on acceleration equal the conventional lenders' claim? Which board has signed off, and on which version of the documents?

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are lenders' counsel for a USD 1,120.0 million financing of a 1,500 MW solar-plus-storage project in a Gulf state. The sponsor wants USD 400.0 million from Islamic banks under an istisna'a–ijara tranche and the rest from conventional banks and an ECA. The ECA asks for its tranche to be secured by a mortgage over the plant and objects to the Islamic financiers owning the plant. The term sheet must be agreed in three weeks.

Reasoning to outline: the conflict is ownership versus security; options are co-ownership with the Islamic financiers holding title to a defined set of assets while the project company retains the rest; usufruct (right of use) structures instead of full title; a security agent holding the Islamic financiers' ownership interest for all senior creditors on enforcement; parity check items 6 and 10. Expert answer: propose a defined-asset istisna'a–ijara over identified plant blocks, with a sale undertaking that operates on enforcement so the security agent can sell the whole plant as a going concern and share proceeds pro rata, and with the ECA's consent rights entrenched.

What would change the answer: local law that does not recognize the Islamic financiers' title without registration (adds cost and time); a Sharia board that will not accept a sale undertaking triggered by conventional lenders' enforcement (may force a separate asset pool); an ECA with its own experience of Islamic co-financing (reduces friction).

## 2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Some projects that a country needs cannot be financed on commercial terms: the tariff they need is too high for the offtaker, the risk is too high for commercial lenders, the tenor is too long, or the currency is wrong. Public and philanthropic money changes those facts by being cheaper (concessional loans and grants), by taking losses first (first-loss capital), by guaranteeing others' losses (partial risk and partial credit guarantees), by paying part of the capital cost (viability-gap funding), or by providing what markets will not (local-currency debt and hedges). This chapter teaches each tool: what it changes, how it is priced or sized, who carries the cost, and how to tell good blending from subsidy that crowds out private money. Political risk insurance belongs to Chapter 60; currency risk analysis and contractual protections (indexation, offshore accounts, payment security) belong to Chapter 59; this chapter owns the financing instruments.

What you will be able to do:

- Measure the concession in a loan, and show how a blended debt package lowers the tariff a project needs (Capabilities 5 and 9).
- Size first-loss capital and a partial risk or partial credit guarantee, and trace the indemnity chain back to the host government (Capabilities 8 and 9).
- Size viability-gap funding for a project whose user revenue cannot carry its capital cost (Capability 9).
- Choose among local-currency solutions for a project with local-currency revenue, and explain the cost of each (Capabilities 5 and 8).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 34.1 How concessional debt lowers the tariff (Illustrative)** (`ex:34.1`). Scenario: in 2026 Senegal's fictional state utility tenders a 220 MWac solar plant with 110 MW / 440 MWh of storage near Kaolack (fictional bidders; all financing terms Illustrative); capex USD 384.7 million, financed 70% debt (USD 269.29 million) and 30% equity (USD 115.41 million); output 436.9 GWh a year; operating costs USD 10.9 million a year; equity requires 13.0% over a 25-year life; no tax, no indexation, level tariff, no ratio constraint (simplifications stated, with forward references to Chapters 35 and 43). Case A, commercial debt: 18-year annuity at 7.35%, debt service USD 27.45 million a year; the level revenue that gives equity 13.0% is USD 52.26 million a year, a tariff of USD 119.62/MWh. Case B, blended: 35% of the debt (USD 94.25 million) from a climate fund at 0.75% for 25 years with 5 years' grace (interest-only USD 0.71 million a year for 5 years, then USD 5.09 million a year for 20 years), the rest (USD 175.04 million) commercial at 7.35% over 18 years (USD 17.84 million a year). Required revenue USD 46.28 million, tariff USD 105.94/MWh, 11.4% lower. The blended debt's average cost (IRR of its debt service) is 4.66%. The climate-fund loan's grant element at 7.35% is 57.9% (Equation 34.1). Excel: the annuity is `=PMT(F5,F6,-F7)` with the rate in F5; the grant element is an `excel` block, `=1-NPV(F9,J30:AH30)/F7` with the market rate in F9 and the debt-service row in J30:AH30. Discuss who captures the concession (the offtaker through the tariff if the tariff is bid competitively with the terms announced, as at Noor; the sponsor if not).

**Example 34.2 First-loss capital and the senior lender's expected loss (Illustrative)** (`ex:34.2`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional Nairobi-based facility lends to a portfolio of distributed solar and mini-grid companies in Kenya and Uganda, with a loss distribution on the total financing of: 0% loss with probability 88%, 20% with 7%, 45% with 4%, 80% with 1%; pool expected loss 4.0% (expected loss as defined in ssec:15.3.1). Without first loss, a senior lender funding 100% bears 4.0% expected loss. With a 15% first-loss tranche from a fictional bilateral donor, senior funds 85%: senior expected loss falls to 2.59% of the senior tranche, and the first-loss tranche's expected loss is 12.0% of its size. Lesson: the first-loss provider is buying down the senior lender's risk at a known expected cost; whether it is worth it depends on whether the senior lender would otherwise not lend at all (additionality) and on the price the senior lender then charges (minimum concessionality). Real-world contrast from FS: t-blended-finance (the SDG Loan Fund's first-loss and second-loss layers, as the sheet states them).

**Example 34.3 A partial risk guarantee behind an offtaker's letter of credit (Illustrative)** (`ex:34.3`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional 320 MW gas-fired IPP near Takoradi, Ghana, sells to a fictional state-owned offtaker that pays monthly capacity charges of USD 7.84 million and energy charges of USD 5.12 million; the PPA requires a standby LC of two months' capacity plus one month's energy = USD 20.8 million (2.65 months of capacity charges, 65.8% of a USD 31.6 million semiannual debt service). The LC bank (fictional) will issue only if a fictional multilateral DFI guarantees its reimbursement claim on the state; the DFI's fee is 1.85% a year unsubsidized, or 0.75% with a donor fee subsidy: annual cost USD 0.385 million against USD 0.156 million, a saving of USD 0.229 million. Trace a default: the offtaker misses two months; the project draws the LC; the offtaker must reimburse the LC bank within a set period; it fails; the LC bank claims on the DFI; the DFI pays and claims on the government under the indemnity. Lesson: the PRG buys liquidity, not solvency: it covers a short gap and leaves the long-term affordability problem where it was (cross-reference Chapter 59).

**Example 34.4 A partial credit guarantee on a local-currency bond (Illustrative)** (`ex:34.4`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional toll-road concessionaire in Peru issues a USD 213.6 million equivalent amortizing sol-denominated bond (fictional issuer; Illustrative terms) carrying a fictional DFI's guarantee that pays losses up to 40% of principal (USD 85.44 million) before investors bear any loss. In a default with 35% recovery, investors would lose USD 138.84 million unguaranteed; with the guarantee they lose USD 53.40 million and recover 75% overall. The writer contrasts this first-loss style PCG with a PCG covering specified late maturities (tenor extension), and with Puerta de Hierro's full guarantee.

**Example 34.5 Sizing viability-gap funding (Illustrative)** (`ex:34.5`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional Indian state tenders an urban water treatment and distribution concession for a city of about 1.4 million people (fictional city and bidders; all figures Illustrative and in USD equivalent; no national VGF scheme is described) costing USD 517.4 million; net operating cash flow starts at USD 48.6 million of revenue less USD 14.2 million of operating costs, with revenue growing 4.5% a year and costs 4.0% a year over 30 years; the project needs an 11.0% pre-financing return to attract bidders. Present value of net cash flows at 11.0%: USD 451.25 million. Viability gap: USD 66.15 million (12.8% of capital cost), paid as a construction grant. Variants for the exercises: bidding the grant (Case T analogy, inputs only), paying the grant in milestones, and clawback if demand exceeds forecasts.

**Example 34.6 Dollar debt against local revenue under devaluation (Illustrative)** (`ex:34.6`). Scenario: in 2026 a fictional bulk water supplier in Ghana with cedi (GHS) revenue indexed to local inflation of 9% a year borrows USD 96.8 million for 10 years at 7.2% (annual debt service USD 13.91 million) when the rate is GHS 12.40 per USD; first-year revenue is set at 1.40 times the dollar debt service in cedis at that rate, indexed for one year (all rates are Illustrative assumptions for the example, not forecasts for the cedi). If the currency depreciates at the 8% a year implied by forward rates, cover (revenue over debt service in GHS) moves from 1.41x in year 1 to 1.54x in year 10; if it depreciates at 12% a year, cover falls from 1.36x to 1.07x. Alternative: a fixed-rate cedi loan of GHS 1,200.3 million (the same principal at GHS 12.40 per USD) at 15.8% (a level annuity of GHS 246.5 million a year) gives cover of only 1.07x in year 1 rising to 2.32x in year 10, because high nominal local rates front-load real debt service. Lesson: local-currency debt removes the devaluation risk but its high nominal rate creates a front-loading problem that inflation-linked (UVR-type) debt or a sculpted profile solves (forward reference Chapter 36). ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 34.1 Blending decision test** (`fw:blending-decision-test`), original. Steps: (1) state the binding gap (affordability, risk, tenor or currency) with numbers; (2) state the counterfactual: what commercial terms are available today, from whom, and with what evidence; (3) choose the tool that addresses the binding gap directly (concessional debt for affordability, first loss or guarantees for risk, long-tenor public lending or PCGs for tenor, local-currency tools for currency); (4) size it at the minimum that makes the deal financeable (test with the project's financial model at the lenders' required ratios); (5) decide who captures the concession and make it competitive (announce the terms before bidding); (6) count the cost to the public sponsor, including contingent liabilities; (7) set an exit or step-down so the concession does not become permanent. Demonstrated on Example 34.1 and on Case P in Section 34.10.

**Framework 34.2 Local-currency route map** (`fw:local-currency-route-map`), original. A decision path: (1) Is revenue in hard currency or indexed to it? If yes, hard-currency debt with convertibility protection (Chapter 59). (2) If revenue is local: can the local bank market lend at the needed tenor? If yes, local banks, possibly with a DFI liquidity line. (3) If not: is there a local institutional market (pensions, insurers)? If yes, local or inflation-linked bonds, possibly with a PCG. (4) If not: can a DFI lend in local currency (funded by its own local bonds or swaps)? (5) If not: hard-currency debt plus a cross-currency hedge from a specialist fund, priced against the devaluation the hedge removes. (6) If none: reduce hard-currency gearing and push currency risk to the offtaker through indexation, with the affordability consequences stated. Demonstrated on Example 34.6 and on Case P (why a cauri loan was not available in 2017).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist (using blended and guarantee tools): binding gap stated with numbers; counterfactual evidenced; tool matched to gap; size tested in the model at lenders' ratios; capture of the concession decided and documented in the tender; guarantee trigger, exclusions, demand procedure and reinstatement mapped against the PPA and the LC; host-government indemnity approved and recorded as a contingent liability; fee and subsidy source confirmed for the full term; local-currency tranche currency, tenor and rate basis matched to revenue indexation; exit or step-down plan.
- Red flags: concessional terms not announced before bidding (the sponsor captures the subsidy); a guarantee sized to a political number rather than to the payment cycle; a PRG covering an LC that the offtaker is not obliged to replenish quickly; local-currency debt at a high fixed nominal rate on a project with inflation-indexed revenue (front-loading); a VGF grant paid upfront without clawback; multiple guarantees covering the same risk twice while another risk is uncovered.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: concession should be the minimum that makes the deal financeable; limit: first-of-a-kind programs need more to set a precedent (Noor I's CTF per kW fell from about USD 1,231 to about USD 680 for Noor II and III). Liquidity PRGs cover months, not years; limit: they can be rolled if reinstated. Local-currency debt fits local revenue; limit: tenor and depth.
- Common mistakes: measuring concession by rate alone without tenor and grace (use the grant element); treating a PRG as protection against the offtaker's insolvency; forgetting the government's indemnity in its debt statistics; assuming a currency hedge removes convertibility risk.
- Questions experts ask: What would happen without this money? Who keeps the subsidy? What does the government owe if the guarantee is called, and has its finance ministry budgeted for it? In which currency does the project earn, and what happens to debt service if that currency halves?

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: you head the PPP unit of a lower-middle-income country's finance ministry. Your first 150 MW solar-plus-storage tender must deliver power at a tariff the state utility can afford. A climate fund offers USD 60.0 million at 0.75% for 25 years; a DFI offers a USD 50.0 million liquidity PRG behind the utility's LC, with a counter-indemnity from your ministry; a bilateral donor offers a USD 25.0 million first-loss tranche; and a currency-hedging fund will hedge local-currency debt at a cost that adds about 4 percentage points a year. Your minister wants all four, announced at the bid launch in six weeks. Recommend.

Reasoning to outline: Framework 34.1: identify the binding gap (affordability versus utility credit versus currency); counterfactual (what bids would look like with only the PRG); avoid stacking (the first loss and the PRG may protect the same lenders against the same risk); the indemnity is your ministry's contingent liability and must fit the guarantee ceiling; announce concessional terms in the RFP so bidders pass them into the tariff; decide currency policy (dollar-indexed tariff moves risk to the utility; local-currency debt with a hedge costs more but protects the utility). Expert answer: take the climate-fund loan, on-lent or offered to all bidders on announced terms; take the liquidity PRG sized to the utility's payment cycle and record the indemnity; decline the first loss unless lenders' feedback shows it is needed; offer a local-currency option with the hedge as a bid variant and evaluate on tariff and risk to the utility.

What would change the answer: utility arrears already high (PRG becomes essential and may need to be larger); a deep local pension market (local bonds instead of the hedge); a guarantee ceiling already near its limit (drop or shrink the PRG and use concessional debt instead); lenders' sounding showing no appetite at any price for the utility's credit (first loss or a PCG needed).
