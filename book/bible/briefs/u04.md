## 14.B Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. The reader leaves Chapter 14 able to look at any project and name every way its cash flows can go wrong, using one consistent vocabulary that the rest of the book relies on. For each category in the coverage map (Section 4.C of `standards.md`) and the four the book adds, the chapter teaches what triggers the risk, how it travels through the project's contracts into a specific line of cash flow, in which phase it bites hardest, how large it typically is, and who usually ends up carrying it. The chapter also teaches why classification matters in money terms: the label a court or counterparty puts on an event (force majeure, change in law, commercial hardship) decides who pays, as the Mundra cases show. It closes by showing that risks travel in clusters, so a list of categories is the start of risk analysis, not the end.

What you will be able to do (the chapter prints these, two to four items, each ending with the capability number):

1. Identify and classify every material risk in a project, and trace each one from its trigger to the line of cash flow it hits (Capability 2).
2. Describe the risk profile of a project across development, construction, operations and end of life, and say where risk peaks and why (Capability 2).
3. Read a contract set and say, for each risk category, which party typically carries it and where the classification of an event could move it (Capability 3).
4. Build a first risk register for a new project from its description (Capability 15).

## 14.C Concepts owned and assumed

### Concepts owned (taught in full here)

Every coverage-map item that architecture.md assigns to Chapter 14 ("every risk category listed in coverage map C, defined with mechanism, example and typical bearer"):

1. Risk in the project finance sense: a variation in project cash flow, value or timing that some party must absorb; exposure; trigger (risk event); transmission path; risk bearer; the difference between a hazard and an exposure.
2. The risk card (Framework 14.1): the five-field way the book describes any risk.
3. Why classification matters: categories map to contractual treatments (relief, compensation, pass-through, termination).
4. The lifecycle risk profile (Framework 14.2, phase-by-category risk map): development, construction and commissioning, ramp-up, steady operations, end of life; the completion peak.
5. Development and permitting risk (including land and consent challenge risk).
6. Construction risk: cost, time (delay) and performance; the cost of delay per day.
7. Technology risk: proven, scaled-up, first-of-a-kind; performance-guarantee and manufacturing scale-up risk.
8. Resource and reserves risk: wind, solar, hydrology, geothermal, mineral and hydrocarbon reserves; risk persisting into operations.
9. Demand and volume risk; operating leverage (the multiplier from revenue shortfall to cash shortfall, a concept this chapter adopts; see change request CR-u04-03).
10. Price and market risk; merchant exposure; basis between the point of sale and the point of settlement (introduced here as a category; Chapter 20 owns basis and shape risk in hedges).
11. Input supply risk: quantity, quality, price, delivery and transport.
12. Operating risk: availability, performance, O&M cost, major maintenance, catastrophic loss, operator capability.
13. Counterparty credit risk: offtaker, contractor, supplier, hedge counterparty, insurer, account bank; concentration risk.
14. Interface risk: between contracts, between contractors, and between the private project and public works.
15. Interest rate risk.
16. Inflation risk, including indexation mismatch between revenue and cost.
17. Refinancing risk.
18. Liquidity risk: timing mismatch between cash in and cash out, distinct from solvency.
19. Currency risk in three forms: devaluation, convertibility, transfer; the FX breakeven rate.
20. Political risk in five forms: expropriation (direct and creeping), political violence, breach of contract by the state, change in law, non-honoring of sovereign financial obligations; sanctions as a sixth, modern form (added by the book).
21. Regulatory risk.
22. Legal and enforceability risk (validity of the concession or contract, enforceability of security and awards, foreign-law events under a domestic-law contract).
23. Environmental and social risk as a cash-flow and credit risk (the standards and management system are owned by Chapter 50).
24. Force majeure as a risk overlay rather than a category: natural and political force majeure; prolonged force majeure.
25. Sponsor risk: sponsor credit, commitment, capability, conflicts of interest (tied supply chains).
26. Climate risk: physical (acute and chronic) and transition, defined as categories (assessment methods owned by Chapter 84).
27. Cyber risk (operational technology and corporate systems).
28. Decommissioning risk (cost, timing, security for the obligation; obligations and reserves owned by Chapter 65).
29. Categories the book adds to the coverage-map list: financing and syndication risk before financial close; model and forecast risk; integrity risk (corruption and fraud exposure, law owned by Chapter 60).
30. Risk clustering and wrong-way risk: risks that are triggered by the same event, and protections that fail in the same state of the world as the loss they protect (concept adopted here; see CR-u04-04).
31. The risk register as an artifact (columns, granularity, ownership), as distinct from the risk matrix (Chapter 15).

**14.2 How the risk profile changes over the project's life** (`sec:14.2`)
- 14.2.1 Development: high probability, small money (`ssec:14.2.1`). Most projects die in development; the money at risk is the development budget, carried by the developer.
- 14.2.2 Construction and commissioning: the peak (`ssec:14.2.2`). Debt is drawn, no revenue, every physical risk live; why completion is the single biggest risk transition in project finance.
- 14.2.3 Ramp-up and steady operations (`ssec:14.2.3`). Risk falls but does not vanish; market, counterparty and political risks rise in relative weight as physical risk falls; the obsolescing bargain is named with a forward reference to Chapter 60.
- 14.2.4 End of life (`ssec:14.2.4`). Decommissioning, handback, terminal value. Framework 14.2 (`fw:phase-risk-map`), the phase-by-category risk map, with Exhibit 14.3 (a pgfplots line chart of illustrative "money at risk" over the phases, labeled Illustrative and with the numbers repeated in a small table) and Exhibit 14.4 (a matrix of the 25 categories against five phases, marked High/Medium/Low/None for a contracted thermal IPP).

**14.3 Development and permitting risk** (`sec:14.3`)
- 14.3.1 What kills projects before financial close (`ssec:14.3.1`). Site control, permits, grid access, offtake award, financing and syndication risk before close (the book's addition). Port of Miami Tunnel's 2008 collapse when its equity sponsor failed, revived in 2009 with a new sponsor, as the financing-risk example.
- 14.3.2 Permits that are challenged after they are granted (`ssec:14.3.2`). Maryland Purple Line: a federal court vacated the environmental approval in August 2016 and delayed the start by about a year; the lesson is that a granted permit is a risk until the challenge window closes. Typical bearer: developer before close; shared or host-government after close depending on the concession.

**14.4 Construction risk: cost, time and performance** (`sec:14.4`)
- 14.4.1 Cost overrun (`ssec:14.4.1`). Mechanism: quantities, prices, productivity, scope, ground; who bears it under lump-sum, multi-contract and cost-reimbursable approaches (named only; Chapters 22 and 23 own the forms). Real case: Ichthys LNG, capital estimate from USD 34 billion at FID to approximately USD 45 billion, with the power plant subcontract collapse as a driver.
- 14.4.2 Delay and what a day costs (`ssec:14.4.2`). Example 14.1. Equation 14.1 (daily cost of delay). Shows why delay cost is usually larger than people expect: lost revenue, interest during the delay, and fixed costs run together, and liquidated damages rarely cover all of it (forward reference to Chapter 22 for calibration).
- 14.4.3 Performance shortfall (`ssec:14.4.3`). Output, efficiency, availability at completion; performance liquidated damages buy down debt (forward reference to Chapter 22); the rejection threshold.
- 14.4.4 When the fixed price does not hold (`ssec:14.4.4`). Vogtle 3 and 4: a "substantially fixed price" EPC contract did not protect the owners when Westinghouse filed for Chapter 11 in March 2017; Toshiba settled its parent guarantee for USD 3.68 billion. Lesson: construction risk transferred to a contractor becomes contractor credit risk (link to 14.10). Note the fact sheet's caveat that Vogtle was utility balance-sheet finance, not non-recourse project finance.

**14.5 Technology risk** (`sec:14.5`)
- 14.5.1 Proven, scaled-up and first-of-a-kind (`ssec:14.5.1`). Definitions by operating hours, number of units, scale relative to reference plants; why lenders ask for a reference list.
- 14.5.2 A plant that runs but does not deliver the contracted output (`ssec:14.5.2`). Ivanpah: first commercial-scale solar tower plant in the US, entered service early 2014, struggled to meet the guaranteed energy production in its PPAs, forbearance agreements in December 2015 and amendments in 2017. Lesson: for a lender, a PPA is only as strong as the plant's ability to deliver the contracted energy.
- 14.5.3 Manufacturing scale-up as technology risk (`ssec:14.5.3`). Northvolt: project-style financing of Northvolt Ett, production ramp-up lagged, Chapter 11 in November 2024 and Swedish bankruptcy in March 2025. Lesson: when the "technology" is a production process, the risk is yield and ramp-up, and offtake contracts do not cure it.

**14.6 Resource and reserves risk** (`sec:14.6`)
- 14.6.1 Resource uncertainty by asset type (`ssec:14.6.1`). Wind, solar, hydrology, geothermal, mineral and hydrocarbon reserves; cross-reference Chapter 9 for P-values without re-teaching them.
- 14.6.2 Resource risk after completion (`ssec:14.6.2`). Example 14.2 (geothermal make-up drilling). Real case: Sarulla, where generation fell after COD because of wellfield problems at one plant and a lender-approved remediation and make-up drilling programme followed. Typical bearer: equity first, with lenders protected by reserves and approval rights (forward reference to Chapter 37).

**14.7 Demand, volume, price and market risk** (`sec:14.7`)
- 14.7.1 Demand and volume risk (`ssec:14.7.1`). Distinguishes demand (users choose whether to buy: tolls, ports, airports) from volume (the asset produces less: wind, curtailment). Example 14.3 with Equation 14.2 (operating leverage). Real cases: Sydney Cross City Tunnel (fewer than 25,000 vehicles a day at full toll against a bid forecast of about 70,000 at opening; receivers December 2006) and Dulles Greenway (opened six months early in 1995; traffic short of projections; in default almost immediately).
- 14.7.2 Price and market risk (`ssec:14.7.2`). Merchant revenue, capture prices (cross-reference Chapter 11), and nodal basis. Real case: northern Chile solar, where contracts settled at the withdrawal node while generators were paid at a zero-price injection node; María Elena Solar insolvent September 2022. Typical bearer: equity, then lenders through thicker cushions (forward reference to Chapter 36 for merchant tails, Chapter 20 for hedges).
- 14.7.3 Curtailment as volume risk created by the grid (`ssec:14.7.3`). Short: Chile curtailment of about a fifth of solar and wind output in 2024; who bears curtailment depends on the PPA's deemed-energy clause (forward reference to Chapter 18).

**14.8 Input supply risk** (`sec:14.8`)
- 14.8.1 Quantity, quality, price and delivery (`ssec:14.8.1`). Fuel, feedstock, water, ore; transportation as a separate link in the chain.
- 14.8.2 The fuel price the bid assumed (`ssec:14.8.2`). Example 14.4 (imported coal, partial escalation). Real case: Tata Mundra and Adani Mundra: Indonesia's 2010–11 benchmark pricing rule; CERC, APTEL and the Supreme Court of India (April 11, 2017) on force majeure and change in law; ADB's 2020 finding that the only workable mitigant was pass-through escalation. Typical bearer: offtaker under pass-through, otherwise the project company.

**14.9 Operating risk** (`sec:14.9`)
- 14.9.1 Availability, performance and cost (`ssec:14.9.1`). How each hits capacity payments, energy revenue and opex; major maintenance cycles; operator capability.
- 14.9.2 Catastrophic loss and the size of insurance limits (`ssec:14.9.2`). Moss Landing 300: January 16, 2025 fire; approximately USD 400 million net book value written off; insurance limits of USD 500 million net of deductibles collected in full by February 2026, while clean-up estimates rose to approximately USD 175 million and other losses kept growing. Lesson: a catastrophic operating loss can exceed the insurance tower and arrives with environmental and decommissioning costs attached.

**14.10 Counterparty credit risk** (`sec:14.10`)
- 14.10.1 Every counterparty is a credit (`ssec:14.10.1`). Offtaker, EPC contractor, O&M operator, fuel supplier, hedge counterparty, insurer, account bank; how each failure reaches cash flow; replacement cost as the measure of exposure.
- 14.10.2 The offtaker that cannot pay (`ssec:14.10.2`). Dabhol: by 2001 the take-or-pay commitment equalled about half of MSEB's budget; guarantee chains are not cash.
- 14.10.3 The contractor that fails mid-build (`ssec:14.10.3`). Carillion: liquidation January 15, 2018; two PFI hospitals whose thin project companies could not fund completion; the NAO's estimate that investors, insurers and Carillion lost at least GBP 603 million on the two hospitals.
- 14.10.4 Concentration (`ssec:14.10.4`). Thames Tideway: all revenue flows through Thames Water, which Tideway itself treats as a high risk. One-counterparty projects are the norm, so concentration is measured, not avoided.

**14.11 Interface risk** (`sec:14.11`)
- 14.11.1 Where one party's work meets another's (`ssec:14.11.1`). Interfaces between contractors, between the plant and the grid, between design and construction, between the project and public works; why interface risk falls into gaps unless a contract assigns it.
- 14.11.2 A wind farm with no line to the market (`ssec:14.11.2`). Lake Turkana Wind Power: 310 MW ready in 2017; the state-built 400 kV line energized in September 2018 after its contractor's insolvency; payments for undelivered energy reported at about EUR 127 million borne by the Kenyan side. Lesson: interface risk does not disappear when allocated; it moves to whoever the contract names and becomes their fiscal or credit problem.

## 14.E Opening and close

Opening. Start in 2010–11, when Indonesia required coal exports to be priced at or above government benchmark prices, even under existing long-term contracts. Two of India's largest power plants, the 4,000 MW Tata Mundra UMPP and Adani's 4,620 MW Mundra plant, had bid tariffs that assumed cheap Indonesian coal; their fuel contracts stopped delivering it. The rest of the story is a fight over a label: the generators called it force majeure and change in law, the regulator said neither applied and granted a compensatory tariff anyway, the tribunal upheld relief, and on April 11, 2017 the Supreme Court of India held that a fuel price rise was commercial hardship and that a change in Indonesian law was not a "change in law" under PPAs covering Indian law. Who paid depended on what kind of risk the event was. The opening ends on that point; no announcement of the chapter's contents.

Close (`sec:14.25`). Crystallize: the taxonomy is a vocabulary for tracing an event to a line of cash flow and to a party, and the categories cluster. Pose the open problem: Kilnworth's register (Section 14.22) names 26 risks and a "typical bearer" for each, but a typical bearer is not an agreed one, and nothing yet says how big each risk is, who can carry it at the lowest price, or whether lenders will lend against what is left. Chapter 15 answers it.

## 14.F Worked examples

**Example 14.1 What a 90-day delay costs a wind farm** (`ex:14.1`, in 14.4.2)
- Scenario: a 182.4 MW onshore wind farm in the US Southwest Power Pool region, COD scheduled for June 2024, PPA price USD 47.80/MWh fixed, PPA term fixed by calendar date so delayed output is lost, not deferred.
- Inputs: P50 net capacity factor 38.6%; construction debt drawn at the scheduled COD USD 236.5 million at an all-in rate of 6.85% a year (simple daily accrual, 365-day year); fixed costs that continue during the delay (site team, extended insurance, owner's engineer) USD 18,400 per day; EPC delay liquidated damages USD 95,000 per day.
- Compute: energy per day = 182.4 × 24 × 0.386 = 1,689.8 MWh; lost revenue USD 80,770 per day; interest USD 44,384 per day; total daily cost USD 143,554 (Equation 14.1: $\mathrm{DC} = C \times 24 \times \mathrm{NCF} \times p + D \times i/365 + F$); 90-day cost USD 12.92 million; liquidated damages for 90 days USD 8.55 million; uncovered USD 4.37 million; LDs cover 66.2% of the daily cost.
- Teaching point: delay cost has three components that the reader can estimate on the back of an envelope, and LDs set as a negotiated rate rarely cover lost revenue as well as interest. State simplification: no lost-revenue recovery through PPA extension; no tax effect.

**Example 14.2 A geothermal field that declines faster than forecast** (`ex:14.2`, in 14.6.2)
- Scenario: a 110.0 MW geothermal plant in a volcanic arc in Southeast Asia, energy sales contract at USD 68.40/MWh, make-up wells drilled to hold output at 110 MW.
- Inputs: forecast steam field decline 2.5% a year (compound); actual decline 4.0% a year; each make-up well adds 5.8 MW; each well costs USD 7.35 million; plant capacity factor 92%.
- Compute over five years: capacity lost without drilling, forecast 13.08 MW, actual 20.31 MW; wells needed, forecast 2.26 rounded up to 3 (USD 22.05 million budget), actual 3.50 rounded up to 4 (USD 29.40 million); overrun USD 7.35 million, 33% over the make-up budget. Lost revenue if one well arrives nine months late: 5.8 × 0.92 × 8,760 × 0.75 = 35,058 MWh, USD 2.40 million.
- Also show the year-by-year capacity table (forecast vs actual without drilling): year 1 107.25 vs 105.60; year 2 104.57 vs 101.38; year 3 101.95 vs 97.32; year 4 99.41 vs 93.43; year 5 96.92 vs 89.69 MW.
- Teaching point: resource risk persists after completion and arrives as capex and lost output; wells come in whole units, so a small change in decline rate can trigger a step change in cost.

**Example 14.3 Operating leverage on a toll road** (`ex:14.3`, in 14.7.1)
- Scenario: a 31 km greenfield toll road in the US Sun Belt, opened 2019, full demand risk on the concessionaire.
- Inputs (first full year, USD million): forecast toll revenue 84.6; operating and maintenance costs 21.3 (treated as fixed); scheduled debt service 49.7.
- Compute: cash available for debt service at forecast 63.3; at revenue shortfalls of 10%, 20% and 30%: revenue 76.1, 67.7, 59.2; cash 54.8, 46.4, 37.9; change in cash −13.4%, −26.7%, −40.1%; surplus over debt service 5.1, (3.3), (11.8). Operating leverage multiplier (Equation 14.2: $\mathrm{OL} = \mathrm{Rev}/(\mathrm{Rev} - \mathrm{Opex})$) = 1.34. Breakeven revenue shortfall $1 - (\mathrm{DS} + \mathrm{Opex})/\mathrm{Rev}$ = 16.1%.
- Teaching point: fixed costs magnify demand risk; a forecast miss that would be ordinary for a traffic model (Sydney Cross City Tunnel missed by far more) consumes all headroom.

**Example 14.4 The coal price the bid assumed** (`ex:14.4`, in 14.8.2)
- Scenario: a 1,320 MW (2 × 660 MW) supercritical coal plant on the west coast of India burning imported coal, energy charge bid in 2007 with only 45% of the fuel cost escalating with a coal index; set in 2012, after a benchmark-pricing rule in the exporting country (Illustrative, modeled on the Mundra fact pattern but not Mundra's numbers; amounts converted to USD for the example).
- Inputs: load factor 80%; specific coal consumption 0.405 t/MWh; delivered coal price assumed in the bid USD 61.50/t; actual USD 97.20/t; the 45% escalable share is assumed to track the actual price.
- Compute: generation 9,250.6 GWh; coal 3,746,477 t; fuel cost at bid price USD 230.41 million; at actual price USD 364.16 million; increase recovered through the escalable share USD 60.19 million; increase borne by the project company USD 73.56 million a year.
- Teaching point: input price risk is borne by whoever the tariff formula leaves holding the non-escalable share; the classification fight (force majeure or not) decides whether anyone else picks it up.

**Example 14.5 Interest rates and inflation on an availability-payment rail PPP** (`ex:14.5`, in 14.12.1 and 14.12.2)
- Scenario: a 22 km availability-payment light-rail PPP procured by a US state transportation department, financial close 2020, senior bank debt USD 386.4 million floating on Term SOFR, 80% swapped to fixed; the availability payment escalates at a fixed 2.0% a year; operations and maintenance costs are indexed to CPI under the O&M subcontract.
- Part (a) inputs: unhedged share 20% (USD 77.28 million); reference rate rises 200 bps. Result: extra interest USD 1.546 million a year.
- Part (b) inputs: indexed opex base USD 14.85 million; CPI 6.5%, 5.2%, 3.9% in years 1 to 3 against the 2.0% escalation assumed in the payment. Results: year-3 opex actual USD 17.287 million against USD 15.759 million planned (gap USD 1.528 million); cumulative three-year gap USD 3.384 million (actual 15.815, 16.638, 17.287; planned 15.147, 15.450, 15.759).
- Teaching point: interest rate and inflation risks come from mismatch, not from rates themselves; a fixed-escalation revenue line and a CPI-linked cost line are a short position in inflation.

**Example 14.6 A mini-perm's maturity** (`ex:14.6`, in 14.12.3)
- Scenario: a 7-year bank mini-perm of USD 540.0 million on a contracted LNG regasification terminal in southern Europe, amortizing on a 20-year notional profile.
- Inputs: scheduled repayments in years 1 to 7 total USD 153.9 million; refinancing margin at maturity 150 bps above the original.
- Compute: balance due at maturity USD 386.1 million (71.5% of original); extra interest at the higher margin USD 5.79 million a year. Pose (no computation) the second leg: if lenders refinance only 80% of the balance, the sponsor must find USD 77.2 million (0.20 × 386.1) or the debt defaults at maturity.
- Teaching point: refinancing risk is the risk that the market will not repeat today's terms on a date fixed today; Chapter 36 shows how lenders structure around it.

**Example 14.7 A local-currency tariff and a dollar loan** (`ex:14.7`, in 14.13)
- Scenario: a 96 MW solar plant selling to a state single-buyer utility under a 20-year tariff fixed in local currency (LCY) and not indexed to the dollar; USD-denominated senior debt; set in 2019 (Illustrative; LCY and the rates are assumptions isolating the mechanism).
- Inputs: annual revenue LCY 9,450.0 million; local-currency opex LCY 1,260.0 million; USD opex (offshore spares and LTSA-type service) USD 2.15 million; annual USD debt service USD 11.40 million; exchange rate at financial close 525.0 LCY per USD.
- Part (a), at 525.0: revenue USD 18.000 million; local opex USD 2.400 million; cash available for debt service USD 13.450 million; cash divided by debt service 1.18 (ratio stated as "cash covers debt service 1.18 times", pointer to Chapter 35); surplus USD 2.05 million.
- Part (b), the local currency loses 40% of its value, so the rate moves to 875.0: revenue USD 10.800 million; local opex USD 1.440 million; cash USD 7.210 million; covers debt service 0.63 times; shortfall USD 4.19 million.
- Breakeven (Equation 14.3: $\mathrm{FX}^{\mathrm{BE}} = (\mathrm{Rev}^{\mathrm{LCY}} - \mathrm{Opex}^{\mathrm{LCY}})/(\mathrm{DS}^{\mathrm{USD}} + \mathrm{Opex}^{\mathrm{USD}})$) = 604.4 LCY per USD; a loss of only 13.1% of the currency's value wipes out the surplus.
- Part (c), convertibility and transfer: the rate stays at 525.0 but the central bank queues conversions for four months; USD 6.00 million of revenue sits trapped onshore when a semiannual debt service payment falls due. Liquidity, not solvency, is the problem (link to 14.12.4).
- Teaching point: devaluation, convertibility and transfer are three different risks with three different protections (Chapters 59 and 60).

**Walkthrough: building a risk register from a project description** (`sec:14.21`)

Artifact: a project description of about one page for an Illustrative project, then the reader builds the register step by step. Project: a 140 MW run-of-river hydropower plant in Nepal's middle hills (Illustrative; no real project named), 36-month construction including a 4.2 km headrace tunnel, a 25-year PPA in local currency with a 30% USD-indexed component, a 118 km transmission line to be built by the state utility, DFI and commercial bank debt in USD, an EPC consortium of an international contractor and a local civil works firm, resettlement of a small number of households.

Steps (the chapter writes each as prose with the partial register shown):
1. Read the description once for the money: where revenue comes from, in what currency, from whom; what the capex is and who builds it; what the debt is.
2. Walk the lifecycle (Framework 14.2) and list events phase by phase.
3. Walk the 25 categories (Exhibit 14.4 as the checklist) and add what step 2 missed; record "not material" explicitly with a reason rather than leaving gaps.
4. Write a risk card (Framework 14.1) for each material risk: trigger, transmission path, cash-flow line, phase, typical bearer.
5. Merge duplicates and split composites (for example, "tunnel" splits into geological cost overrun and delay; "currency" splits into devaluation on the 70% local share, convertibility and transfer).
6. Mark clusters (Section 14.20): devaluation, offtaker credit and political intervention; geology, delay and contractor credit.
7. Assign a register owner for each risk on the sponsor side and note open questions for diligence (forward reference to Chapter 48).
8. Stop: the register lists and describes. Sizing, allocation and pricing wait for Chapter 15.

## 14.K Practitioner's notebook

`\subsection*{Checklist}`: a 15 to 20 item first-pass risk identification checklist, one sentence each with its reason (for example: identify every party whose failure stops revenue and ask who replaces them and how fast; identify every currency in revenue, opex and debt; identify every interface where two contractors' scopes meet; ask which permits can still be challenged and until when).

`\subsection*{Red flags}`: revenue in one currency and debt in another with no indexation; a single offtaker whose own revenue is politically set; first-of-a-kind technology presented as "proven" because a pilot ran; a transmission line or road access built by someone with no contract with the project company; a fixed nominal price signed long before FID; a sponsor who is also the EPC contractor and O&M operator; a bullet maturity inside the asset's life; forecasts that exceed every competing forecast (Sydney tunnels); construction and demand risk both live at once (Eurotunnel).

`\subsection*{Rules of thumb and their limits}`: completion is the largest single risk transition (limit: resource and market risks persist, as Sarulla and Chile show); risk transferred becomes counterparty credit risk (limit: unless secured by cash, bonds or a creditworthy guarantor); an indexed tariff moves currency risk to the offtaker but not out of the project (limit: only as far as the offtaker can pay and convert); daily delay cost runs at roughly lost revenue plus interest plus fixed costs (limit: excludes knock-on contract penalties and reputation).

`\subsection*{Common mistakes}`: listing hazards instead of exposures; treating force majeure as a category rather than a relief mechanism; merging devaluation, convertibility and transfer into one "FX risk" line; ignoring liquidity because the project is solvent; omitting the risks of the counterparties who carry transferred risk; writing "not applicable" with no reason.

`\subsection*{Questions experts ask}`: "What has to happen for this project to miss a debt payment, and how many of those things share a cause?"; "If this counterparty fails, who replaces them, at what cost, and how long does it take?"; "Which of these risks would a court classify differently from the way the contract labels it?"; "What does the project look like on the day a protection is needed, and who is still standing?"

## 14.L Judgment drill

The situation. You are an associate in a bank's project finance team. Your director must present a first view to the credit committee in 48 hours on a 220 MW solar plant with a 110 MW / 440 MWh battery in a Southern African market (Illustrative): a 20-year PPA with a state utility rated three notches below the sovereign; 70% of the tariff indexed to the US dollar; the battery's revenue partly merchant from year 6; a 76 km, 132 kV line to be built by the utility; a battery chemistry new to that market from a supplier with two reference sites. The sponsor's register lists 31 risks scored on a heat map. Your director asks for "the five that decide whether we can lend, and why."

Reasoning it through (outline the writer expands to prose): rank by how each risk reaches debt service and whether anyone creditworthy stands behind it, not by the sponsor's scores. Expected expert top five: (1) offtaker credit plus convertibility as one cluster, since the 70% dollar indexation moves devaluation risk onto a utility that earns local currency (Section 14.13, 14.20); (2) interface risk on the utility-built line, with the question of whether deemed-energy payments come from the same weak utility (wrong-way, 14.20.2; Lake Turkana); (3) battery technology and supplier credit together; (4) merchant price risk on the battery from year 6 (cannibalization, Chapter 11 cross-reference); (5) regulatory and political risk on the 30% local-currency tariff share and tariff affordability. Explain why construction cost of solar, normally a top risk, ranks lower here (mature technology, lump-sum EPC available).

What would change the answer: a sovereign guarantee or DFI partial risk guarantee behind the utility (moves item 1 down, item 2 down if it covers deemed energy); the line already built and energized (item 2 drops out); a fully contracted battery toll (item 4 drops out); a tier-1 battery supplier with a parent guarantee and many reference sites (item 3 drops); a full-USD tariff (raises convertibility weight relative to devaluation).

## 15.B Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapter 14 gave the reader a list; Chapter 15 gives them a method. The reader learns to run the seven-step cycle (identify, analyze, quantify, allocate, mitigate, price, monitor) on a live project; to quantify a risk well enough to compare it with what transferring it costs; to build a risk matrix and see where heat maps mislead; to allocate each risk by asking who controls it, who can absorb its tail, who will charge least, and who will still pay in the bad state of the world; to draft pass-through and back-to-back provisions that actually match; to see what residual risk equity and debt are left holding and why the payoff shapes make lenders obsess over the downside and sponsors over the upside; to read the price each bearer charges for the risk it takes; and to say precisely what "bankable" means through the bankability ladder. The chapter ends on the gap between allocation on paper and in practice, with Carillion, Dabhol, Metronet and the Sydney tunnels as evidence.

What you will be able to do:

1. Quantify a project risk as an expected loss and a tail, build a risk and allocation matrix, and explain what the matrix hides (Capabilities 2 and 4).
2. Allocate each material risk to a bearer using the allocation test, and draft pass-through and back-to-back terms that do not leave a gap (Capabilities 3 and 6).
3. Explain what residual risk equity and lenders each hold, how each bearer prices risk, and place any risk on the bankability ladder (Capabilities 1, 5 and 14).
4. Discount a contractual protection for the chance that its provider will not pay when the loss occurs (Capability 15).

## 15.E Opening and close

## 15.F Worked examples

**Example 15.1 Sizing a cost-overrun risk from three estimates** (`ex:15.1`, in 15.3.1 and 15.3.2)
- Scenario: a 412 km, 500 kV transmission line and three substations built by a privately financed transmission concessionaire in Peru, base capex USD 486.3 million, procured under multiple contracts so the owner bears the overrun (Illustrative).
- Inputs: overrun scenarios from the estimator's three-point review converted to four discrete outcomes: 0% with probability 0.55; 8% with 0.30; 20% with 0.12; 35% with 0.03.
- Compute: expected overrun USD 28.45 million (5.85% of capex); outcome amounts 0.00, 38.90, 97.26 and 170.20; cumulative probability 0.55, 0.85, 0.97, 1.00; a contingency equal to the expected loss covers only the 55% no-overrun case; covering 85% needs USD 38.90 million; the 90th percentile is the 20% outcome, USD 97.26 million; the average overrun in the worst 15% of outcomes is USD 111.85 million; standard deviation USD 40.61 million.
- Teaching point: the expected loss is what a well-diversified bearer would charge; the tail decides whether a thin project company can carry it at all, which is why lenders ask for contingency plus a further layer (Chapter 16).


**Example 15.2 A risk matrix for a desalination plant, and what it hides** (`ex:15.2`, in 15.4.1 and 15.4.3)
- Scenario: a 250,000 m3/day seawater reverse-osmosis plant on the Red Sea coast, capex USD 368.7 million, 25-year water purchase agreement with a state water company, grid power supply contract with energy cost passed through only partially (Illustrative; no country named).
- Scales: likelihood 1 (below 2%), 2 (2% to below 10%), 3 (10% to below 30%), 4 (30% to below 60%), 5 (60% or more), each the probability of occurrence over the period stated per risk (construction for R2; the first ten operating years for the others; the writer states this horizon mix openly because it is itself one of the matrix's hidden flaws); impact 1 (below USD 1 million), 2 (1 to below 5), 3 (5 to below 20), 4 (20 to below 60), 5 (60 or more), measured as loss to project cash.
- Inputs and results (probability, loss USD m, L, I, score, EL USD m):
  - R1 membrane output below guarantee: 0.25, 6.4, 3, 3, 9, 1.600
  - R2 six-month construction delay: 0.20, 18.9, 3, 3, 9, 3.780
  - R3 algal bloom shutdowns: 0.45, 2.7, 4, 2, 8, 1.215
  - R4 offtaker pays more than 90 days late: 0.15, 11.2, 3, 3, 9, 1.680
  - R5 change in law on brine discharge requiring a longer outfall: 0.08, 46.5, 2, 4, 8, 3.720
  - R6 power cost pass-through gap: 0.35, 3.9, 4, 2, 8, 1.365
  - R7 major earthquake: 0.01, 240.0, 1, 5, 5, 2.400
  - R8 termination by the state without compensation: 0.015, 368.7, 1, 5, 5, 5.530
- Rankings: by score R2, R4, R1 (all 9), then R5, R6, R3 (8), then R8, R7 (5). By expected loss: R8 (5.53), R2 (3.78), R5 (3.72), R7 (2.40), R4 (1.68), R1 (1.60), R6 (1.37), R3 (1.22). Total expected loss USD 21.29 million.
- Teaching point: the heat map puts the two risks that could destroy the project in its lowest band; ordinal scores multiply bands, not money. Both rankings are needed: one for management attention, one for allocation and pricing.

**Example 15.3 Residual risk and the asymmetry of debt and equity** (`ex:15.3`, in 15.6.1 and 15.7.1)
- Scenario: a stylized single-period view of a contracted waste-to-energy project in the Netherlands at the end of its construction (Illustrative): project value at the test date V, senior debt claim D = USD 300.0 million.
- Base distribution: V = 240, 300, 360, 420 with probabilities 0.15, 0.35, 0.35, 0.15; mean 330.0. Debt value 291.00 (expected shortfall to lenders USD 9.00 million); equity value 39.00; sum 330.0.
- Mean-preserving spread (a riskier technology choice with the same expected value): V = 180, 290, 370, 480, same probabilities; mean 330.0. Debt value 278.50 (expected shortfall 21.50); equity value 51.50; sum 330.0.
- Result: the riskier choice moves USD 12.50 million of value from lenders to equity with no change in the project's expected value.
- Teaching point: this is why lenders resist risk-increasing decisions that sponsors like, and why sponsors will accept volatility lenders will not. State the simplification (one period, no time value, no costs of distress) and why the lesson survives.

**Example 15.4 Paying a contractor to take construction risk, or keeping it** (`ex:15.4`, in 15.8.2)
- Scenario: the Peru transmission project of Example 15.1, choosing between a lump-sum turnkey contract and the multi-contract approach.
- Inputs: lump-sum premium 9.2% over the cost-reimbursable estimate of USD 486.3 million; expected overrun if retained USD 28.45 million (Example 15.1); if retained, lenders require the sponsor to commit tail support equal to the P90 overrun (USD 97.26 million) for the three-year build, which the sponsor's rating agency and board treat as using capital: a charge of 8% a year on half the commitment.
- Compute: transfer price USD 44.74 million (1.57 times the expected loss; risk premium USD 16.29 million); retention cost = expected loss 28.45 + capital charge 97.26 × 0.5 × 0.08 × 3 = 11.67, total USD 40.12 million (Equation 15.5: $C^{\mathrm{retain}} = \mathrm{EL} + k \times S \times T$, with $k$ the annual capital charge rate, $S$ the support counted, $T$ years); retention is cheaper by USD 4.62 million.
- Teaching point: the comparison turns on who values the tail; a sponsor with a strong balance sheet may retain, one without cannot, and the lenders may still prefer the fixed price because it moves the tail to a party they can assess (bankability ladder). Note the result is sensitive to the capital charge; Exercise 15.10 repeats with other inputs.

**Example 15.5 A back-to-back that is not** (`ex:15.5`, in 15.5.3)
- Scenario: a 420,000 t/yr waste-to-energy plant in the north of England under a 25-year availability-based contract with a county council; the O&M operator's liability for availability deductions is capped (Illustrative; amounts in USD for comparison with the book's other examples, with a GBP equivalent at an assumed 1.27 USD per GBP stated once).
- Inputs: unitary charge USD 38.4 million a year (daily USD 105,205 on a 365-day year); a 45-day outage caused by the O&M operator's failure, deducted at 100%; O&M fee USD 7.6 million a year; O&M liability cap for deductions 25% of the annual fee, so USD 1.9 million a year.
- Compute: deductions USD 4.734 million; recovered from the O&M operator USD 1.900 million; retained by the project company USD 2.834 million. A second outage in the same year is borne in full.
- Teaching point: back-to-back means matched in trigger, quantum, timing and cap; a cap set as a share of the subcontract fee usually breaks the match. Clause 15.1 shows the drafting choices.
- GBP equivalents: compute and print once if the writer keeps the GBP setting (38.4 / 1.27 = GBP 30.2 million).

**Example 15.6 What a delay-LD promise is worth** (`ex:15.6`, in 15.10.1)
- Scenario: a 450 MW combined-cycle plant in Bangladesh (Illustrative) whose EPC contractor owes delay LDs of USD 165,000 a day; the scenario studied is a 210-day delay.
- Inputs: LDs due USD 34.65 million (cap of 20% of a USD 320 million price, USD 64 million, does not bind); probability the contractor is in financial distress given a delay this long 0.30 (wrong-way: long delays and contractor distress share causes); in distress the lenders collect only a 5% on-demand performance bond (USD 16.0 million); without a bond, recovery in distress is 25% of the claim.
- Compute (Equation 15.4: $\mathrm{E}[R] = \sum_s p_s \times R_s$): with the bond 0.70 × 34.65 + 0.30 × 16.00 = USD 29.05 million (83.9% of face); without the bond 0.70 × 34.65 + 0.30 × 0.25 × 34.65 = USD 26.85 million (77.5%).
- Teaching point: protections are worth their collectable value in the state of the world where they are needed, which is why lenders want on-demand bonds and creditworthy parent guarantees; forward reference to Chapter 22.

## 15.G Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: turning a risk register into an allocation matrix** (`sec:15.12`)

Artifact: the 140 MW Nepal run-of-river hydropower register from Chapter 14's walkthrough (Exhibit 14.6). The reader converts it into an allocation matrix (Exhibit 15.6, longtable, 12 to 15 rows).

Steps:
1. Filter: drop risks with negligible value effect after a quick sensitivity (Section 15.2.2); keep a "watch" list.
2. Quantify the top risks: expected loss and a tail figure for each quantifiable risk (tunnel geology overrun, delay, hydrology), stress cases for the rest (tariff de-indexation, transmission delay by the state utility).
3. For each risk run the allocation test (Framework 15.2) and write the result as one line: preferred bearer, why, and the question that could overturn it.
4. Name the instrument for each allocation (EPC clause, PPA deemed-energy clause, insurance, state guarantee), cross-referencing the Part IV chapter that owns it, without drafting it.
5. Check matching for every back-to-back and pass-through line (scope, trigger, timing, quantum, cap, procedure, law).
6. Write the residual column: what is left with the project company, capped or uncapped.
7. Place each risk on the bankability ladder (Framework 15.3) and mark the weakest rung.
8. Record the price: who charges for each allocation and roughly how (premium in EPC price, insurance premium, guarantee fee, equity return).
9. Write the monitoring indicator for each retained risk.

## 15.K Practitioner's notebook

`\subsection*{Checklist}`: allocation-matrix completion checklist (each risk has a bearer, an instrument, a cap, a residual, a ladder rung, an indicator); matching checks for every back-to-back (scope, trigger, notice, timing, quantum, cap, law, forum); collectability check for every bearer (rating, security, correlation with the loss).

`\subsection*{Red flags}`: a matrix where every risk ends green after mitigation; a back-to-back cap expressed as a share of the subcontract fee; a pass-through to an offtaker whose own tariff is politically frozen; a risk "allocated to the government" without a guarantee, budget line or payment mechanism; a sponsor case that treats contingency as profit; a contractor that holds an early unconditional termination right.

`\subsection*{Rules of thumb and their limits}`: the cheapest bearer is the one with control and diversification (limit: only if it can pay in the bad state); the project is as bankable as its weakest material rung (limit: lenders sometimes accept one lower rung with structural compensation); a risk premium above about twice expected loss is worth testing against retention (limit: tail size and the retaining party's capital, Example 15.4).

`\subsection*{Common mistakes}`: adding expected losses of correlated risks as if independent; using heat-map scores as money; confusing allocation (who pays) with mitigation (what reduces or absorbs it); ignoring how a party's exit right changes its incentives; assuming the state will absorb what it never signed for.

`\subsection*{Questions experts ask}`: "Who pays if this happens, and will they still be solvent that day?"; "What is the most this costs us, not the average?"; "Which two risks on this list are really one risk?"; "What is the weakest rung, and what does it cost to move it?"; "What does the government's budget say about the guarantee it gave?"

## 15.L Judgment drill

The situation. You are the lead arranger on a 150 MW wind farm in East Africa (Illustrative) with a 20-year PPA from a state utility rated B-, not guaranteed by the sovereign. The 220 kV line to the nearest substation is being built by the state transmission company with separate DFI funding; it is about 40% complete. The PPA says the utility pays deemed energy if the line is late. If the line is 18 months late, deemed energy is about USD 2.85 million a month, USD 51.3 million in total. The sponsor wants you to accept the interface risk as allocated and launch syndication next week. Your credit committee meets in four days.

Reasoning it through (outline): run the allocation test: control sits with the transmission company; capacity and collectability fail because the deemed-energy payer is the same weak utility whose finances would be strained in the scenario (wrong-way); Lake Turkana as precedent (allocation held but cost became a political and fiscal issue). Place the risk on the ladder: rung 2 at best as allocated. Options ranked by cost and effect: sovereign guarantee or DFI partial risk guarantee covering deemed energy (to rung 3); sponsor completion support that runs until line energization plus a debt-funded interest reserve covering a stated delay (to rung 3 or 4); sequencing construction start to line progress; a lower gearing and larger DSRA through the delay window. Expert answer: do not launch syndication on the current allocation; take the term sheet to committee with a condition for sovereign or DFI support of deemed-energy payments, or sponsor support until energization, and price the difference.

What would change the answer: the line is energized or within three months of completion with an EPC contractor of strong credit (risk drops to rung 4 or 5); the utility's payments are already supported by a sovereign guarantee that covers deemed energy (rung 3); the DFI funding the line also lends to the wind farm (the multilateral relationship creates an incentive, but not a legal obligation, so it moves the risk only modestly); the PPA lets the wind farm sell to other buyers through an alternative connection (volume risk instead of credit risk).

## 16.B Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapter 15 left every material risk with a named bearer and some risks with no bearer able to pay. Chapter 16 teaches the eight families of tools that make allocations stick and absorb what is left: contracts, insurance, guarantees (including payment security), reserves and liquidity, hedges, structural features, sponsor support and credit enhancement. For each family the reader learns what it does to a risk (reduce, transfer, or absorb), what it costs and who pays, and how it fails. Then the chapter teaches how the tools combine into a layered loss stack, how to choose among them with a selection grid and a cost-benefit test, and how mitigation fails through correlation, collectability and over-engineering. Instrument detail is owned elsewhere (insurance Chapter 27; reserves and hedging requirements Chapter 37; guarantees Chapters 17, 34 and 60; payment security Chapter 59; sponsor support Chapter 26); this chapter owns the overview, the selection logic and the comparative cost.

What you will be able to do:

1. Match each material risk to the tool families that can reduce, transfer or absorb it, and explain what each tool costs and who pays (Capabilities 2 and 5).
2. Arrange tools into a layered loss stack and trace which layer pays for a given loss (Capabilities 3 and 10).
3. Compare the annual cost of alternative mitigants and decide which to buy, including when a mitigant is not worth its price (Capabilities 5 and 15).
4. Write a mitigation plan for a risk matrix that a credit committee can approve (Capabilities 8 and 14).

## 16.F Worked examples

**Example 16.1 Three ways to cover three months of late payment** (`ex:16.1`, in 16.5.2)
- Scenario: a 340 MW open-cycle gas plant in South Asia, 2024, selling to a sub-investment-grade state utility; monthly invoice USD 9.35 million; lenders want three months of cover against late payment (USD 28.05 million) (Illustrative).
- Option A, standby LC procured by the utility from its bank and confirmed by an international bank: issuing fee 1.40% a year (paid by the utility and recovered in the tariff), confirmation fee 0.85% a year (paid by the project company). Annual cost USD 0.631 million in total (utility USD 0.393 million, project company USD 0.238 million).
- Option B, cash reserve funded at financial close at the project's blended funding cost: 75% debt at 7.1%, 25% equity at 15.0%, blended 9.075%; deposit rate 4.2%. Carrying cost (Equation 16.1) 28.05 × (0.09075 − 0.042) = USD 1.367 million a year.
- Option C, committed liquidity facility from a DFI: commitment fee 0.75% a year, USD 0.210 million undrawn, plus interest when drawn.
- Teaching point: the cheapest-looking option moves cost and credit exposure to another party (the utility pays most of the LC; the DFI facility adds debt when drawn); the cash reserve is dearest but needs no one else to perform. Forward reference to Chapter 59 for the instruments and Chapter 37 for reserves.

**Example 16.2 Choosing an insurance deductible for a battery plant** (`ex:16.2`, in 16.3.2)
- Scenario: a 300 MW / 1,200 MWh battery storage project in California, property damage and business interruption renewal in 2025 (Illustrative).
- Inputs: premium USD 3.42 million a year with a USD 1.0 million deductible, or USD 2.61 million with a USD 5.0 million deductible; expected frequency of losses between USD 1.0 million and USD 5.0 million 0.32 a year with an average size of USD 2.15 million; frequency of losses above USD 5.0 million 0.06 a year.
- Compute: premium saving USD 0.81 million; extra expected retained loss 0.32 × (2.15 − 1.0) + 0.06 × (5.0 − 1.0) = USD 0.608 million; net expected saving USD 0.202 million a year.
- Teaching point: the higher deductible wins on expectation but adds a USD 4.0 million retained hit in a bad year; whether the project can absorb it (liquidity, lenders' maximum deductible) decides. Moss Landing shows why the limit, not the deductible, is the bigger question for this asset class.

**Example 16.3 Tracing a construction overrun through the loss stack** (`ex:16.3`, in 16.10.2)
- Scenario: a solar-plus-storage project in Chile's Atacama region (Illustrative): 400 MW of PV and 200 MW / 800 MWh of storage, capex USD 618.4 million, EPC split into supply and installation contracts.
- Inputs: owner-risk overrun USD 42.6 million plus financing cost of delay USD 9.8 million, total funding need USD 52.4 million; contingency 5% of capex (USD 30.92 million); delay LDs received from the installation contractor USD 8.7 million; no DSU proceeds (no physical damage); standby facility and contingent equity drawn 75:25 for the remainder; committed standby USD 18.0 million and contingent equity USD 6.0 million.
- Compute: after contingency and LDs USD 12.78 million remains; standby drawn USD 9.585 million; contingent equity USD 3.195 million; committed layers not exhausted. **Example 16.4 What currency cover costs** (`ex:16.4`, in 16.6.2)
- Scenario: the 96 MW solar plant of Example 14.7 (LCY tariff, USD debt) asks what it would cost to lock today's exchange rate (Illustrative).
- Inputs: spot 525.0 LCY per USD; one-year LCY interest rate 14.50%; one-year USD rate 4.60%; flat curves assumed.
- Compute: one-year forward 574.69 (the forward price of a dollar is 9.46% above spot); ten-year forward on flat curves 1,296.9, 2.47 times spot.
- Teaching point: the forward is set by the interest differential, so cover "costs" roughly the expected depreciation; what the project buys is certainty, at a price that makes a dollar loan priced in LCY no cheaper than a local-currency loan; long-dated cover is rarely available at all, which is why Chapters 34 and 59 teach local-currency solutions. State simplification: covered interest parity on flat curves, no credit charge.

**Example 16.5 Whether a partial credit guarantee is worth its fee** (`ex:16.5`, in 16.11.2)
- Scenario: a USD 250.0 million 144A project bond for a container terminal concession in Côte d'Ivoire, 2026 (Illustrative).
- Inputs: spread without guarantee 4.85%; with a DFI partial credit guarantee covering 40% of principal (USD 100.0 million), spread 3.20%; guarantee fee 1.25% a year on the guaranteed amount; upfront fee 0.60%, amortized over a 10-year average life for the comparison; bond treated as non-amortizing for simplicity.
- Compute: annual spread saving USD 4.125 million; annual guarantee fee USD 1.250 million; upfront fee USD 0.600 million (USD 0.060 million a year); net annual benefit USD 2.815 million (Equation 16.3: $\mathrm{NB} = \Delta\mathrm{EL} + \Delta\mathrm{Fin} - C$, with no change in expected loss to the issuer here).
- Teaching point: the guarantee is worth buying when the market prices the covered risk above the guarantor's fee, which happens when the guarantor's preferred-creditor position or relationship with the host state lowers the risk it bears; forward reference to Chapter 34 for PCG structure.

## 16.G Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: writing a mitigation plan for a risk matrix** (`sec:16.13`)

Artifact: a mitigation plan for the 250,000 m3/day desalination plant of Example 15.2 (eight risks), presented as Exhibit 16.5 (table) plus the one-page plan text a credit committee would read.

Steps:
1. Start from the allocation matrix and its residual column; list only residuals.
2. For each residual, apply the selection grid (Framework 16.1): reduce if controllable, insure if insurable, guarantee if a creditworthy party will price it, reserve if frequent and moderate, hedge if market-traded, structure if it is a cash-flow volatility the debt can be shaped around, sponsor support if it is time-limited and sponsor-controllable, credit enhancement if it is a tail only the public or multilateral sector can hold.
3. Price each candidate tool (premium, fee, carrying cost, capital) on an annual basis.
4. Run the cost-benefit test (Framework 16.3) for each; drop tools whose cost exceeds benefit unless needed to lift the weakest rung.
5. Check common-mode failure: list each tool's provider and which providers share a risk with the loss or with each other (for R4 and R8, a sovereign).
6. Arrange the retained tools in a loss stack for each major risk; mark any gaps.
7. Re-place each risk on the bankability ladder after mitigation and state the project's weakest rung.
8. Write the plan: one paragraph per major risk (risk, tool, cost, provider, residual, rung), then a summary of total annual mitigation cost and what is deliberately left unmitigated, with the reason.

Indicative content for the eight risks (the writer develops it): R1 performance guarantees and LDs from the membrane supplier plus O&M incentives; R2 EPC delay LDs, DSU insurance, contingency; R3 intake design and pre-treatment (reduce), O&M reserve, deemed-availability relief in the water purchase agreement if negotiable; R4 LC and state guarantee of the water company; R5 change-in-law pass-through; R6 energy pass-through and a power hedge; R7 earthquake cover subject to market capacity; R8 termination compensation backed by a state guarantee and political risk insurance.

## 16.K Practitioner's notebook

`\subsection*{Checklist}`: for each mitigant record provider, rating or credit support, trigger, amount and limit, duration and fall-away conditions, cost and who pays it, documentation home, and the losses it shares a cause with.

`\subsection*{Red flags}`: three layers of support that all depend on the same sovereign; a hedge notional above firm production or above the debt balance it hedges; insurance limits set at replacement cost for an asset whose clean-up and liability costs scale differently; a reserve funded with debt from the same lenders it protects with no step-down; completion support that falls away on a date rather than on a test; a guarantee whose call procedure takes longer than the DSRA lasts.

`\subsection*{Rules of thumb and their limits}`: insure the rare and large, reserve the frequent and moderate, contract the controllable, hedge the tradable (limit: market capacity and basis); an LC is cheaper than cash but only as good as its issuer and replenishment (limit: wrong-way issuers); every mitigant should pass the cost-benefit test unless it lifts the weakest rung.

`\subsection*{Common mistakes}`: counting a mitigant twice (the same sovereign behind the LC and the guarantee); hedging the forecast rather than the firm exposure; treating sponsor support as free because it is contingent; forgetting the carrying cost of reserves; buying insurance below the lenders' required limits and discovering it at the first claim.

`\subsection*{Questions experts ask}`: "Who stands behind this, and what happens to them in the scenario where we need it?"; "What does this cost a year, and who pays?"; "When does this protection fall away, and is the risk gone by then?"; "If we drop this tool, which rung do we fall to?"; "What is not covered by anything, and is that a choice?"

## 16.L Judgment drill

The situation. You are the CFO of the sponsor of a 410 MW solar-plus-storage IPP in a sub-investment-grade emerging market (Illustrative). Senior debt is USD 520.0 million with annual debt service of USD 61.4 million. After a credit committee review, the lenders ask for a twelve-month DSRA instead of six because of offtaker payment risk. The extra six months is USD 30.7 million. Your options: fund it in cash at close (blended funding cost 9.1%, deposit rate 4.0%: carrying cost USD 1.566 million a year); provide a sponsor LC from your relationship banks (fee 1.8%: USD 0.553 million a year, using your corporate credit lines); or buy political risk insurance on 90% of the debt at 1.05% (USD 4.914 million a year). You must reply before the lenders' syndication meeting on Friday.

Reasoning it through (outline): identify the risk the lenders are pricing (commercial late payment by the offtaker, not political events), so PRI misses the target unless the offtaker is state-owned and the cover includes non-honoring, and even then the waiting period is longer than the liquidity gap; compare cash and LC costs and credit-line usage; propose the LC with a step-down to six months after a stated number of consecutive on-time payments, or ask the lenders to accept offtaker-side payment security (a larger SEKA-style LC or a DFI liquidity guarantee) instead, which costs the project less and addresses the risk at its source. Expert answer: offer the sponsor LC with a step-down, conditioned on the offtaker LC being sized to at least two months, and decline PRI for this purpose.

What would change the answer: the sponsor's credit lines are scarce or its rating is under pressure (cash becomes preferable); a DFI offers a liquidity PRG at a subsidized fee (cheapest at source); the offtaker is a state entity covered by a sovereign guarantee with fast payment terms (lenders may accept six months); deposit rates rise close to funding cost (cash carrying cost falls).
