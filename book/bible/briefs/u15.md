## 69.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Thermal plants are the asset class project finance learned on, and the one that has failed most instructively: Dabhol, Paiton and Mundra all failed with the plant running perfectly. This chapter teaches the reader to look at a gas, coal or oil-fired plant the way a credit committee does in 2026: first by asking what role the plant plays in its system (baseload, mid-merit, peaker, firming capacity for renewables), because the role decides how it earns money; then by following the fuel from wellhead or mine to burner tip and asking who bears price, volume and deliverability at each link; then by matching revenue model, contract set and debt terms to that role. It closes with the transition question every thermal deal now faces: how a lender gets repaid by a plant whose dispatch, fuel and permits may shrink before the loan matures.

What you will be able to do (printed under `\section*{What you will be able to do}`):
- Screen a thermal power project by its system role and fuel chain, and name the two or three risks that decide bankability (Capability 2, Capability 13).
- Test a thermal plant's contract set for gaps between dispatch, fuel take-or-pay and tariff pass-through (Capability 3).
- Size the debt capacity of a contracted versus a merchant gas plant and explain the difference to a credit committee (Capability 5, Capability 14).
- Judge how energy-transition policy, fuel-supply risk and ECA rules limit tenor and structure for a new thermal plant (Capability 15).

## 69.5 Opening

A Case P scene is not used here; the opening is the Paiton I moment from `paiton`. In 1999 both 615 MW units of Paiton I reached commercial operation in East Java into a market whose currency and demand had collapsed, and PLN, the state utility that had signed for their capacity, dispatched them to zero and then went to court in Jakarta to annul the contract. Two paragraphs set the paradox: a technically excellent plant, contracted on capacity, earning nothing because the buyer could not pay and did not need the power. ## 69.6 Worked examples

**Example 69.1. Screening four technologies for a coastal single-buyer system** (`ex:69.1`, Illustrative; Section 69.1.2). A planner in a coastal single-buyer system compares technologies at 2015 price levels, 10.0% real discount rate. Inputs:

| Technology | Capex (USD/kW) | Fixed O&M (USD/kW-year) | Heat rate (kJ/kWh LHV) | Fuel | Variable O&M (USD/MWh) | Life (years) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| OCGT | 720 | 14.6 | 10,450 | Gas USD 6.40/MMBtu GCV | 4.1 | 25 |
| CCGT | 1,085 | 23.8 | 6,350 | Gas USD 6.40/MMBtu GCV | 3.6 | 25 |
| Coal (imported) | 2,140 | 52.3 | 9,300 | Coal USD 86.5/t at 25.1 GJ/t NCV | 4.7 | 30 |
| HFO engines | 980 | 31.2 | 8,450 | HFO USD 412/t at 40.2 GJ/t NCV | 9.8 | 20 |

Gas fuel cost per MWh uses the Case P convention: HR × 1.108 × price / 1,055.056. Solid and liquid fuel: HR / (NCV × 1,000) × price per tonne. Annualized fixed cost = capex × CRF(10%, life) + fixed O&M; CRF(10%, 25) = 0.11017; CRF(10%, 30) = 0.10608; CRF(10%, 20) = 0.11746.

Results: annualized fixed cost (USD/kW-year) OCGT 93.92, CCGT 143.33, coal 279.31, HFO 146.31. Fuel cost (USD/MWh) OCGT 70.24, CCGT 42.68, coal 32.05, HFO 86.60; total variable cost OCGT 74.34, CCGT 46.28, coal 36.75, HFO 96.40. Cost per MWh at capacity factors 10%, 30%, 50%, 80%: OCGT 181.55, 110.07, 95.78, 87.74; CCGT 209.90, 100.82, 79.00, 66.73; coal 355.60, 143.03, 100.52, 76.61; HFO 263.42, 152.08, 129.81, 117.28. OCGT is cheapest below 1,761 hours a year (capacity factor 20.1%); the CCGT is cheapest above that; coal never beats the CCGT at these prices (crossover would need 14,269 hours); HFO engines are dominated. Sensitivity: at gas USD 9.80/MMBtu, CCGT variable cost is 68.95 USD/MWh and coal beats the CCGT above 4,223 hours (capacity factor 48.2%). Lesson: the technology choice is a bet on the fuel price and the capacity factor; the screening curve shows where the bet flips. Equation 69.1: $c(\mathrm{CF}) = \frac{F \times 1{,}000}{8{,}760 \times \mathrm{CF}} + v$, where $F$ is annualized fixed cost per kW-year and $v$ variable cost per MWh; Excel: `=F_annual*1000/(8760*CF)+v`.

**Example 69.2. Merchant gross margin of a CCGT in an energy-only market** (`ex:69.2`, Illustrative; Section 69.3.3). A 620 MW CCGT in an energy-only market with scarcity pricing; heat rate 6,450 kJ/kWh LHV; gas USD 3.45/MMBtu GCV; variable O&M USD 3.20/MWh; availability 92.5%; fixed O&M USD 26.40/kW-year; capex USD 1,085/kW. Price duration simplified to four blocks: 120 hours at USD 845.00/MWh; 1,460 hours at 52.80; 3,880 hours at 33.10; 3,300 hours at 19.60 (8,760 hours). SRMC = 6,450 × 1.108 × 3.45 / 1,055.056 + 3.20 = USD 26.57/MWh. The plant runs in the top three blocks: generation 3,131.3 GWh (capacity factor 57.7%); gross margin by block USD 56.32 million, 21.96 million, 14.53 million, nil; total USD 92.82 million (USD 149.71/kW-year); fixed O&M USD 16.37 million; margin after fixed costs USD 76.45 million. Scarcity hours (1.4% of the year) produce 60.7% of gross margin. If the 120 scarcity hours cleared at USD 52.80 instead (a price cap or a capacity mechanism that suppresses scarcity), gross margin falls to USD 38.30 million (USD 61.78/kW-year) and margin after fixed costs to USD 21.93 million. Debt capacity: on a 12-year annuity at 7.25% sized at 2.00x (an illustrative merchant ratio; the chapter states that US banks quoted merchant gas pricing but no merchant gas DSCR in the fact sheets), debt is USD 299.6 million with scarcity (44.5% of the USD 672.7 million capex) and USD 86.0 million without (12.8%). Equation 69.2: spark spread $= p - \mathrm{HR} \times 1.108 \times p^{\mathrm{fuel}}/1{,}055.06$; clean spark spread subtracts carbon cost $e \times p^{\mathrm{CO_2}}$, with $e$ = 0.3618 tCO₂/MWh for this plant (6.45 GJ/MWh × 56.1 kg/GJ). The carbon case is left to Exercise 69.6.

**Example 69.3. Heat-rate headroom under a fuel pass-through** (`ex:69.3`, Illustrative; Section 69.4.2). A 450 MW CCGT sells 2,890 GWh a year; the PPA pays fuel at a contracted heat rate of 6,520 kJ/kWh LHV; gas costs USD 7.85/MMBtu GCV; HHV/LHV 1.108. Fuel paid by the offtaker: 2,890,000 MWh × 1,000 kWh/MWh × 6,520 × 1.108 / 1,055,056 = 19.7884 million MMBtu. In a year when actual heat rate is 6,410 kJ/kWh (new and clean), the plant burns 19.4545 million MMBtu (fuel bill USD 152.72 million) and keeps a gain of USD 2.62 million. In the year before a major inspection, at 6,575 kJ/kWh, it burns 19.9553 million MMBtu (USD 156.65 million) and loses USD 1.31 million. Lesson: heat-rate headroom is a profit line for equity and a volatility line for CFADS; lenders size on the downside heat rate and the offtaker's auditor tests the contracted value.

**Example 69.4. Take-or-pay exposure when dispatch falls** (`ex:69.4`, Illustrative; Section 69.4.1). A 400 MW CCGT, heat rate 6,480 kJ/kWh LHV, burns 65,330 MMBtu a day at full load (400 × 24 × 1,000 × 6,480 × 1.108 / 1,055,056). GSA: DCQ 58,300 MMBtu a day (89.2% of full-load need), ACQ = DCQ × 365 = 21,279,500 MMBtu, take-or-pay 85% of ACQ = 18,087,575 MMBtu, gas USD 6.95/MMBtu. At 72% energy dispatch (no part-load penalty assumed) the plant burns 17,168,638 MMBtu: shortfall 918,937 MMBtu, take-or-pay payment USD 6.39 million. At 48% dispatch with a 4.6% part-load penalty it burns 11,972,264 MMBtu: shortfall 6,115,311 MMBtu, payment USD 42.50 million. The writer then shows the PPA question: if the offtaker caused the low dispatch, does the PPA pass the take-or-pay payment through (Case P does: "dispatch-caused GSA take-or-pay amounts" are a pass-through, Case Bible 1.4)? If not, the USD 42.50 million falls on CFADS. Make-up gas (recoverable later) is mentioned as partial relief with its own risk.

**Example 69.5. What starts cost a cycling plant** (`ex:69.5`, Illustrative; Section 69.2.2). One F-class gas turbine under an LTSA with a variable fee of USD 518 per EOH and 10 EOH per start (Chapter 24 convention). Baseload year: 7,900 operating hours, 25 starts, EOH 8,150, LTSA variable cost USD 4.222 million, output 3,175,800 MWh at an average 402.0 MW when running, USD 1.33/MWh; starts are 3.1% of EOH. Cycling year: 4,100 hours, 260 starts, EOH 6,700, cost USD 3.471 million, output 1,521,100 MWh at 371.0 MW average, USD 2.28/MWh; starts are 38.8% of EOH. Lesson: as renewables push a CCGT into cycling duty, maintenance cost per MWh rises by about 71% while total revenue falls; models that hold LTSA cost per MWh constant overstate CFADS.

## 69.8 Frameworks

**Framework 69.1: Fuel-chain trace** (`fw:fuel-chain-trace`; original). Purpose: find the link in a thermal plant's fuel chain where price, volume, quality or deliverability risk is not matched by a contract. Steps: (1) Draw the chain from source to burner tip (field or mine; processing; pipeline, rail, ship or LNG terminal; plant storage). (2) For each link, name the contract and counterparty. (3) For each link, record who bears price change, volume shortfall, quality deviation and physical interruption. (4) Compare each with the PPA: does the PPA pass it through, deem availability, or leave it with the project company? (5) Test each counterparty's incentive to perform when the market moves against it (Mundra) and the law that governs its obligation. (6) Mark every unmatched cell; size it with the cash-flow impact of a 12-month interruption or a 30% price move; decide on mitigation (stock, dual-fuel, alternative supply, sponsor support, reserve). Demonstrated on Example 69.4 and in the judgment drill. One sentence on when to use it: any thermal deal at screening and again at the GSA term sheet.

Established tool used (credited as standard power-system planning practice, not a named author): the screening curve (Section 69.1.2). Chapter 15's bankability ladder and Chapter 28's gap scan are applied, not re-taught.

## 69.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (itemize, each with its reason): system role and dispatch study from an independent source; screening-curve position under low and high fuel prices; fuel-chain trace completed (Framework 69.1); GSA take-or-pay against downside dispatch, year by year; pass-through of dispatch-caused take-or-pay confirmed in the PPA; contracted versus guaranteed heat rate and degradation allowance; part-load curve in the PPA; deemed availability for fuel-supplier failure; LTSA fee basis (EOH, starts) and its cap; water, emissions and permit renewals over the loan life; ECA eligibility under the Arrangement as revised; transition stress (dispatch decline and carbon cost) in the downside case.

Red flags: dispatch assumption taken from the sponsor's own model; a PPA sized for baseload in a system whose demand forecast depends on one industrial customer; unindexed fuel components (Mundra); a fuel supplier whose home law can override its contract; a GSA longer than the reserves certification supports; a tariff denominated in a currency the buyer does not earn (cross-reference Chapter 59); LTSA priced on hours while the plant will cycle; scarcity hours carrying most of a merchant plant's margin.

Rules of thumb and their limits: "capacity payment covers debt service" holds only while the buyer can pay; "fuel is a pass-through" holds only to the contracted heat rate and only for the fuel the PPA names; "CCGT beats coal" depends on gas price (Example 69.1 crossover at USD 9.80/MMBtu); "merchant gas debt is half of contracted" is a market observation from 2024 US pricing, not a rule.

Common mistakes: modeling LTSA cost per MWh; ignoring the part-load penalty at low dispatch; treating make-up gas as free; assuming deemed availability covers a supplier failure that is the supplier's force majeure; sizing merchant debt on average prices instead of price-duration blocks.

Questions experts ask: Who dispatches this plant, and what happens to our capacity payment if they stop? Where does the gas come from in year 15? What does the offtaker pay for if the gas supplier fails? What heat rate does the PPA pay for at 50% load? How many starts a year does the LTSA price assume? Which lenders in the group have thermal exclusion policies that limit their hold or tenor?

## 69.12 Judgment drill

The situation (second person, numbers, deadline). You are the credit officer for an arranging bank on a 520 MW CCGT IPP in a single-buyer market. The PPA runs 20 years; the sponsor asks for 18-year amortizing debt at 75% gearing. The domestic gas field's certified reserves cover 14 years of the GSA's DCQ at the plant's base dispatch; the GSA allows the seller to reduce DCQ pro rata if the field underperforms. An ECA covers the turbine package; under the Arrangement as revised, a non-CCSU power plant gets at most 12 years of ECA support. Credit committee meets Thursday.

Reasoning it through (prose outline): the fuel-chain trace shows the binding risk is fuel volume after year 14, not the plant or the buyer; reserves tail and debt tenor must be aligned (debt fully repaid with a buffer before the reserves run out, or a backstop fuel route contracted); the ECA tranche is capped at 12 years, so an 18-year profile needs DFI or commercial tenor behind it; deemed availability for fuel shortfall shifts the risk to the offtaker and its guarantor, which is acceptable only if the sovereign support covers it; the answer an expert gives: offer a debt profile maturing no later than year 13 to 14 of operations unless (a) the PPA deems availability for gas shortfall backed by the government guarantee, or (b) an LNG import or alternative supply route is contracted; size at the shorter tenor and show the sponsor the tariff consequence.

What would change the answer: a reserves recertification raising 2P to cover 20 years (tenor can lengthen); a sovereign guarantee covering deemed capacity payments for gas shortfall (risk moves to the state); dual-fuel capability with on-site storage and a liquid-fuel supply contract (partial mitigation, at a heat-rate and cost penalty); a buyer whose credit is weak (shorter tenor regardless).

## 70.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Onshore wind and utility-scale solar are now the largest single source of project finance deal flow, and the most standardized; the risk has moved from the technology, which works, to the price the energy earns where and when it is produced. This chapter teaches the reader to follow a megawatt-hour from the resource to the bank account (gross yield, losses, curtailment, the node, the hour, the capture price, the hedge or contract), to read the contract set that wraps that chain, to know the financing terms the market applied in 2023 to 2026 by revenue quality, and to recognize the failures (Chile's decoupled nodes, Lake Turkana's missing line, Uri's frozen turbines, Ivanpah's under-performing towers) as failures of a specific link in that chain.

What you will be able to do:
- Trace a wind or solar project's revenue from resource to cash and identify the link that decides bankability (Capability 2, Capability 13).

## 70.6 Worked examples

**Example 70.1. Capture decline on a merchant solar plant** (`ex:70.1`, Illustrative; Section 70.3.3). A 180 MWac solar plant produces 412.6 GWh a year at P50 (capacity factor 26.2%), loses 3.5% to curtailment, and sells at the hub. Hub average price USD 46.30/MWh, held flat in real terms. Capture ratio 0.88 in year 1, falling 1.8 points a year to a floor of 0.64. Year 1: capture price USD 40.74/MWh, revenue USD 16.22 million. Year 5: ratio 0.808, USD 37.41/MWh, USD 14.90 million. Year 10: 0.718, USD 33.24/MWh, USD 13.24 million. Year 14: 0.646, USD 29.91/MWh, USD 11.91 million. The floor binds from year 15 (0.64; USD 29.63/MWh; USD 11.80 million). Cumulative revenue years 1 to 15 is USD 208.7 million against USD 243.3 million if capture stayed at 0.88. Lesson: a falling capture ratio is a revenue decline with the hub price unchanged; lenders size on it, not on the hub.

**Example 70.2. One cash flow, two debt amounts** (`ex:70.2`, Illustrative; Section 70.6.1). A solar project's CFADS is USD 21.74 million a year for 18 years; all-in debt cost 6.35%; annuity factor 10.5487. Sized at 1.30x as fully contracted: annual debt service USD 16.723 million, debt USD 176.4 million. Sized at 1.75x as merchant: debt service USD 12.423 million, debt USD 131.0 million. Difference USD 45.4 million, about a quarter of the contracted debt. At 1.40x (a contracted wind ratio): USD 163.8 million. The writer ties the ratios to the dated US ladder (verified) and says plainly that a real deal sizes each revenue bucket at its own ratio (cross-reference Chapter 36 and Case R's bucket sizing in Chapter 31).

**Example 70.3. A fixed-price contract at the wrong node** (`ex:70.3`, Illustrative; Section 70.4.2). A 120 MW solar plant has a contract to deliver 260 GWh a year in set daytime hours at USD 61.40/MWh, settled at the load node in the center. It generates 296.8 GWh, of which 19% is curtailed; the injection node averages USD 4.20/MWh in solar hours; the withdrawal node averages USD 88.70/MWh in the contract hours. Contract revenue USD 15.96 million; cost of buying the contract volume at the withdrawal node USD 23.06 million; spot sales of uncurtailed output USD 1.01 million; net revenue negative USD 6.09 million. In the design case (both nodes at USD 48.10/MWh, no curtailment) the same plant earned USD 17.73 million. The break-even withdrawal price, with everything else unchanged, is USD 65.28/MWh. Lesson: shape, basis and volume are three separate exposures; a fixed price removes none of them.

**Example 70.4. Choosing the inverter loading ratio** (`ex:70.4`, Illustrative; Section 70.2.2). A 180 MWac plant compares an ILR of 1.20 (216 MWdc) with 1.35 (243 MWdc). Specific yield before clipping 1,840 kWh/kWdc a year; clipping loss 0.8% at 1.20 and 3.1% at 1.35; incremental DC cost USD 410/kWdc. Energy: 394.26 GWh at 1.20; 433.26 GWh at 1.35; increment 39.0 GWh a year. Incremental capex 27 MWdc × USD 410/kW = USD 11.07 million; capital recovery factor at 7.4% over 30 years 0.08385; annualized USD 0.928 million; cost of incremental energy USD 23.80/MWh. Lesson: the extra DC buys shoulder-hour energy below the plant's capture price in Example 70.1 even at the floor, so a higher ILR raises CFADS; the IE checks clipping estimates and inverter warranties.

**Example 70.5. What the investment tax credit is worth to a solar project in 2026** (`ex:70.5`, Illustrative, US law as of October 3, 2026; Section 70.4.4). A 200 MWac solar project costs USD 1,146/kWac (USD 229.2 million); 94% of cost is eligible basis; credit rate 30% (no adders assumed); credit USD 64.63 million; sold under the transferability rules at USD 0.925 per USD 1 of credit, proceeds USD 59.79 million. Spread over 30 years at 7.4% the proceeds equal USD 5.013 million a year, or USD 11.42/MWh on 438.9 GWh a year. The writer states that wind and solar credits now require placing in service by December 31, 2027 unless construction began by July 4, 2026 (`t-us-tax-credits`), that the PFE material-assistance rules apply to construction beginning after December 31, 2025, and that the reader must check the current statute and IRS notices (26 U.S.C. §§48E, 6418; Notices 2025-42 and 2026-15). The price per dollar of credit (0.925) is the Case R input also used for R7 and R8 (Case Bible 3.6); the writer says it is an illustrative price, consistent with the 2024 market price points quoted in `t-us-tax-credits` (ITC about 92.5 cents).

## 70.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: building the resource-to-revenue bridge for a solar plant** (`sec:70.9`). The artifact is Exhibit 70.5, an Illustrative one-page bridge table for a 150 MWac plant, built step by step with Framework 70.1:
1. Start from the IE's P50 net yield at the meter (GWh), stating the loss assumptions already inside it (soiling, mismatch, inverter, transformer, availability).
2. Subtract curtailment by cause (economic, grid, regulatory), each with its source and whether any contract compensates it.
3. Split the remaining energy by hour-block (morning shoulder, midday, evening shoulder) from the yield model's 8,760 profile.
4. Price each block at the node, not the hub: hub price times hub-to-node basis.
5. Apply contract terms: which blocks a PPA or hedge covers, at what price, settled where.
6. Compute captured revenue by block and the plant's capture ratio; compare with the market advisor's.
7. Run the low case (capture decline 1.5 times faster) and a curtailment stress (+3 points).
8. Mark every line by who bears it (project, offtaker, hedge counterparty) and which revenue bucket it belongs to for sizing.
9. Write the credit-paper sentence: the link with the largest downside per MWh.

## 70.8 Frameworks

**Framework 70.1: Resource-to-revenue bridge** (`fw:resource-revenue-bridge`; original). Purpose: turn a wind or solar yield estimate into revenue with every loss and price adjustment visible and owned. Steps: (1) gross resource; (2) technical losses and availability; (3) curtailment by cause; (4) delivery profile by hour or block; (5) node price and basis; (6) contract and hedge overlay; (7) captured revenue; (8) risk owner and sizing bucket for each line. When to use: screening, IE and market report review, and lender sizing of any variable renewable asset. Demonstrated in the walkthrough and Examples 70.1 and 70.3.

Applied, not re-taught: Chapter 15 bankability ladder; Chapter 28 gap scan.

## 70.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: IE yield report with loss stack and P-values; market advisor capture-price forecast by node and hour-block, base and low; curtailment history and forecast by cause; interconnection agreement, network upgrade obligations and evacuation completion; turbine or module supplier credit and warranty backing; availability guarantee basis (time or energy) and LD rate; cold-weather package (wind) and hail or wind-stow design (solar trackers) as stated by the IE; tax-credit qualification evidence (beginning-of-construction, PFE diligence) for US projects; hedge settlement node and volume definition; land rights for the full debt life plus decommissioning.

Red flags: capture forecast stated at the hub only; a fixed-price contract settled at a node different from injection; curtailment assumed equal to history in a market adding capacity faster than transmission; evacuation built by a third party with no deemed-energy cover (Lake Turkana); first-of-a-kind technology sized on P50 (Ivanpah); a bid tariff below the recent auction trend with no explanation (Chile, REIPPPP late rounds); a hedge with fixed hourly volume on a wind farm without force-majeure or volume relief (Uri); policy support that is statutory rather than contractual (Spain).

Rules of thumb and their limits: the US DSCR ladder (contracted solar 1.25x to 1.30x, wind 1.35x to 1.40x, merchant 1.75x to 2.0x) is a dated US bank view, not a global rule; capture ratios fall with penetration, but storage and transmission can reverse the trend locally (Chile's batteries); a higher ILR raises energy cheaply until clipping and inverter warranties bite.

Common mistakes: modeling curtailment as a fixed percentage across the debt life; applying one-year P90 to a portfolio without correlation (Chapter 9); treating tax-credit proceeds as project revenue available to senior lenders when tax equity ranks ahead; ignoring the cost of hedge collateral or LCs (Case R's R3 USD 12.0 million LC).

Questions experts ask: At which node is this settled, and what was the basis last year? What share of curtailment is compensated? Who builds the line, and what happens to our revenue if it is late? What does the OEM guarantee, on what basis, and who stands behind it? If the tax credit failed, what would equity lose and what would lenders lose?

## 70.12 Judgment drill

The situation. You are the lead arranger on a 300 MWac solar plant in a northern desert region of a market with nodal pricing and fast-growing solar. The sponsor won a 20-year auction contract at a record-low price, settled at the load node 900 km south; it asks for 75% gearing sized at 1.30x on P50 with no basis hedge and no storage. The market advisor's base case shows injection-node prices near zero for 40% of solar hours by year 5 and curtailment rising from 4% to 15%. The term sheet goes to the sponsor tomorrow.

Reasoning it through: apply Framework 70.1; the contract fixes price but leaves basis and volume with the project (Example 70.3 mechanism); the record-low bid embeds an assumption that basis stays low; the expert answer: do not size the contracted bucket at a contracted ratio until basis is covered; either (a) require co-located storage or a financial transmission right or basis hedge for the contract volume, or (b) size the basis-exposed revenue at merchant ratios and cap gearing well below the request; add a curtailment stress, a liquidity reserve for negative settlement, and a covenant tied to the commissioning date of the planned transmission line. Show the sponsor the debt amount under each.

What would change the answer: a firm, financed transmission line with a delivery date before COD (basis risk falls); a contract settled at the injection node (basis moves to the buyer); storage sized to shift the contract volume (shape and some basis covered); a creditworthy buyer willing to take curtailment risk (volume risk moves).

## 71.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Offshore wind turned the risk profile of onshore wind inside out: revenue is usually firm, because governments award long CfDs or certificate contracts, while construction becomes a marine campaign worth billions, split across a handful of contractors and a few dozen specialized vessels, and exposed to weather, supply chains and serial defects in ever-larger turbines. This chapter teaches how offshore wind projects are built, contracted and financed, how lenders protect themselves through construction (contingency, interfaces, insurance, ECA support, phase ring-fencing), and why the most expensive failure of the 2020s, Ocean Wind, happened before any debt was signed: a fixed revenue price locked years before costs and interest rates were.

What you will be able to do:
- Explain what drives offshore wind economics and construction risk, and read an offshore wind contract package for interfaces and gaps (Capability 3, Capability 13).
- Structure the debt for an offshore wind phase, including two-part gearing for generation and transmission assets and ECA participation (Capability 5, Capability 8).
- Measure a developer's pre-FID exposure between revenue award and financial close, and decide what to lock and when (Capability 12, Capability 15).

## 71.6 Worked examples

**Example 71.1. Two-part gearing for a 1.2 GW phase** (`ex:71.1`, Illustrative, modeled on the structure described in `dogger-bank`; Section 71.7.1). Generation assets cost GBP 2,684.3 million; transmission assets GBP 561.8 million; the forecast regulated transfer value of the transmission assets is GBP 598.4 million. Generation debt at 67.5% gearing: GBP 1,811.9 million. Transmission bridge debt at 90% of forecast transfer proceeds: GBP 538.6 million. Total debt GBP 2,350.5 million against total capex GBP 3,246.1 million: blended gearing 72.4%; equity GBP 895.6 million. The 90% advance leaves a 10% cushion: a transfer value 12% below forecast (GBP 526.6 million) leaves the transmission debt GBP 12.0 million short. At 70.0% generation gearing (Exercise 71.6): total debt GBP 2,417.6 million, blended 74.5%. Lesson: the headline gearing of a phase mixes two credits; read each separately. (State GBP as the currency code per style sheet.)

**Example 71.2. A fixed price meets cost inflation and higher rates** (`ex:71.2`, Illustrative, modeled on the mechanism in `ocean-wind`; Section 71.5.2). A 1,000 MW project wins a 25-year contract at USD 96.40/MWh in year 1 escalating 2.0% a year; net capacity factor 44.5% (3,898.2 GWh a year); opex USD 112/kW-year escalating 2.5%; capex spent evenly over three years. At bid capex of USD 3,950/kW, the unlevered pre-tax project IRR is 5.56%. If capex rises to USD 5,180/kW before FID (31% higher), the IRR falls to 3.33%. Restoring 5.56% needs a first-year price of USD 117.01/MWh (21.4% higher). If financing cost moves the required return up by 2.0 percentage points as well (a stylized proxy for a 300 bps rise in long rates on a leveraged structure; the writer states the simplification), the price needed is USD 138.42/MWh (43.6% higher). Lesson: the developer was short inflation and rates from award to FID; the contract had no indexation for either. (Ignore tax credits; state that the ITC would raise the IRR but not change the mechanism.)

**Example 71.3. The cost of a lost installation season** (`ex:71.3`, Illustrative; Section 71.4.1). A 950 MW phase expects 3,478.6 GWh a year (net capacity factor 41.8%) under a CfD at GBP 58.20/MWh. A late vessel pushes turbine installation past the weather window and full generation slips seven months. Lost revenue GBP 118.1 million; additional interest during construction on GBP 2,930 million of drawn debt at 6.4% for seven months GBP 109.4 million; total GBP 227.5 million before vessel standby and remobilization costs and before any delay LDs recovered. With a four-month slip (Exercise 71.7): lost revenue GBP 67.5 million, extra interest GBP 62.5 million. Lesson: the delay cost is dominated by revenue and interest, not by the contractor's extra costs; delay LDs and DSU insurance (Chapter 27) must be measured against this number. The writer notes that where a revenue contract's term runs from a fixed date, delay also shortens contracted life (principle only).

**Example 71.4. From gross to net energy offshore** (`ex:71.4`, Illustrative; Section 71.2.2). A 1,200 MW phase: gross 4,812.0 GWh a year; wake loss 9.8%; electrical loss 2.6%; availability 94.1%; other losses 1.5%. Net 3,918.5 GWh (net capacity factor 37.3%). At availability 96.0% (better access vessels): 3,997.6 GWh, a gain of 79.1 GWh a year. Lesson: offshore availability is partly a logistics decision, and lenders ask what it costs to buy.

## 71.8 Frameworks

**Framework 71.1: Pre-FID exposure clock** (`fw:pre-fid-exposure-clock`; original). Purpose: measure how long and how much a developer is exposed between fixing its revenue price and fixing its costs and financing. Steps: (1) list each price-setting date: revenue award, turbine and vessel reservations, EPC or package price fixing, interest-rate hedge, tax-credit qualification, FID, financial close; (2) for each, record whether the price or rate is fixed, indexed or open; (3) chart the windows where revenue is fixed but a cost or rate is open; (4) estimate the sensitivity of project IRR to each open item (Example 71.2 method); (5) decide what to lock early (reservations, hedges), what to index (seek contract indexation), and what exit costs are acceptable (completion security, reservation fees). When to use: at bid, at every reservation commitment, and at FID. Demonstrated on Ocean Wind and in the judgment drill.

## 71.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: package map and interface matrix (Chapter 23); vessel contracts and their weather and standby terms; installation schedule against weather windows with P50 and P90; serial-defect provisions and warranty caps in turbine and cable contracts; marine warranty surveyor scope; CAR and DSU program with offshore-specific deductibles; transmission arrangement (owned, OFTO sale, or third-party); revenue contract milestone, long-stop and delay mechanics; ECA eligibility and content; sponsor completion support and sell-down terms; decommissioning security.

Red flags: a revenue price fixed with no indexation and FID more than two years away; reservation payments that are non-refundable and large relative to equity; one vessel on the critical path with no substitute; a turbine model with little operating history at the rated size; export cable with no spare length or repair plan; contingency below the IE's P90; a sell-down at close that leaves no sponsor with a controlling stake through construction.

Rules of thumb and their limits: generation and transmission gearing differ (Dogger Bank: about 65% to 70% versus 90% of OFTO proceeds), but only where a regulated sale exists; ECAs follow turbine and cable content; delay cost is mostly revenue and interest (Example 71.3).

Common mistakes: comparing strike prices in different base years (2012 versus 2024 prices); reading headline debt as gearing of generation assets; treating weather delay as insurable; ignoring the OFTO transfer value risk.

Questions experts ask: What is fixed today and what is still open? Which vessel installs the turbines, and what happens if it is late? What does the turbine supplier owe us if a blade defect appears in year three across every unit? Who owns the export cable after COD? What do we lose if we walk away now?

## 71.12 Judgment drill

The situation. You sit on the investment committee of a sponsor that won a 20-year CPI-indexed CfD for a 1.4 GW offshore phase. FID is 26 months away. The only suitable installation vessel for the planned turbine size offers a slot reservation requiring a non-refundable deposit equal to 12% of the T&I contract price now, with the rest at FID. Without the reservation, the next slot is a year later, after the CfD's milestone date. Turbine price is indexed to steel until FID; financing will be hedged only at financial close. The committee meets Monday.

Reasoning: apply Framework 71.1; the CfD is indexed to CPI (better than Ocean Wind's fixed escalator), but capex, steel and rates remain open; the vessel reservation trades a certain sunk cost for the schedule that protects the revenue contract; an expert would reserve the slot if the deposit is small relative to the cost of missing the milestone, negotiate a step-down refund if FID fails for reasons outside the sponsor's control, seek a pre-hedge or deal-contingent hedge for rates, and set an internal kill point with a known exit cost.

What would change the answer: a second suitable vessel (lower value of reservation); a CfD with no milestone risk (less urgency); a turbine supplier willing to fix price now (less open exposure); a deposit larger than the equity the sponsor can lose without board approval.

## 71.16 Neighbors

Chapter 70 (Onshore wind and solar) teaches plants with no fuel bill whose revenue depends on resource, capture price, curtailment and policy, using the resource-to-revenue bridge, the dated US DSCR ladder, and the Chile, REIPPPP and Lake Turkana cases. Chapter 72 (Hydropower and geothermal) turns to the two renewable technologies whose risk lies in the ground and the water: long-lived, civil-heavy hydro plants with hydrology and geology risk, financed by DFIs on long tenors; and geothermal plants whose resource is proven only by drilling.
