## 61-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Financial close converts a stack of signed documents into a monthly cash machine that the lenders control: money leaves the accounts only when the independent engineer certifies progress, the cost-to-complete test balances, and nothing has gone wrong that the finance documents treat as a default. This chapter teaches the reader to run that machine from either side of the table during the two to four years when a project is most likely to fail. It covers the drawdown cycle and its tests, the management of contingency, how lenders monitor progress and spot slippage before the contractor admits it, how change orders, claims, delay liquidated damages and insurance proceeds actually move through the accounts, what happens when the EPC contractor gets into financial trouble or walks away, and how lenders decide that a project has reached financial completion so that sponsor support falls away and the credit becomes an operating credit. The Case P installment carries Bélanou from notice to proceed (August 1, 2018) through COVID-19, a failed foundation pour, a transformer destroyed by a grid surge, the insurance claim, the overrun funding, the November 2021 completion tests, and Project Completion under the finance documents on June 30, 2022.

What you will be able to do (2 to 4 items, each ending with the capability number):

- Run a drawdown: check the independent engineer's certificate, apply the cost-to-complete test, and decide whether to fund, part-fund, or block a request (Capability 10).
- Read a construction monitoring report, measure schedule slippage with earned value, and identify the signs of an overrun or contractor distress months before a default (Capabilities 7 and 11).
- Trace a delay, a change order, or an insured loss through the EPC contract, the PPA, the insurance policies and the accounts to the party that finally pays, and size the gap that sponsors or standby funding must fill (Capabilities 3 and 10).
- Specify a financial completion test and decide when the evidence justifies releasing sponsor support (Capabilities 6 and 10).

## 61-6. Worked examples

All Illustrative unless stated. Every figure below was computed in Python in round 1 and reconciles. Party names are fictional (name check per Case Bible Part 5 before drafting).

Example 61.1 Running the cost-to-complete test on a solar drawdown (Illustrative) (`ex:61.1`)
- Scenario: a 236 MW solar PV plant in Uzbekistan's Bukhara region with a 25-year USD-denominated PPA with the state single buyer, financed by a club of two DFIs and one commercial bank; financial close August 2024; 22-month EPC program; the test is run for the Month 14 drawdown in September 2025. Fictional parties: project company Tolqin Quyosh Energy LLC; EPC contractor Jinhai Power Construction; IE Ardley Bryce Engineering. Gearing 70:30, debt and equity drawn pro rata.
- Inputs (USD m): total budget including financing costs 247.8; facilities senior debt 173.5 and equity 74.3; costs paid to date 131.6; cash in the disbursement account 3.4; remaining budget 116.2, of which unused contingency 9.1, so remaining base costs 107.1; IE's estimate of identified overruns on remaining work 13.9 (tracker delivery delay, substation transformer price, grid-code studies).
- Computed: drawn to date 135.0 (debt 94.5, equity 40.5); undrawn debt 79.0, undrawn equity 33.8; available funds = 79.0 + 33.8 + 3.4 = 116.2. IE cost to complete = 107.1 + 13.9 = 121.0. Funding shortfall = 121.0 − 116.2 = 4.8. The contingency (9.1) is already inside the available funds and absorbs 9.1 of the 13.9 overrun; the remaining 4.8 blocks the drawdown until cured.
- Teaching: the test uses the IE's estimate, not the budget; at budget the project balances exactly, which is why the test can fail with contingency still unspent; cure options (sponsor top-up, standby, re-scoping) and who decides.
- Formula eq:61.1: $\mathrm{Shortfall}_t = \max\left(0,\ \mathrm{CTC}_t - (U^{D}_t + U^{E}_t + \mathrm{Cash}_t)\right)$, where CTC is the IE's cost to complete including identified overruns, $U^D$ and $U^E$ are undrawn debt and equity commitments, and Cash is the balance in construction accounts. ## 61-8. Frameworks

- Framework 61.1 Delay cash bridge (`fw:delay-cash-bridge`), original. Purpose: show in one table what a day of delay costs the project company and which source pays for each part. Steps: (1) count critical-path delay days by cause and contractual category (contractor risk, employer risk, force majeure); (2) for each category compute the daily cost to the project company: interest, fixed costs, lost net revenue, PPA delay LDs out; (3) list the daily inflows: EPC delay LDs (subject to cap), DSU (only for insured physical damage, after the time deductible), PPA relief (RCOD extension removes LDs out); (4) net the two and cumulate over the delay; (5) map the cumulative gap to the funding order (contingency, standby, contingent equity, sponsor support). Demonstrated in Example 61.4 and in 61.15.4, where the Case P daily rates come from P-F33 (FC base daily interest, fixed costs, PPA delay LDs and EPC delay LDs per day).
- Framework 61.2 Contractor distress ladder (`fw:contractor-distress-ladder`), original. Rungs with triggers and actions: (1) watch: profit warning, provisions, rating actions, slow subcontractor payment, increase IE visits and request contractor's cost-to-complete; (2) protect: reduce payments ahead of progress, insist on bond renewals, verify subcontractor payments, prepare step-in and assignment documents; (3) intervene: call bonds where entitled, direct payments to key subcontractors under direct agreements, seek parent guarantee performance; (4) replace: terminate, assign subcontracts, procure replacement, price latent defects (Example 61.5); (5) restructure: if the gap exceeds available support, open the conversation that Ch 64 teaches. Demonstrated on Carillion (61.9) and Purple Line (61.10).
- Framework 61.3 Completion test matrix (`fw:completion-test-matrix`), original. A matrix of seven test families (physical, operational, performance, financial, legal and permits, reserves and accounts, no default) against three questions: what evidence proves it, who certifies, and what is released when it passes. Demonstrated in 61.11.2 and in the Case P completion (61.15.5), where the ten elements of annex P 1.12 are mapped onto the seven families.

## 61-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (monthly drawdown review): IE certificate received and unqualified or reservations understood; cost-to-complete test passes on the IE's estimate; contingency drawings allocated to named items; payments certified not ahead of earned value; claims and variation registers updated; insurances in force and notifications made; permits on schedule; E&S action plan items on track; equity proportion respected; no default notice outstanding.

Red flags: float on the critical path consumed faster than time elapses; programme re-baselined twice; contractor's group issues a profit warning or a provision; bond issuer seeks to cap or not renew; subcontractors complain of late payment; commissioning team mobilization slips; the owner's engineer and IE disagree on percent complete by more than a few points; insurers' loss adjuster issues a reservation of rights on causation.

Rules of thumb and their limits (all indicative, writer dates and places them): a cost-to-complete test with no headroom at mid-construction fails more often than it passes in the second half; contingency used should not run ahead of percent complete (fails for front-loaded civil risk such as geotechnics, where contingency is properly spent early); an SPI below 0.90 at mid-programme rarely recovers without acceleration costs (limit: early-programme SPI is noisy); delay LDs typically cover interest and fixed costs but not lost margin (depends on calibration, Ch 22).

Common mistakes: certifying a payment milestone on delivery of equipment that has not been inspected; treating DSU as cover for any delay; letting EOT under the EPC and RCOD extension under the PPA drift apart; settling a claim without lenders' consent above the threshold; releasing retention against a bond from a weakening bank; declaring financial completion before the reserve accounts are funded.

Questions experts ask: "What does the IE's cost to complete include that the contractor's does not?"; "Which delays are on the critical path, and who owns each day?"; "If the contractor failed tomorrow, how much have we paid that has not been earned?"; "Does the PPA give the same relief the EPC gives for this event?"; "What exactly does the IE certificate not cover?"; "What is released at completion, and what evidence releases it?"

## 61-12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the credit officer for the mandated lead arranger on the 312 MW Rio Grande do Norte wind farm of Example 61.6. It is Month 23, December 2025. The sponsor asks lenders to declare financial completion on Friday so that USD 24.6 million of contingent equity commitments and the sponsors' completion guarantee fall away and the first distribution can be paid at the next test date. The reliability run passed but with two turbine-transformer trips; the power-curve test shows output 2.4% short; the contractor has paid USD 12.28 million of performance LDs, which have prepaid debt, and the IE's updated banking case shows a minimum DSCR of 1.348x against a 1.35x completion test (Example 61.6 figures). The IE certifies completion with a reservation on the transformer trips. Deadline: Friday.

Reasoning outline: separate the elements (physical completion is achieved; the financial test misses by 0.002x; the reservation is on a recurring equipment fault that could signal a serial defect); weigh what the guarantee and contingent equity protect against (completion risk, not operating risk) against what the trips might be (operating risk, warranty claim); options: (a) refuse until the test passes, (b) grant completion with a condition subsequent and retain a portion of contingent equity or an LC until a 90-day trip-free period, (c) grant on materiality grounds. Expert answer: (b), with the retained support sized to the potential cost of a fleet-wide transformer replacement and the 0.002x shortfall cured by a small sponsor prepayment or accepted as a technical waiver because it falls inside rounding of the IE model; document the reasoning in the credit file.

What would change the answer: if the trips are traced to a known serial defect on the transformer model, refuse completion until the root-cause fix is in place; if the PPA has a termination right tied to reliability, refuse; if the sponsor is investment grade with a strong guarantee, accept (b) with a smaller retention; if the IE's updated case relies on optimistic availability, rerun at the banking case first.

Close of chapter (61.18): crystallize that completion moves risk from the construction balance sheets (contractor, sponsor) to the operating cash flow alone; end on the open problem Chapter 62 answers: from the first test date onward the lenders have no guarantees to call, only covenants, reports and reserves, so how do owner and lender run a project through 20 years of semiannual tests?

## 62-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. After lenders' completion a project company has 15 to 25 years of operation in front of it and no sponsor guarantee behind it. What protects the lenders now is a rhythm: reports on fixed dates, a budget they approve, ratio tests twice a year that decide whether the sponsors see any cash, reserve accounts that fill and empty, and a consent process for anything the documents did not foresee. What creates value for the sponsors is running the plant better than the base case and finding upgrades the lenders will let them finance. This chapter teaches both sides of that rhythm: the operating covenant calendar, budgets and their variance rules, running a distribution test on a real test date, lock-up and trapped cash in practice, outage planning against availability targets, operational-technology cyber controls, managing the O&M operator and LTSA provider, running and pricing waivers and amendments in ordinary operations, and financing optimization projects (uprates, repowering, hybridization) inside an existing financing. Its Case P installment carries Bélanou from its first operating year through the June 30, 2023 historic DSCR breach to the October 26, 2023 waiver.

What you will be able to do:

- Run the operating covenant calendar of a project finance borrower: reports, budget approval, compliance certificates and model updates (Capability 10).
- Perform a distribution test on a test date, explain lock-up and trapped cash, and decide whether an equity cure or a waiver is the better route after a breach (Capabilities 5 and 10).
- Draft and negotiate a waiver request and price the waiver from both sides (Capabilities 6 and 11).
- Evaluate an optimization project such as hybridization or an uprate, including the lender consents and the pro forma ratio tests it needs (Capabilities 12 and 15).

## 62-5. Opening

## 62-6. Worked examples

Example 62.1 Testing an operating budget against variance rules (Illustrative) (`ex:62.1`)
- Scenario: a 220 MWac portfolio of four solar plants in Extremadura, Spain, project-financed in 2022 and USD-reported for comparability; the test compares the approved 2025 budget with the September 2025 full-year forecast. Fictional parties: owner Dehesa Solar Portfolio SL; O&M operator Valcorba O&M Services SL; facility agent a Madrid branch of an international bank (unnamed). Finance documents allow aggregate opex up to 110% of the approved budget, and any discretionary line up to 115%; insurance, grid fees and taxes are non-discretionary.
- Inputs (USD m), budget against current-year forecast: O&M contract 6.42 against 6.42; land leases 1.87 against 1.87; insurance 1.36 against 1.71; grid and market fees 2.11 against 2.29; asset management 1.18 against 1.18; security and other 0.74 against 0.81; corrective maintenance 1.18 against 1.93. Totals 14.86 against 16.21.
- Computed: aggregate variance +9.1% (inside 110%); line variances: insurance +25.7% (non-discretionary, permitted), grid fees +8.5%, security +9.5%, corrective maintenance +63.6% (discretionary line breach: needs consent or must be funded from a permitted source).
- Teaching: the aggregate test passes while a line test fails; how the asset manager presents the corrective maintenance overrun (inverter failures; warranty claim pending) to get consent; what the default budget would be if consent is refused.

Example 62.2 A distribution test on a semiannual test date (Illustrative) (`ex:62.2`)
- Scenario: a 186 MW onshore wind farm in Chile's Biobío region with a USD PPA with a copper-mining offtaker; lock-up at historic DSCR below 1.20x; test on the 12 months to December 31, 2024. Fictional parties: project company Parque Eólico Cerro Ñipas SpA; facility agent and model bank unnamed.
- Inputs (USD m): CFADS H1 19.62 and H2 19.12; senior debt service H1 15.07 and H2 15.04; projected DSCR for the next 12 months 1.31x (from the updated banking case); DSRA full; MMRA contribution due in H2 0.86.
- Computed: historic DSCR = (19.62 + 19.12) / (15.07 + 15.04) = 38.74 / 30.11 = 1.287x, pass; cash available for distribution at the December payment date = 19.12 − 15.04 − 0.86 = 3.22. Variant: if H1 CFADS had been 16.31 (a gearbox failure), historic DSCR = 35.43 / 30.11 = 1.177x, lock-up; the 3.22 stays in the project company until the release condition is met.
- Teaching: a 12-month look-back carries a bad half-year into two tests; the documents' release condition (one pass or two consecutive passes) decides whether the cash is released in June 2025 or December 2025.

Example 62.3 A forced outage and the availability headroom (Illustrative) (`ex:62.3`)
- Scenario: a 450 MW open-cycle gas turbine peaking plant (two 225 MW units) near Mombasa, Kenya, with a 20-year capacity-and-energy PPA with the national utility; capacity charge USD 11.42/kW-month; capacity payment = charge × contracted capacity × min(1, availability / 90%), with no bonus above target; the six-month settlement period January 1 to June 30, 2025 (181 days). Fictional party: project company Pwani Peaking Power Ltd.
- Inputs: planned outage of unit 1 for 14 days; forced outage of unit 2 for 23 days.
- Computed: full capacity payment for the period USD 30.834 million. Planned outage alone: availability 96.13%, no loss. With the forced outage: availability = 1 − (225 × 14 + 225 × 23) / (450 × 181) = 89.78%, payment 30.758, loss USD 0.076 million. If the forced outage lasts 41 days: availability 84.81%, loss USD 1.779 million.
- Teaching: the first 22.2 unit-days of forced outage in this period cost nothing because of headroom between actual availability and the target; beyond it every unit-day costs about USD 0.095 million (18.8 excess unit-days in the 41-day case give the USD 1.779 million loss); planned outages spend headroom, so timing them matters. Cross-ref Ch 18 (eq:18.1) for the formula; this example does not re-derive it.

Example 62.4 What a waiver costs and what a cure costs (Illustrative) (`ex:62.4`)
- Scenario: a 124 MW geothermal plant in Aydın province, western Turkey, with a USD-denominated feed-in tariff, project-financed in 2019 by a club of three Turkish and international banks; a well workover program cut output in the first half of 2025; historic DSCR at the June 30, 2025 test 1.04x against an event-of-default level of 1.10x and lock-up at 1.20x; one equity cure still available. Fictional party: project company Bozdoğan Jeotermal Elektrik AŞ.
- Inputs (USD m): senior debt outstanding 286.3; average balance over the next 18 months 279.0; annual debt service 31.2; lenders' proposed waiver terms: fee 0.30% of outstanding, margin uplift 0.75% for 18 months, advisors' costs 0.62.
- Computed: CFADS = 1.04 × 31.2 = 32.448; waiver fee 0.859; margin uplift 3.139; total waiver cost 4.618. Equity cure to 1.10x: 1.10 × 31.2 − 32.448 = 1.872; cure to the 1.20x lock-up level: 4.992.
- Teaching: the cure is cheaper in cash but uses a limited right, is equity at risk that may be trapped, and does not solve a structural decline; the waiver costs more but buys time and can carry conditions; how the sponsor chooses (link to judgment drill).

Example 62.5 Adding a battery to a solar plant inside an existing financing (Illustrative) (`ex:62.5`)
- Scenario: a 147.5 MWac solar plant in Maricopa County, Arizona, project-financed in 2021; in March 2026 the sponsor proposes a 62 MW / 248 MWh battery behind the same interconnection, with a 15-year capacity toll from the local utility. Fictional party: project company Saguaro Flats Solar LLC.
- Inputs: capex USD 268/kWh (shared interconnection) = USD 66.46 million; toll USD 11.95/kW-month on 62 MW = USD 8.891 million a year; energy-shifting margin USD 1.86 million a year; opex USD 1.71 million a year; augmentation of 6% of MWh in year 8 at USD 190/kWh = USD 2.827 million; project discount rate 8.5%; life 15 years. Lenders permit additional pari passu debt sized at 1.35x on contracted CFADS (toll less opex) and 2.00x on merchant CFADS (the energy-shifting margin), capped at 70% of capex, 12-year annuity at 6.90%.
- Computed: annual CFADS 9.041; project NPV at 8.5% USD 7.14 million; project IRR 10.26%. Additional debt: DSCR-based 49.9, cap 46.52 binding; annual debt service 5.826; DSCR 1.552x; in year 8 the augmentation cuts cash to 6.214 and DSCR to 1.066x unless an augmentation reserve is built; equity 19.94; equity IRR 15.4%.
- Teaching: the hybrid is valuable but the existing lenders must accept a new pari passu creditor, a new counterparty, new technology risk and a year-8 dip; the pro forma combined ratio test and an augmentation reserve are the price of consent.

## 62-8. Frameworks

- Framework 62.1 Operating covenant calendar (`fw:operating-covenant-calendar`), original. A one-page annual calendar listing every recurring obligation and right with the date, the owner on the borrower side, the reviewer on the lender side, and the consequence of a miss. Steps: (1) extract every dated obligation from the CTA, accounts agreement, ICA and key contracts; (2) place on a 12-month calendar with lead times; (3) mark the test dates and work back to the data cut-off; (4) assign owners; (5) flag items whose miss is an event of default after grace. Demonstrated on the Example 62.2 wind farm and on Case P in 62.11.
- Framework 62.2 Waiver request ladder (`fw:waiver-request-ladder`), original. Classifies a request by what it touches and therefore who must agree and how long it takes, using the thresholds taught in Ch 51 (ssec:51.7.1) and Ch 53: rung 1 technical or administrative consent (agent or majority); rung 2 waiver of a breach without amendment (majority lenders); rung 3 amendment of covenant levels, reserves or distribution conditions (supermajority in many documents); rung 4 amendment of payment terms, pricing, maturity, security release (all-lender matters); rung 5 matters needing ECA or DFI policy approval (E&S, sanctions, tenor beyond policy limits). For each rung: decision-maker, typical timetable, and the price in Illustrative terms (no market fee levels; no fact sheet). Demonstrated in 62.7 and Case P (the 2023 letter touches rungs 2, 4 and 5; annex P 1.15.11).
- Framework 62.3 Optimization gate (`fw:optimization-gate`), original. Five questions before spending capex in an operating project: (1) does it create value at the project level on a stand-alone NPV; (2) which contracts and permits must change and at what cost; (3) does it fit inside permitted capex and permitted debt, or does it need consent; (4) what is the pro forma effect on minimum DSCR, LLCR and the dip years; (5) who bears the new technology, counterparty and interface risk. Demonstrated in Example 62.5.

## 62-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (each test date): data cut-off met; CFADS reconciled to bank statements; exclusions applied as defined; ratio recomputed independently by the agent or model bank; reserves at required levels; projected case updated on approved assumptions; no default and no potential default; officer's certificate signed by an authorized signatory; distribution conditions all met before cash leaves the distribution account.

Red flags: receivables aging beyond the PPA's payment terms; forced outage rate rising for two periods; opex variances explained as "timing" twice; the O&M operator's incentive payments rising while availability falls; insurance renewal quoted late or with reduced limits; projected DSCR propped up by an assumption change rather than performance; requests to change the calculation basis near a test date.

Rules of thumb and their limits (qualitative practitioner judgment, dated and placed by the writer, with no fee or margin figures): call the agent before the certificate, not after; a first waiver for a cause outside the borrower's control prices low, a second prices like a restructuring; outages planned in the low-dispatch season cost less headroom; an equity cure is right for a one-off dip and wrong for a trend.

Common mistakes: counting insurance proceeds for property damage in CFADS; testing on accrual numbers when the documents say cash; letting the default budget roll for a second year; treating a waiver as an amendment (it does not change future tests); assuming a majority lender consent is enough for a deferral of principal; signing an O&M settlement that changes fees without lender consent; reporting a cyber incident to the regulator before the lenders when the information covenant requires prompt notice to both.

Questions experts ask: "What moved CFADS this period, in order of size?"; "Which assumption in the projected case would you least like the IE to look at?"; "How much headroom is left on availability this period?"; "What is the next test date and what would fail it?"; "Which lenders need internal credit approval for this waiver, and how long does each take?"

## 62-12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the CFO of the 124 MW geothermal plant in Example 62.4. The June test shows historic DSCR of 1.04x; the event-of-default level is 1.10x. The workover programme is 70% complete and the reservoir engineer forecasts recovery to 1.32x within 12 months, with a 25% chance that one more production well is needed (USD 6.8 million). You have one equity cure left (cures cannot be consecutive). The lenders' proposed waiver costs USD 4.618 million over 18 months; a cure to 1.10x costs USD 1.872 million and leaves you in lock-up below 1.20x. The compliance certificate is due in 14 days.

Reasoning outline: the decisive factors are (1) whether the dip is temporary (reservoir evidence says yes, with a 25% tail), (2) the value of keeping the last cure for a later, worse event, (3) the waiver's conditions beyond price (lock-up release test, reporting, and what a waiver signals to the ECA and rating agencies). An expert would negotiate the waiver down (shorter uplift period tied to the remediation plan, fee credited if the next test passes) and keep the cure; if the lenders insist on full terms, cure to 1.10x now and pre-negotiate a waiver of the lock-up only if the extra well is needed.

What would change the answer: a reservoir decline curve rather than a workover issue (structural: neither cure nor waiver; restructure or refinance, Ch 63 and Ch 64); a lender group that includes an ECA with a slow approval process (cure, because a waiver cannot be signed in 14 days); a cure clause that counts the cure as CFADS for four quarters (cure becomes more valuable); a sponsor that cannot fund (waiver is the only route).

Close of chapter (62.14): a waiver suspends the test; it does not change the debt that caused the breach. End on the question Ch 63 answers: once the crisis passes and the project has an operating record, can the sponsors replace the construction-era debt, with its margins, swaps and restrictions, by something cheaper, longer and lighter, and who has to agree?

## 63-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. The debt that builds a project is priced for construction risk, sized on forecasts, and hedged against rates that will move. Once the plant has run for a year or two, all three can be improved, and much of the value sponsors earn in project finance comes from doing so: replacing bank debt with longer, cheaper bonds, repricing margins, re-gearing to release equity, and selling stakes to investors who want operating yield. Each move has costs that are easy to miss (swap breakage, make-whole, fees, tax, lost grandfathering, consent fees) and parties who must agree (lenders who stay, ECAs and DFIs, offtakers, governments that share in gains or bear termination exposure). This chapter teaches the reader to measure a refinancing gain correctly, to choose between refinancing and repricing, to execute a refinancing on a settlement date without leaving breakage on the table, to price and execute the sale of a stake in a project-financed company, and to use holdco leverage without letting it break the group. The Case P installment covers the June 2025 bond refinancing and the September 2026 sale of 24% to Coldharbour; the Case R installment covers the December 2025 private placement and holdco repricing.

## 63-5. Opening

## 63-6. Worked examples

All Illustrative; party names fictional (name check before drafting); every figure recomputed in Python in round 1.

Example 63.1 Measuring a refinancing gain on a contracted wind farm (Illustrative) (`ex:63.1`)
- Scenario: a contracted 210 MW wind farm in western Oklahoma with a 20-year PPA with an investor-owned utility, COD June 2023; the refinancing is evaluated in June 2026, three years after COD. Fictional parties: project company Sallisaw Ridge Wind LLC; existing lenders a five-bank club; the bond house and the hedge counterparty unnamed. Existing bank loan USD 312.4 million outstanding, 11 years remaining, equal annual principal (USD 28.4 million a year); floating at SOFR plus 2.75%, swapped in full at 3.10%, all-in 5.85%. Refinancing option: fixed-rate senior notes at 5.05% (current swap rate 3.85% plus a 1.20% spread) on the same amortization; refinancing settles on an interest payment date (no loan breakage); costs 1.10% of principal plus USD 1.9 million.
- Computed: annual saving year 1 = 312.4 × 0.80% = USD 2.499 million; nominal savings over 11 years USD 15.0 million; PV of savings at the new debt rate 5.05% = USD 12.22 million (at a 9% equity rate: USD 10.59 million). Swap unwind: market 3.85% against fixed 3.10%, so the project receives; value USD 12.0 million (discounted at 3.85%). Costs USD 5.336 million. Net gain USD 18.88 million (debt-rate view). Cross-check: PV of the margin reduction alone (2.75% to 1.20% on the balance, at 5.05%) = USD 23.67 million, less costs = USD 18.33 million; the difference reflects discounting the swap at 3.85% rather than 5.05%. Weighted average life 6.0 years.
- Teaching: the saving shown against the old all-in rate (0.80%) understates the margin compression (1.55%) because the old swap was below market; the swap unwind receipt makes up the difference; a reader who counts both against a market-rate comparison double-counts; a reader who forgets the swap undercounts. Two discount rates must not be mixed without saying so.
- Formula eq:63.1: $G = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{(i^{\mathrm{old}} - i^{\mathrm{new}}) D_{t}}{(1+i^{\mathrm{new}})^{t}} + V^{\mathrm{swap}} - B^{\mathrm{loan}} - M - C$, with $V^{\mathrm{swap}}$ the swap value to the project (positive when the project receives; valuation method from eq:6.7 in Ch 6), $B$ loan breakage, $M$ make-whole, $C$ transaction costs. Excel layout and formula in walkthrough 63.8.

Example 63.2 The make-whole cost of refinancing fixed-rate notes (Illustrative) (`ex:63.2`)
- Scenario: USD 156.4 million of bullet senior secured notes issued in 2023 by a contracted gas-fired peaking plant in Texas, 6.20% semiannual coupon, seven years (14 periods) remaining at the July 2026 evaluation date, make-whole at Treasuries plus 50 bps (an Illustrative spread: `t-project-bonds` says to state no typical make-whole spread). Fictional issuer: Brazos Bend Peaking LLC.
- Computed (eq:63.2 applying eq:30.2): with the comparable Treasury at 4.10% (discount 4.60%), PV of remaining payments USD 171.232 million; make-whole premium USD 14.832 million (9.48% of par). Sensitivity: Treasury 3.10% gives 24.964; 5.10% gives 5.373; 5.70% gives nil (the formula floors at par).
- Formula eq:63.2 (caption "Make-whole cost at the refinancing settlement date, applying eq:30.2"; R-078, R-116): $\mathrm{MW} = \max\left(0,\ \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c P/2}{(1+y/2)^{k}} + \frac{P}{(1+y/2)^{n}} - P\right)$ with $y$ the Treasury yield plus the spread. Excel: `=MAX(0, PV(F6/2, F7, -F5*F4/2, -F5) - F5)`.
- Teaching: the make-whole is defined in Ch 30; here it is a cost line in a refinancing decision: it makes early refinancing of bonds uneconomic unless rates rise or the project re-gears; compare with bank debt that prepays at par on an interest date (Clause 63.1).

Example 63.3 Re-gearing after completion (Illustrative) (`ex:63.3`)
- Scenario: an operating 140 MWac solar plant in Chile's Atacama region with 15 remaining years on a USD PPA with a distribution company, considering an equity-release refinancing in 2026. Fictional party: project company Fotovoltaica Pampa Ilusión SpA. Flat CFADS of USD 41.8 million a year for 15 remaining contract years; existing debt USD 205.7 million, 9 years remaining, annuity at 6.10% (debt service USD 30.374 million, DSCR 1.376x). New debt sized at 1.30x over 13 years at 5.40%; costs 1.2% plus USD 2.4 million.
- Computed: new debt capacity USD 294.9 million; gross equity release 89.2; costs 5.94; net release 83.26; new debt service 32.154. Equity value at 9%: before 154.84, after 179.46; gain 24.62. Variant at 1.35x: capacity 283.97, release 78.27.
- Teaching: re-gearing converts future distributions into cash today and is worth more when the refinancing rate is below the equity discount rate; it raises termination exposure for a public counterparty whose compensation covers debt (link to 63.4.2 and Ch 58).

Example 63.4 Pricing a 30% stake in a project company (Illustrative) (`ex:63.4`)
- Scenario: a developer offers 30% of a contracted 250 MWac solar project company in Portugal's Alentejo (shares and shareholder loans pro rata) to a Dutch pension fund manager in 2026. Fictional parties: seller Montado Renováveis SA; the buyer unnamed. Distributions to all equity: USD 22.6 million in year 1, growing 1.5% a year for 18 years. Buyer's discount rate 11.0%; seller's reserve rate 10.0%. Locked-box date December 31, 2025, nine months before completion on September 30, 2026; ticker 6.0% a year on the price; permitted leakage zero; actual leakage USD 0.84 million (a distribution paid after the locked-box date).
- Computed: equity value at 11.0% USD 190.37 million (30%: 57.11); at 10.0% USD 203.36 million (30%: 61.01); bid-ask gap 3.90; at 10.5%: 59.01. With the buyer's value: ticker 57.11 × 6.0% × 9/12 = 2.57; leakage deduction 0.84; amount payable 58.84.
- Teaching: the price is a discount-rate negotiation; the ticker compensates the seller for cash the project keeps after the locked-box date; leakage is deducted pound for pound; deferred consideration bridges disagreement on a specific risk (Case P uses it for SEKA arrears). Locked-box mechanics are taught in Ch 47 (ssec:47.3.4); this example applies them.

Example 63.5 Holdco coverage when the opco locks up (Illustrative) (`ex:63.5`)
- Scenario: a holdco owning three contracted wind farms in the Texas Panhandle in 2026 has a term loan of USD 186.0 million at 8.75% with 1% annual amortization, serviced only from opco distributions of USD 31.4 million a year; holdco debt service reserve of six months. Fictional party: borrower Llano Estacado Wind Holdings LLC.
- Computed: holdco debt service 18.135; distribution coverage 1.731x; six-month reserve 9.067. If opco locks up for one year, distributions fall to zero and the reserve covers half a year; the holdco defaults in month seven unless sponsors inject cash. If distributions fall to USD 24.0 million, coverage 1.323x.
- Teaching: holdco lenders are structurally subordinated to every opco creditor and to opco covenants; one opco lock-up can be a holdco default; holdco documents therefore watch opco headroom and require reserves; cross-ref Ch 31 for initial sizing.

Example 63.6 A margin loan on listed yieldco shares (Illustrative) (`ex:63.6`)
- Scenario: in January 2026 a sponsor holds 41.3 million shares of a US-listed renewable yieldco at USD 18.40 and borrows USD 247.5 million against them from a prime broker; collateral call at 50% LTV; cure to 40%. Fictional parties: the vehicle Halcyon Renewable Yield Inc.; the sponsor unnamed.
- Computed: collateral value 759.92; LTV 32.6%; the call price is USD 11.99 per share (a 34.9% fall). At USD 13.50 the LTV is 44.4%; at USD 11.00 it is 54.5% and the sponsor must repay USD 65.78 million (247.5 − 0.40 × 454.3) to restore 40%, or post enough extra shares to do the same.
- Teaching: the loan is safe until it is not; a forced sale depresses the price that secures the loan; the vehicle's independence and governance become the equity story (link to SunEdison).

## 63-8. Frameworks

- Framework 63.1 Refinancing gain bridge (`fw:refinancing-gain-bridge`), original. ## 63-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (before launching a refinancing): identical-profile gain computed; swap close-out indications obtained from the counterparty and checked independently; make-whole or call premiums computed; settlement on an interest payment date; tax grandfathering conditions checked; remaining lenders' consents mapped; rating feedback obtained; public counterparty gain-share and termination compensation effects quantified; transaction costs budgeted with a cap; equity NPV effect run through the model.

Red flags: a gain that exists only at the equity discount rate; a refinancing that upsizes debt near a grandfathering threshold; swap counterparties who are also arrangers of the new debt quoting the unwind; a buyer whose price relies on a refinancing it has not tested with lenders; a seller who has not checked minimum-holding covenants; a holdco lender who relies on distributions from an opco near its lock-up level.

Rules of thumb and their limits (qualitative practitioner judgment dated by the writer; no fact sheet supports market figures, so none are printed): post-COD refinancings of contracted assets cut margins most where construction-era pricing included a construction premium; refinancing a bond before its par call date rarely pays unless rates have risen (Example 63.2); repricing beats refinancing when breakage exceeds a year's saving; in a stake sale the consents, not the share purchase agreement, usually set the timetable (Case P 2026: signing April 14, completion September 30).

Common mistakes: comparing the new rate with the old all-in hedged rate and also counting the swap unwind; forgetting the cost of carry on pre-funded bond proceeds; treating gain-share as optional when the contract requires it; assuming lenders consent to a change of control because the buyer is better capitalized; letting holdco debt amortize faster than opco distributions grow.

Questions experts ask: "What is the gain on an identical profile, before any re-gearing?"; "Who quoted the swap unwind and against what curve?"; "Which consents can kill the timetable?"; "What does the buyer know about the offtaker that we do not price?"; "If the opco locks up for a year, what happens at the holdco?"

## 63-12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the investment director of an infrastructure fund that owns 100% of the Example 63.1 wind farm. A bank offers to refinance at a 1.55% lower margin on the identical profile (net gain USD 18.88 million) in 60 days; a bond house proposes 13-year notes that would re-gear (Example 63.3-style, release of about USD 83 million) but carry a make-whole and need a rating, taking five months. Your fund is in year 8 of a 10-year life and the investment committee meets in three weeks to approve a sale process for the asset next year.

Reasoning outline: the decisive factors are (1) the exit plan (a buyer values a long fixed-rate structure but pays less for an asset burdened with a make-whole if it wants to re-lever), (2) execution risk and timing relative to the sale, (3) the equity release's value to the fund's distribution to investors in the last years. An expert would take the bank repricing now (fast, prepayable at par, keeps flexibility for the buyer) and leave re-gearing to the buyer, unless the sale is uncertain, in which case the bond release returns capital and locks in tenor.

What would change the answer: a rate environment where long fixed rates are expected to rise (favor the bond now); a buyer universe of pension funds that wants long amortizing bonds (bond adds value to the sale); an offtaker with gain-share rights (lower the net gain of both); a fund extension that removes the exit deadline.

Close of chapter (63.14): refinancing and sale both assume someone wants the project's risk at a better price than the original lenders charged. End on the open problem Ch 64 answers: when traffic, prices or costs move the wrong way and no lender will refinance (Case T's mini-perm maturing in May 2022), what do lenders, sponsors and the state do?

## 64-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Projects fail in a small number of ways, and almost all of them show up in the numbers months or years before a default. When they fail, the people around the table have very different options: sponsors hold an option on the upside and can walk; senior lenders hold security that is worth most if the project keeps running; subordinated and public lenders hold claims whose value depends on what the others accept; the state holds the concession, the tolls or the tariff, and the political consequences. This chapter teaches the reader to spot distress early, to run the first ninety days (reservation of rights, creditor organization, standstill), to buy time with waivers, deferrals and amend-and-extend, to value the alternatives (restructure, sell, enforce, terminate), to size sustainable debt and allocate the new equity in a debt-for-equity swap, to enforce over shares and assets when that is the better route, and to use the insolvency tools of the main jurisdictions: US Chapter 11, English schemes of arrangement and Part 26A restructuring plans, and the civil-law and EU preventive frameworks. It closes with what is different about PPPs and with what the state does in a project restructuring. The Case T installment carries the Merrick Link from its first failed lock-up test in December 2019 through default, standstill, sponsor support, amend-and-extend, and a court-sanctioned restructuring plan with cross-class cram-down effective December 18, 2023.

What you will be able to do:

- Read the early warning signs of project distress and set up the first ninety days of a lender response (Capability 11).
- Value the alternatives open to creditors, size sustainable debt, and design a debt-for-equity restructuring whose allocation of value will survive a court's scrutiny (Capabilities 11 and 12).
- Choose between consensual restructuring, distressed sale, enforcement over shares or assets, and a court process in the US, England and the main civil-law systems (Capabilities 8 and 11).
- Negotiate a PPP restructuring with the contracting authority and the state as parties with their own levers (Capabilities 9 and 15).

## 64-6. Worked examples

All Illustrative; party names fictional (name check before drafting); every figure recomputed in Python in round 1.

Example 64.1 Liquidity runway at a container terminal (Illustrative) (`ex:64.1`)
- Scenario: a container terminal concession on Colombia's Caribbean coast with USD-indexed tariffs, project-financed in 2019; in March 2025 its anchor shipping line moves its regional hub, and volumes fall 41%. Fictional party: concessionaire Terminal Portuaria del Darién SA.
- Inputs: monthly CFADS before the loss USD 3.9 million; monthly senior debt service USD 3.1 million; fixed-cost drag on the volume loss USD 0.35 million a month; DSRA USD 18.6 million (six months); unrestricted cash USD 6.4 million.
- Computed: new monthly CFADS = 3.9 × 0.59 − 0.35 = 1.951; DSCR 0.629x; monthly shortfall 1.149; DSRA alone lasts 16.2 months; with cash 21.8 months.
- Formula eq:64.1: $R = \frac{\mathrm{Cash} + \mathrm{DSRA}}{\mathrm{DS}_m - \mathrm{CFADS}_m}$ (months, when the denominator is positive). Excel: `=IF(J12>J11, (J14+J15)/(J12-J11), "no shortfall")`.
- Teaching: the runway is the time available to negotiate; DSRA drawing is not distress management but it buys the months in which it happens; the clock starts at the first drawing, not at default.

Example 64.2 The price of an amend-and-extend (Illustrative) (`ex:64.2`)
- Scenario: a EUR 287.5 million bank mini-perm on an availability-based acute hospital PPP in Ireland, closed in 2014, matures in June 2024 with no refinancing market for its sector; lenders offer an 18-month extension. Fictional party: project company Liffeyside Health Partners DAC.
- Inputs: extension fee 0.75%; margin from 2.40% to 3.40%; 100% cash sweep; monthly reporting; a milestone to sign a refinancing or restructuring term sheet within 12 months.
- Computed: fee EUR 2.156 million; extra margin 1.00% × 287.5 × 1.5 = EUR 4.313 million; total EUR 6.469 million plus the opportunity cost of swept cash.
- Teaching: an A&E prices time, not the underlying credit problem; the milestone converts it into a bridge to a restructuring; without one it becomes the first of several.

Example 64.3 Recoveries under four alternatives (Illustrative) (`ex:64.3`)
- Scenario: the Wenlow Estuary Crossing, a fictional user-pay bridge and approach-road concession in the north of England let in 2011 and financed under English law; by early 2025 traffic is structurally below the banking case; the senior lenders' advisor's case gives CFADS of GBP 38.2 million in year 1 growing 2.0% a year for 25 remaining concession years. Fictional parties: concessionaire Wenlow Crossing Ltd; senior lenders a club of four banks and one swap counterparty; subordinated lender a mezzanine fund. Amounts in GBP million.
- Inputs (GBP m): senior principal 621.8; accrued interest 18.2; swap close-out amount 41.6 ranking pari passu with senior debt; senior class claim 681.6; subordinated loan 112.4. Enterprise value as a going concern at 7.25% = 520.1. Restructuring requires GBP 24.6 million of new money (already reflected in the enterprise value). Enforcement sale proceeds = 88% of enterprise value less costs of 6.8 = 450.9. Termination under a market-value (retendering) regime = 80% of enterprise value less retendering costs of 9.5 = 406.6.
- Computed recoveries of the senior class: enforcement sale 450.9 (66.2%); termination 406.6 (59.7%); restructuring (Example 64.5) 488.6 (71.7%). Subordinated: nil in sale and termination.
- Formula eq:64.2: recovery of class $k$ $= \min\left(C_k,\ \max\left(0,\ V - \sum_{j<k} C_j\right)\right)$ for value $V$ and claims $C$ in order of priority. Excel: `=MIN(F20, MAX(0, F$15-SUM(F$18:F19)))`.

Example 64.4 Sizing sustainable debt and the write-down (Illustrative) (`ex:64.4`)
- Same project. New senior debt sized on the advisor's case at a minimum DSCR of 1.40x, sculpted, 22-year tenor (three-year tail), fixed rate 5.10%.
- Computed: sustainable debt GBP 424.7 million; write-down 37.7% of the 681.6 claim (GBP 256.9 million cancelled or converted); post-restructuring equity value 520.1 − 424.7 = 95.5. Variant at 1.30x: sustainable debt 457.3, write-down 32.9%, equity value 62.8.
- Formula eq:64.3: $D^{\mathrm{sus}} = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{\mathrm{CFADS}_t / \mathrm{DSCR}^{*}}{(1+i)^{t}}$; an application of Ch 36's sculpting identity (eq:36.2 and eq:36.3), which the caption cites and the text does not re-derive (R-116). Excel: `=SUMPRODUCT(J12:AE12/$F$8, J15:AE15)` with row 15 the discount factors.
- Teaching: the ratio choice moves debt and equity in opposite directions; lenders who take equity prefer less debt and more equity if they can hold equity, more debt if they cannot (bank regulatory capital, bond investment mandates).

Example 64.5 Allocating the new equity (Illustrative) (`ex:64.5`)
- Same project. Post-money equity 95.5 (enterprise value 520.1 includes the effect of the 24.6 of new money).
- Allocation: new-money providers 30% (value 28.6 for 24.6 invested); senior class 67% (64.0); subordinated lender 3% in warrants (2.9).
- Computed: senior total value 424.7 + 64.0 = 488.6 (71.7% recovery). Value available to existing creditors before new money 495.5; relevant alternative (sale) 450.9; restructuring surplus 44.6, allocated: senior 37.7 (84.5%), new money 4.0 (9.1%), subordinated 2.9 (6.4%). Variant: new money at 35% leaves seniors 483.9 (71.0%) and gives new money 33.4 for 24.6 (8.8 of the surplus, 19.8%).
- Teaching: the surplus, not total value, is what classes fight over; Petrofac shows a court refusing a plan where new money took a disproportionate share; give juniors enough to make the allocation defensible.

Example 64.6 Testing a cross-class cram-down (Illustrative) (`ex:64.6`)
- Same project, English Part 26A plan launched in 2025. Classes: senior (681.6) votes 92% in value in favor; subordinated (112.4) votes against.
- Analysis: Condition A (no member of the dissenting class worse off than in the relevant alternative): the subordinated class gets 2.9 under the plan against nil in the sale, so satisfied. Condition B (75% in value of a class with a genuine economic interest in the relevant alternative approves): the senior class is in the money and approves, so satisfied. Discretion and fair allocation: the subordinated class receives 6.4% of the surplus; the writer shows how a court applying Adler and Petrofac would examine whether that share is justified and whether there was a genuine attempt to negotiate. Contrast with Chapter 11: with seniors impaired, the absolute priority rule allows junior recovery only if the senior class accepts.
- Teaching: the arithmetic of the tests is easy; the fight is over the relevant alternative's value and the allocation of surplus.

## 64-8. Frameworks

- Framework 64.1 Distress early-warning dashboard (`fw:distress-dashboard`), original. Ten indicators in three groups with amber and red thresholds: financial (historic DSCR against lock-up and default levels; projected DSCR; DSRA balance and drawings; liquidity runway in months; payables days; covenant headroom in CFADS terms), commercial (volume or availability against banking case for two consecutive periods; offtaker payment days), and structural (months to a debt maturity or mini-perm step-up without refinancing; sponsor financial health). Rule: two reds or four ambers trigger the first-ninety-days protocol. Demonstrated on Example 64.1 and Case T 2019 to 2020.
- Framework 64.2 Restructure, sell, enforce or terminate (`fw:restructure-sell-enforce-terminate`), original. Decision tree: (1) is the project viable as a going concern at some level of debt? if no, terminate or liquidate; (2) is there a buyer at a price above the restructuring value net of execution risk? if yes, sell (consensually or by enforcement); (3) can the creditors agree among themselves? if yes, consensual restructuring; if no, (4) does a court process with cram-down exist in the governing law and the project's location, and does the plan pass its tests? if yes, use it; if no, (5) enforce over shares at holdco level to change control and then restructure. Each node lists the evidence needed. Exhibit 64.4 draws it in TikZ. Demonstrated on Example 64.3 and on Case T (64.14.4).
- Sustainable-debt sizing (64.7.1) is presented as an application of Ch 36's sculpting method, not as a new framework.

## 64-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (first ninety days): reservation of rights sent; information request issued; advisors appointed with fee letters at borrower cost; coordinating committee formed with clear mandate; trading restrictions and confidentiality agreed; liquidity runway computed weekly; hedge counterparties brought in early; the grantor or offtaker engaged with one voice; standstill terms ready before a payment default.

Red flags: a sponsor that stops answering technical questions; a request to release reserves "temporarily"; a counterparty (offtaker, grantor) changing its own position; an A&E without milestones; a junior creditor with blocking leverage in a court process (springing liens, separate class); hedge liabilities growing as rates move; distressed-debt buyers accumulating a blocking stake.

Rules of thumb and their limits (qualitative practitioner judgment; writer dates and places): sustainable debt is sized on the lenders' advisor's case, not the sponsor's; the first restructuring should leave enough equity cushion to avoid a second (Eurotunnel); asset enforcement in a contract-based business usually destroys value relative to share enforcement; court processes are leverage in negotiations more often than they are the route actually used.

Common mistakes: negotiating haircuts before agreeing the case; forgetting swap close-outs in claims; treating the subordinated public lender as a junior commercial lender; assuming the grantor will pay to avoid termination; allocating all surplus to the class that votes; ignoring tax on debt forgiveness (forward pointer to Ch 67).

Questions experts ask: "What is the relevant alternative, and who can prove its value?"; "Which class can block, and what does it want?"; "How long is the runway?"; "What does the grantor gain from a deal, and what can it give that costs it little?"; "Which court has jurisdiction, and would the plan pass there?"

## 64-12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the senior lenders' financial advisor on the Example 64.3 Wenlow Estuary Crossing in March 2025. The sponsors propose a deal: senior claims rescheduled at par over a longer tenor at a lower margin (no write-down), sponsors inject GBP 15.3 million and keep 100% of the equity, junior lender unaffected. Your analysis shows sustainable debt of GBP 424.7 million at 1.40x and GBP 457.3 million at 1.30x against claims of GBP 681.6 million. The sale alternative recovers 66.2%. The sponsors' offer is valid for 10 days; the coordinating committee meets in four.

Reasoning outline: the decisive facts are that rescheduling at par leaves debt above any sustainable level on the advisor's case (a second default is likely), that the sponsors' 15.3 buys an option on recovery rather than a credit improvement, and that a sale or a debt-for-equity plan gives the seniors more value with less risk. Expert answer: reject the par rescheduling; counter with a debt-for-equity term sheet (Example 64.5 shape) that offers the sponsors a minority stake or warrants for their new money and operational continuity; set a milestone and prepare the Part 26A route in parallel in case the junior or sponsors hold out.

What would change the answer: a credible traffic recovery evidenced by the latest periods (a larger sustainable debt and less equity for lenders); a lender group that cannot hold equity (prefers a sale or more debt with PIK); a grantor willing to contribute term or toll changes only if the sponsors stay (keeps sponsors in with a meaningful stake); a governing law without a cram-down tool (enforcement over holdco shares becomes the fallback).

Close of chapter (64.17): a restructuring settles who owns the project and what it owes until the contracts end; Ch 65 asks what happens when they do end: what the owner must remove, return or keep, and how much of today's value depends on those last years.

## 65-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Every project ends: its contracts expire, its equipment wears out, or its economics fail. What happens then has been written into permits, leases, concessions and PPAs decades earlier, and it carries real money: decommissioning that must be paid for when revenues have stopped, handback conditions that require heavy spending in the last years of a concession, and decisions to extend, repower or retire that decide whether a project's last decade is worth anything at all. This chapter teaches the reader to identify and estimate end-of-life obligations, to choose and size the security for them, to run a handback programme from the contracting authority's or the project company's side, to compare life extension, repowering and retirement, and to measure how much of a project's value rests on its tail. Its installments are the planning of Bélanou's 2046 handback to SEKA and Case R's repower-or-retire question for Thatcher Flats, with the decommissioning obligations of the Mesa Corta portfolio.

What you will be able to do:

- Estimate a project's decommissioning obligation, net of salvage, and choose and size its security (Capabilities 7 and 12).
- Run a handback programme: define the conditions, schedule the surveys and works, and fund them with a reserve, retention or bond (Capabilities 9 and 10).
- Compare life extension, repowering and retirement on value and financeability (Capabilities 12 and 15).
- Measure the share of value that sits beyond the contract or the debt, and challenge terminal-value assumptions in bids and valuations (Capabilities 12 and 14).

## 65-6. Worked examples

Example 65.1 Estimating a wind farm's decommissioning cost (Illustrative) (`ex:65.1`)
- Scenario: a 172.8 MW wind farm of 48 turbines of 3.6 MW in Wyoming, at COD; county permit requires removal of turbines and foundations to one meter below grade and road restoration.
- Inputs (USD, COD-year prices): turbine and tower removal USD 168,400 per turbine; foundation removal USD 61,200 per turbine; roads and collection-system restoration USD 2.94 million; substation removal USD 1.12 million; contingency 10%; salvage of steel, copper and components USD 92,300 per turbine.
- Computed (USD m): turbines 8.083; foundations 2.938; base 15.081; contingency 1.508; gross 16.589; salvage 4.430; net 12.158. In year-25 nominal terms at 2.5% cost inflation: gross 30.75; net 22.54 (salvage assumed to inflate at the same rate; the writer states this simplification and why the lesson survives).
- Teaching: salvage is volatile and regulators often ignore it when sizing security; contingency belongs in the estimate because removal is a construction project; the number in today's money understates the nominal obligation by half over 25 years.

Example 65.2 Sinking fund or surety bond (Illustrative) (`ex:65.2`)
- Same wind farm. Security must cover the gross nominal cost (USD 30.75 million in year 25).
- Option A, sinking fund: equal annual contributions from year 15 to year 25 (11 payments) into a reserve earning 3.0%: contribution USD 2.401 million a year; total paid 26.41; PV at an 8% equity rate 5.84.
- Option B, surety bond on the gross cost escalating at 2.5%: premium 1.1% a year for 25 years: total premiums 6.39; PV of premiums 2.48; plus the cost itself paid from operating cash in year 25 (PV 4.49); total PV 6.97.
- Computed breakeven premium: about 0.60% a year (at which the bond option's PV equals the sinking fund's 5.84). If the reserve earns 4.5%, the contribution falls to 2.222 and its PV to 5.40.
- Formula eq:65.1: $A = \frac{F \times i}{(1+i)^{k} - 1}$ for future obligation $F$, reserve yield $i$ and $k$ contributions. Excel: `=F12*F13/((1+F13)^F14-1)` or `=PMT(F13, F14, 0, -F12)`.
- Teaching: the bond looks cheap year by year but leaves the full cost to be paid when revenues have fallen; the sinking fund costs negative carry but puts cash aside while the asset earns; lenders and regulators weigh the risk that end-of-life cash is not there, not only the PV.

Example 65.3 Funding a toll road's handback works (Illustrative) (`ex:65.3`)
- Scenario: a 30-year toll road concession in Portugal (USD-equivalent figures); handback requires pavement residual life of at least five years at expiry. The survey at T−5 finds pavement works of USD 38.6 million in today's prices, to be carried out in equal thirds in years T−3, T−2 and T−1 (years 3, 4, 5 of the reserve period).
- Inputs: cost inflation 2.8% a year; reserve earns 3.5%; equal annual contributions at the end of each of the five years; works paid at year end.
- Computed: nominal works 13.978 (year 3), 14.369 (year 4), 14.772 (year 5), total 43.12; contribution USD 8.320 million a year; reserve balance at year ends 8.32, 16.93, 11.87, 6.23, 0.00; total contributed 41.60. At a 2.0% reserve yield the contribution is 8.449.
- Teaching: the reserve must be built before the works, in the years when the concessionaire's incentive to spend is weakest; the authority's protections (retention of payments, handback bond, joint survey) and the concessionaire's (agreed scope, dispute resolution by expert) are what turn the clause into money.

Example 65.4 Extend, repower or retire a 20-year-old wind farm (Illustrative) (`ex:65.4`)
- Scenario: a 120 MW wind farm in Iowa at the end of its PPA and design life. Discount rate 8.0%; cost inflation 2.5%.
- Retire now: decommissioning net cost USD 6.9 million. NPV −6.90.
- Extend 10 years: refurbishment capex 14.8; output 290 GWh a year at a merchant capture price of USD 31.20/MWh (revenue 9.048) less opex USD 46/kW-year (5.52), cash flow 3.528 a year; decommission in year 10 at 6.9 inflated. NPV +4.78.
- Repower: 132 MW at USD 1,150/kW (capex 151.8) plus 3.1 to remove the old turbines net of salvage; output 485 GWh a year; 15-year PPA at USD 44.60/MWh (revenue 21.631) then 10 merchant years at USD 30.10/MWh; opex USD 30/kW-year (3.96); cash flow 17.671 a year in the PPA years and 10.639 in the merchant years; decommissioning of the new plant at USD 62/kW inflated over 25 years (15.17). NPV +16.64. Breakeven PPA price at which repowering equals extension: about USD 41.74/MWh.
- Teaching: retirement is never free; extension wins when merchant prices hold and capex is modest; repowering wins when a new contract is available and needs the interconnection and land rights that the old project kept alive. At a merchant price of USD 27.80/MWh the extension's NPV falls to −1.83 (still better than retirement); the breakeven merchant price against retirement is about USD 25.20/MWh.

Example 65.5 How much of the value is in the tail (Illustrative) (`ex:65.5`)
- Scenario: equity cash flows of USD 12.4 million a year for 20 contracted years, then USD 6.1 million a year for 10 merchant years after the PPA ends.
- Computed PV (USD m): at 8%: contracted 121.75, tail 8.78 (6.7% of total); at 10%: 105.57 and 5.57 (5.0%); at 12%: 92.62 and 3.57 (3.7%). Repowering example cross-check: in Example 65.4 the merchant years carry 13.0% of gross PV at 8%.
- Teaching: the tail is small at bid discount rates, which is why bidders who need it to win are stretching; it matters more to long-hold investors at lower rates; lenders give it little credit, so it is equity's risk.

## 65-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (end-of-life review, run every five years and annually from T−7): obligations listed with source and scope; estimate updated with dated unit costs and salvage; security amount, form and escalation checked against the estimate; reserve funding on track; handback conditions mapped to components with current residual life; contract end dates aligned (fuel, LTSA, O&M, land, grid, permits) with the offtake end; options (extend, repower, retire, transfer) refreshed with current prices.

Red flags: supply or service contracts ending before the offtake contract; a security sized on net cost when salvage prices have fallen; reserves that the documents let sponsors release on a ratio test; a concessionaire that has stopped lifecycle spending in the last five years; permits that lapse with the original equipment; interconnection rights that terminate on retirement.

Rules of thumb and their limits (indicative; writer dates and places): decommissioning estimates made at COD understate nominal cost by roughly half over 25 years at 2.5% to 3% inflation (arithmetic, not a market norm); a sinking fund beats a surety bond when the bond premium exceeds the reserve's negative carry; life extension decisions are driven more by the merchant price than by the capex; the tail rarely decides a bid at double-digit discount rates.

Common mistakes: treating BOOT transfer for USD 1 as zero value to the sponsor without valuing the handback cost; ignoring decommissioning in a buyer's valuation of an old asset; relying on salvage at today's commodity prices; letting the handback reserve compete with distributions in the waterfall; assuming the authority will accept a cash settlement instead of works.

Questions experts ask: "Which contract ends first, and what replaces it?"; "Is the security sized on gross or net, and who can call it?"; "What does the handback survey measure, and who chooses the surveyor?"; "What merchant price makes extension worth more than retirement?"; "How much of the bid value comes after the debt and the contract?"

## 65-12. Judgment drill

The situation: you are the contract manager for the contracting authority on the Example 65.3 toll road, five years before expiry. The concessionaire proposes to skip the pavement works and pay the authority USD 30.0 million in cash at expiry instead, citing a lower contractor quote it says the authority could obtain. The handback survey priced the works at USD 38.6 million in today's money (USD 43.12 million nominal over three years); the concessionaire's equity holders are a fund in its final year; the handback bond in the contract is USD 15.0 million.

Reasoning outline: the decisive factors are the gap between the cash offered and the nominal works cost (43.12 against 30.0), the credit risk of a cash promise from a project company with no future revenue, the authority's ability to procure and supervise the works itself after expiry, and the bond's coverage. Expert answer: refuse the settlement as proposed; require either the works programme with the reserve built as in Example 65.3, or a cash settlement paid into an escrow in installments from now, sized to the nominal works cost plus the authority's procurement and supervision costs, with the bond retained until the post-handback defects period ends.

What would change the answer: an authority that plans to reconstruct the road anyway (cash may be worth more than works); an independent estimate at or below USD 30.0 million in nominal terms (accept with escrow); a concessionaire with a creditworthy parent guarantee (installments less needed); a new concession procured to start at expiry whose bidder will price the works (the authority can transfer the job, keep the cash).

Close of chapter (65.11): end-of-life obligations and values are fixed in the first contracts and paid for in the last years; end on the open problem Ch 66 answers: how do these long-dated obligations, the reserves that fund them, the 2026 sale that deconsolidated Bélanou from Kilnworth, and the service-concession structure of a BOOT plant appear in the sponsors' and project company's accounts?
