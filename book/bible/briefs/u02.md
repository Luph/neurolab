## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every later chapter prices something that happens in the future: a tariff paid for 25 years, a loan repaid over 15, a decommissioning bill due in 2046, an equity stake sold in 2026. This chapter gives the reader the arithmetic that turns future money into present decisions: compounding, discounting, annuities, net present value, internal rate of return and its traps, irregular dates, payback, and the difference between real and nominal money, ending with the mechanics of contract indexation that decide whether a 25-year price keeps its value. It is the reader's first full calculation chapter and the reader's first spreadsheet: ssec:5.2.2 teaches cells, relative and absolute references, time across columns and the copy-across check, so that every later formula in the chapter can be built in a blank workbook.

What you will be able to do (2 to 4 items for the chapter opener; binding):

- Discount and compound any cash flow stream, including annuities, growing streams and irregular dates, and build each calculation in a blank Excel workbook (Capabilities 4 and 12).
- Compute and interpret NPV, IRR, MIRR, XIRR and payback, and recognize the four situations in which an IRR misleads (Capabilities 12 and 15).
- Convert between real and nominal values and trace how full, partial, lagged and currency-converted indexation changes the value of a long-term contract price (Capabilities 3 and 4).

## 5. Opening

Real case: Ocean Wind 1 (fact sheet `ocean-wind`). In June 2019 New Jersey's Board of Public Utilities awarded Ørsted's 1,100 MW Ocean Wind 1 an offshore renewable energy certificate price of USD 98.10/MWh in the first year, escalating at 2% a year over 20 years (escalator: medium confidence; say "reported to escalate"). Four years later Ørsted cited a US risk-free rate up approximately 300 basis points since the award, cost inflation and supply delays, stopped development on October 31, 2023, and booked DKK 19.9 billion of impairment on the project in its 2023 accounts (the full-year Ocean Wind 1 figure; never the DKK 28.4 billion nine-month US portfolio figure, per the sheet's "Do not state"). Open on the gap between a price that looked fixed and the two things that moved around it: the purchasing power of the dollars it paid, and the rate at which those dollars were discounted. ## 6. Worked examples

- **Example 5.1 Growing a deposit under different compounding** (`ex:5.1`). Panama, March 2026: Hidroeléctrica Río Caldera SA (fictional), owner of a 64 MW run-of-river plant in Chiriquí, places a USD 12.6 million insurance settlement on a five-year deposit at 4.85% a year with its account bank. Compute simple interest and compound values. Results: simple USD 15.66 million; annual compounding USD 15.97 million; semiannual USD 16.01 million; monthly USD 16.05 million; daily USD 16.06 million; continuous USD 16.06 million. Rule of 72: 14.8 years to double versus exact 14.6 years. Excel (excel block): `=FV(4.85%/2,10,0,-12.6)`; the writer also shows the cell-reference form `=FV(F5/2,F6*2,0,-F7)` with the rate in F5, years in F6 and deposit in F7.
- **Example 5.2 Effective and periodic rates** (`ex:5.2`). Manila, May 2026: a USD loan to Tarlac Sunfields Inc. (fictional, 75 MW solar) is quoted at 6.42% a year paid semiannually: effective annual rate 6.52% (excel block: `=EFFECT(6.42%,2)`); monthly compounding would give 6.61%. The sponsor, Kaliwa Renewables (fictional), uses a 9.25% annual hurdle; as a semiannual periodic rate that is 4.5227% (not 4.625%); doubled, it quotes as 9.045% "semiannual basis". Lesson: always state the compounding basis of a quoted rate.
- **Example 5.3 Present value of a deferred payment** (`ex:5.3`). - **Example 5.9 A mine with a reclamation bill** (`ex:5.9`). Nye County, Nevada, 2026: Toquima Gold LLC (fictional) evaluates a short-life heap-leach pit. Cash flows (USD m): year 0 (52.6); years 1 to 4 +38.4 each; year 5 (106.3) reclamation. ## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 5.1 Four questions before trusting an IRR** (`fw:irr-four-questions`, box in ssec:5.6.5), original. Purpose: decide in one minute whether an IRR can be used to rank or accept. Steps: (1) Do the cash flows change sign more than once? If yes, plot the NPV profile and use NPV. (2) Are the alternatives different in size? If yes, compute the incremental IRR or compare NPVs. (3) Are the alternatives different in timing? If yes, find the crossover rate and compare it with the discount rate. (4) Are large cash flows returned early to an investor who cannot reinvest at the IRR? If yes, report MIRR at a stated reinvestment rate. Demonstrated on Examples 5.9 to 5.12.
- **Framework 5.2 Indexation audit** (`fw:indexation-audit`, box in ssec:5.10.4), original. Purpose: test any indexed price in a contract or model. Steps: (1) Base value and base date. (2) Index name, publisher, region and series; fallback if it changes, is not published or is rebased. (3) Indexed share versus fixed share, and whether that split matches the cost it is meant to track. (4) Lag between index month and application. (5) Reset frequency. (6) Currency of the index versus the currency of the cost and of payment; base exchange rate for any converted share. (7) Caps, floors, collars or fixed escalators. (8) Who bears the residual mismatch: write it down for inflation above and below the base case. Demonstrated on Hinkley Point C (CPI-indexed strike of GBP 92.50/MWh in 2012 prices, reduced to GBP 89.50/MWh in 2012 prices when Sizewell C reached FID, and restated by the NAO at GBP 128.94/MWh in 2025 prices; fact sheet `hinkley-sizewell`, items 4 and 13; the writer prints amounts as "GBP", never "£", and never states that the strike is still GBP 92.50/MWh) and on Ocean Wind 1 (fixed escalator, no capex or rate indexation).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 5.14)

Checklist:
- State every rate with its compounding basis, day count and whether it is real or nominal, pre- or post-tax.
- Confirm that cash flows and discount rate share currency, inflation basis and risk.
- Check the Excel NPV range excludes year 0.
- Check every input sits in one cell and every formula references it with an absolute reference; click the last column of each row to confirm the copy-across.
- Plot the NPV profile whenever cash flows change sign more than once.
- Use XIRR when cash flows fall on real dates; state the annualization convention for periodic IRRs.
- Run the indexation audit (Framework 5.2) on every indexed revenue and cost line.

Red flags:
- An IRR quoted without its cash flow profile or date basis.
- A model that indexes revenue at CPI but holds opex flat (or the reverse) without a contractual reason.
- A real discount rate applied to nominal cash flows in a bid model (overstates value by double-digit percentages; Example 5.16).
- A local-currency indexed share described as "inflation-protected" in USD terms.
- A levelized tariff comparison that hides very different early-year prices.
- A number typed inside a formula (`=C7*1.025`) instead of a referenced input.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Rule of 72 for doubling time: within about 0.35 years of the exact answer between 4% and 12% (at 8% it gives 9.0 years against 9.01); at 20% it gives 3.6 years against 3.8.
- Fisher approximation (real ≈ nominal − inflation): fine below about 5% inflation; use the exact form in high-inflation economies (Kessara's 2023 inflation of 34.8% is an example from the Case Bible, Section 1.1).
- Payback under one third of contract tenor is a comfortable screen for contracted assets; meaningless for comparing projects of different lives.

Common mistakes:
- Mixing annual rates with semiannual periods; doubling a semiannual IRR and calling it annual without saying so.
- Treating a fixed escalator as inflation protection.
- Using the IRR guess argument to "find" the IRR you want when two exist.
- Discounting a growing perpetuity with growth close to the discount rate.
- Forgetting the dollar signs on the rate cell, so every copied discount factor after the first is wrong.

Questions experts ask:
- "Is that IRR on dates or on periods, and nominal or real?"
- "What happens to this price if inflation runs 2 points above the base case for five years?"
- "Which index, which month, which publisher, and what happens if it is rebased or not published?"
- "If you remove the terminal value, what is left?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 5.15)

Situation (second person, deadline): You are the financial adviser to Sierra Madre Eólica (fictional sponsor) on a 20-year USD-denominated PPA for an illustrative 96.6 MW wind farm in Coahuila, Mexico, producing 262.4 GWh a year (P50), selling to Grupo Cementero del Bravo (fictional industrial offtaker), November 2026. The sponsor's required real return is 5.5%. The debt will be fixed-rate in USD; most opex is indexed to CPI.

Computed figures (nominal discount rate = (1.055)(1 + inflation) − 1):
- Inflation 1.5%: PV of revenue A USD 197.13 million, B USD 181.56 million (A ahead by 15.57).
- Inflation 2.5%: A 180.92, B 181.56 (B ahead by 0.64).
- Inflation 4.0%: A 160.19, B 181.56 (B ahead by 21.37).
- Breakeven inflation 2.46%. Option A's year-20 price in real terms: USD 66.41/MWh at 1.5% inflation, 54.59 at 2.5%, 40.82 at 4.0%; Option B stays at USD 57.90/MWh real.

Reasoning outline: B's PV is the same at any inflation because it is fully real; A is a bet that inflation stays below about 2.5%. The sponsor's costs are mostly indexed, so A creates a real margin squeeze in high-inflation years; but fixed-rate nominal debt service is better covered by A's higher early nominal revenue (lenders may prefer A's early years and dislike B's lower year-1 cash). The expert answer: take B unless the debt can be sized and hedged on a nominal basis that B's lower early revenue cannot support, or unless the sponsor holds an explicit view on low inflation; a third way is to ask for a partial indexation (for example 60% CPI with a higher starting price) that matches the indexed share of costs. Present lender, sponsor and offtaker lenses.

What would change the answer: debt that is CPI-linked (favors B); opex fixed under long-term contracts (weakens B's advantage); a sponsor planning to sell in year 5 to a buyer who prices on real cash flows (favors B); an offtaker whose own revenues are in pesos and track Mexican rather than US inflation (Chapter 59).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A project financing is mostly debt, and debt is a contract about principal, interest, fees and dates. This chapter teaches the reader how a loan works from the lender's ledger outward: what is borrowed and when, how interest accrues under each day-count convention, how principal comes back (bullet, annuity, straight-line, and in outline sculpted), the difference between fixed and floating rates, what a reference rate is and why LIBOR died, how SOFR, SONIA, €STR and EURIBOR are set and applied (term rates in advance, overnight rates compounded in arrears), what a margin in basis points means, how a bond is priced, and how an interest rate swap converts floating to fixed, what it is worth after rates move, and how it can turn from protection into a liability. The reader leaves able to read the interest and hedging lines of a term sheet and to compute every cash flow they imply.

What you will be able to do (binding):

## 5. Opening

Real case: Indiana Toll Road (fact sheet `indiana-toll-road`). In 2006 the concessionaire paid approximately USD 3.8 billion for a 75-year lease and financed it with a nine-year interest-only bullet bank loan hedged by a 20-year accreting interest rate swap whose pre-set rates rose from 3% to 11.3% by 2023 (medium confidence: say "according to a later industry case study"). When rates fell after 2008 the swap's value moved against the concessionaire; at its September 2014 Chapter 11 filing the swap liability was about USD 2.15 billion within secured claims of about USD 6 billion (high confidence). Open on the instrument bought to remove interest rate risk becoming a claim more than half the size of the original debt, then ask what a swap is and why its value moves. ## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 6.12)

Checklist: base rate, setting method and lookback; credit adjustment spread for legacy tranches; floor; day count; interest period and payment-date alignment with the model; commitment fee base; hedge notional versus projected balance; swap index versus loan index; breakage provisions.

Red flags: a hedge whose notional exceeds the loan balance at any date (over-hedging becomes speculation and a lender-ranked claim); a floored loan hedged with an unfloored swap; an accreting swap against a short bullet loan; a model that applies an annual rate to semiannual periods without a day count; a term sheet quoting "SOFR" without saying which one.

Rules of thumb and their limits: 1 bp a year on USD 100 million is USD 10,000 a year (Actual/360 adds 1.4%); the annuity profile's WAL is a little above half the tenor at mid-single-digit rates (6.0 years on a 10-year loan at 6.35%), and rises with the rate; a swap's MTM is roughly (change in par rate) × remaining annuity × notional, valid for parallel moves only.

Common mistakes: treating a forward curve as a forecast; comparing swap quotes without asking what charge over mid each includes (Chapter 38); quoting all-in cost without fees; mixing Actual/365 and Actual/360 rates in one comparison.

Questions experts ask: "Which rate does the loan reference, which does the swap reference, and who pays for the difference?"; "What is the breakage on a prepayment in year 5 if rates fall 150 bps?"; "Is the hedge counterparty pari passu, and does it vote?"; "What happens to this clause if Term SOFR is not published for a week?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 6.13)

Situation: You are CFO of Solaria Manchega SL (fictional sponsor) for an illustrative 214 MW solar plant in Castilla-La Mancha, Spain, with a EUR 186.4 million, 17-year straight-line amortizing loan at 6M EURIBOR + 1.65% from a club of Spanish and French banks, October 2026. The term sheet requires at least 75% of the balance hedged. The hedge bank quotes a 17-year amortizing payer swap at a mid rate of 2.71% plus 12 bps (all-in 2.83%). Your board meets Thursday and asks whether to hedge 75%, 90% or 100%. Average outstanding balance over the life (opening balances): EUR 98.68 million.

Computed figures (average annual base-rate cost on the average balance, EUR million, assuming floating EURIBOR averages the swap mid plus a parallel move): hedge 75%: 2.516 if rates average 1 point lower, 2.763 flat, 3.257 if 2 points higher; hedge 90%: 2.682, 2.781, 2.978; hedge 100%: 2.793 in every case. The extra 15 points of hedging cost about EUR 0.018 million a year in charge over mid when rates are flat.

Reasoning: the cost of more hedging is small (the charge), the benefit is protection against a scenario that would also hit inflation-indexed opex; revenue is partly merchant or partly CfD-indexed (state the assumption), so the correlation of revenue with rates decides. Expert answer: hedge 90% for the contracted period and leave the unhedged 10% as headroom for prepayments and sweeps that would otherwise over-hedge; consider a cap on the tail if the loan may be refinanced (breakage risk). Present the lender's view (minimum 75%, prefers higher), the hedge bank's view (credit line usage, ranking), and the sponsor's view (breakage at refinancing).

What would change the answer: a likely refinancing within five years (hedge shorter or less, because breakage could be large); revenue indexed to inflation with rates correlated to inflation (supports less hedging); a cash sweep that accelerates repayment (lower hedge ratio to avoid over-hedging); a negative-rate environment with a loan floor (requires a floored swap).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Lenders lend against cash, but they receive audited accounts, test covenants that reference them, pay tax computed from them, and see distributions blocked by them. The reader must therefore read a project company's financial statements fluently and know exactly where accounting profit and cash part company. This chapter builds the three statements from the accounting equation, links them, and then teaches the items that dominate a project company's accounts: depreciation of a single large asset, interest capitalized during construction, deferred tax (including tax holidays), working capital and receivables from a weak offtaker, provisions such as decommissioning, the presentation of shareholder loans and restricted cash, and the handful of tax rules (withholding, thin capitalization, interest caps, loss expiry) that turn accounting profit into cash tax. It ends with a disciplined method for reading a set of project accounts and with Bélanou Power's first full-year accounts.

What you will be able to do (binding):

- Build and link an income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement for a project company, and reconcile profit to cash (Capabilities 4 and 10).
- Explain and compute depreciation, capitalized interest, deferred tax, working capital, provisions and the cash tax that withholding, thin capitalization and interest caps produce, and say how each affects cash and distributions (Capabilities 4 and 12).
- Read a project company's audited accounts and identify the signals a lender or buyer should act on (Capabilities 10, 11 and 14).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned

1. The accounting equation; double entry at the level needed to follow a transaction through the statements (debits and credits named, not drilled).
2. The income statement: revenue, operating costs, EBITDA, depreciation and amortization, EBIT, interest, profit before tax, tax, net income.
3. Accrual accounting versus cash accounting; revenue recognition when earned; receivables, payables, prepayments, accruals.
4. The balance sheet: non-current assets (property, plant and equipment, construction in progress, intangibles), current assets (inventory, receivables, cash, restricted cash), equity (share capital, retained earnings), liabilities (senior debt with current portion, shareholder loans, payables, provisions, deferred tax, derivative liabilities).
5. The cash flow statement (indirect method): operating, investing, financing; classification choices for interest paid under IFRS.
6. How the three statements link; the balance check.
7. Depreciation and amortization: cost, useful life, residual value, straight-line, units-of-production, declining balance; component depreciation; book versus tax depreciation.
8. Impairment at project-company level: carrying amount, recoverable amount, value in use (uses Chapter 5 discounting).
9. Capitalized borrowing costs: interest during construction (IDC) and capitalized fees in the cost of the asset; IAS 23 principle; effect on later depreciation and tax (R-008). Concession accounting is not taught here (R-016; forward reference to sec:66.4 in Section 7.13 only).
10. Deferred tax: temporary differences, deferred tax liabilities from accelerated tax depreciation, deferred tax assets from tax losses and deferred deductions (tax holidays), measurement at the rate expected on reversal.
11. Working capital in accounts: receivables days, payables days, inventory (spares, fuel), and the cash tied up when an offtaker pays late; expected credit loss named in one sentence with a forward reference (Chapter 66 owns IFRS 9 ECL).
12. Provisions and contingent liabilities (accounting sense): decommissioning provision at present value and its unwinding; onerous contracts; contingent liabilities by disclosure.
13. Reading a project company's accounts: audit opinion and going-concern language, accounting basis and functional currency, the lenders' reporting basis defined in the finance documents as distinct from the statutory or IFRS accounts, restricted cash, related-party transactions (shareholder loans, affiliate O&M), debt and covenant notes, subsequent events.
14. Accounting-based limits on distributions (distributable reserves) in principle, with the dividend trap named and forward-referenced to Chapter 42 (ssec:42.5.2).
15. The tax rules that reach a project's cash (ssec:7.11.4): taxable income computed from accounting profit (add-backs, tax depreciation in place of book depreciation, deductible interest); tax loss carryforward and expiry; withholding tax on interest and dividends paid to foreign shareholders and lenders, and who bears it; thin capitalization (related-party debt above a ratio to equity, interest on the excess disallowed); an earnings-based interest limitation (net interest capped at a percentage of tax EBITDA, with or without carryforward of the disallowed amount). One illustrative rule set; real regimes are dated and placed only as the fact sheets verify (the EU's 30% EBITDA cap under ATAD from 2019 and the UK's 30% corporate interest restriction, `t-interest-limitation` items 6 and 13; the OECD Model's caps of 5% and 15% on dividends and 10% on interest, `t-tax-treaties-wht` items 1 and 4), each in one sentence with a forward reference to Chapter 67.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 7.14)

Checklist: Framework 7.1 steps as a checklist; reconcile EBITDA to operating cash flow; reconcile revenue to contract terms; tie debt balances to the facility agent's statements; tie restricted cash to the accounts agreement; confirm the tax holiday dates, the deferred deduction balance, losses and their expiry dates, and any interest disallowed and carried forward; check which accounting basis each covenant uses.

Red flags: receivables days rising faster than revenue; emphasis of matter on going concern or a dispute; capitalized costs continuing after COD; derivative liabilities large relative to equity; related-party fees rising without contractual basis; a deferred tax asset recognized on losses that the tax code lets expire (Case P: five years); interest disallowed under thin capitalization year after year (it never comes back); auditor change late in a year.

Rules of thumb and their limits: in a stable contracted project, operating cash flow before working capital is close to EBITDA minus cash tax (breaks when receivables move); depreciation of a single-asset company is close to cost over contract life (breaks with component accounting and impairment); EBITDA multiples from corporate deals mislead for finite-life assets; cash tax in a project's early years is far below the headline rate times book profit (Example 7.12: 10.7%), until holidays end and losses run out.

Common mistakes: reading net income as cash available to shareholders; double-counting IDC as both capitalized and expensed in a model; forgetting that shareholder loan interest is an expense that may be partly non-deductible under thin capitalization (ssec:7.11.4) and bears withholding when paid abroad; treating restricted cash as available.

Questions experts ask: "Show me receivables by age and by counterparty"; "What is capitalized in PP&E, line by line?"; "When does the tax holiday end, what deferred deductions remain, and when do the losses expire?"; "Where are the swaps on the balance sheet and what did they cost this year?"; "Which accounting basis do the covenants use?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 7.15)

Situation: You are the credit analyst at Kettleby Bank (fictional), facility agent for an illustrative 380 MW gas-fired plant in the Republic of Corredana (the fictional dollarized country of Chapter 1's deal), owned by Termoeléctrica Cerro Guanaco SA (fictional), whose 20-year USD PPA with ELNACOR (fictional state offtaker) runs to 2041 and transfers the plant for USD 1 at expiry. PP&E is USD 437.9 million, depreciated straight-line over 20 years (USD 21.90 million a year). Retained earnings are negative because of early losses, so Corredanan company law (illustrative assumption, stated) blocks dividends even though cash is building in the distribution account. On Wednesday, October 7, 2026 the sponsor proposes to extend the depreciation life to 30 years (USD 14.60 million a year, raising book profit by USD 7.30 million a year) and asks the lenders to "note" the change before the board meeting on Friday.

Reasoning outline: the change alters no cash flow and no lender ratio built on cash; it may be legitimate if the useful life really is 30 years (the plant's technical life, contract life and handback terms decide); it is cosmetic here, because the PPA ends in 2041 and the asset transfers for USD 1 (a BOOT plant cannot have a book life longer than its use to the owner, unless a residual value is justified). Tax depreciation is separate and unaffected unless local law ties tax to book. The lenders' interest: dividends paid from accounting profit that the plant has not earned in cash terms could breach distribution conditions in spirit; but the lenders' protection is the distribution test (Chapter 37, ssec:37.4.1), not depreciation policy. Expert answer: do not object to an auditor-approved change consistent with technical life and contract life; do insist that distributions remain subject to the cash-based tests and that the change is disclosed; because the PPA term ends before the new life, object and propose the shareholder-loan route (forward reference to ssec:42.5.3).

What would change the answer: a PPA extension option and an IE report supporting a 30-year life (accept); local law allowing capital reduction to clear the deficit (prefer that route); a covenant that references accounting net worth (the change then affects compliance, so lenders must consent).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Project finance exists largely because debt is cheaper than equity and a ring-fenced, contracted project can carry far more of it than a company could. This chapter explains why: how debt and equity divide a project's outcomes, how leverage lifts the expected equity return and widens its spread, why in a frictionless world that trade would be a wash (Modigliani and Miller), what breaks the wash (tax, distress costs, agency and information costs), and how investors price risk through the capital asset pricing model, the cost of debt, the weighted average cost of capital and adjusted present value. It ends with why project finance sustains gearing of 70% to 90% and what that leverage costs the sponsor in downside exposure and control. The chapter supplies the discount rates that Chapter 5 took as given.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 8.11)

Checklist: state whether a return is project or equity, pre- or post-tax, nominal or real, and in which currency; check the hurdle rises with gearing; check the tax shield is usable in each year (holiday, thin capitalization, interest cap); check the cost-of-equity inputs (risk-free rate tenor, currency and date, beta source and gearing, market risk premium source, variant and date, country risk premium source); run the base and a downside at each gearing.

Red flags: an equity IRR rising with gearing compared with an unchanged hurdle; a WACC at initial gearing applied to a project whose debt amortizes; a country risk premium added on top of insured political risk; betas taken from diversified utilities and relevered to 80% without a debt beta; a bid that only clears the hurdle at maximum gearing; a market risk premium quoted without its source and date.

Rules of thumb and their limits: each 5 points of gearing added near 75% raises base equity IRR by roughly 1.5 to 2 points in a long contracted asset with level debt and no tax (Example 8.3: 70% to 75% adds 1.5 points, 75% to 80% adds 2.1) and the effect grows with gearing; the downside IRR rises much less (0.9 and 1.2 points); the rule fails when debt cost rises with gearing, and it fails badly in a real project with fees, reserves and sculpted debt (Case P's P-F05 adds 0.5 points from 75% to 80%).

Common mistakes: discounting equity cash flows at a WACC; using the risk-free rate of one currency with cash flows in another; treating a lower WACC from more debt as free value; forgetting that shareholder loans are equity for return purposes but debt for tax (ssec:8.2.1, ssec:7.11.4; Chapters 32 and 67).

Questions experts ask: "What gearing does the bid need to win, and what happens to the equity at the downside?"; "Whose risk premium is this: ours, the market's, or the offtaker's?"; "If rates rise 150 bps before close, what gearing do we lose and what return do we keep?"; "Which date, which tenor, which premium?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 8.12)

Situation: You lead the sponsor team of Harlow Vantage Energy (fictional) bidding an illustrative 132 MW wind farm in the Gulf of Suez region of Egypt, with a 25-year USD-denominated PPA with the state transmission company (illustrative terms), March 2027. The plant costs USD 163.9 million, with pre-financing cash flow of USD 17.42 million in year 1 growing 1.2% a year for 25 years; debt at 6.40% fixed over 17 years (annuity) from a club of DFIs and Gulf banks (illustrative). Your CFO wants 80% gearing to lift the equity IRR above the 16.0% hurdle the investment committee meets on Monday; lenders have offered up to 80%.

Computed figures: project IRR 10.70%. At 70% gearing: debt service USD 11.27 million; equity IRR 15.97% base, 12.88% with cash flows 10% lower, 11.36% with 15% lower; year-1 cash after debt service 6.15 (base); cash covers debt service 1.55 times (base). At 80% gearing: debt service USD 12.88 million; equity IRR 18.61%, 14.43%, 12.46%; year-1 cash after debt service 4.54; cover 1.35 times (base), 1.22 times (10% lower), 1.15 times (15% lower). Cash flow can fall 35.3% in year 1 before it fails to cover debt service at 70% gearing, but only 26.1% at 80%. (Cover figures are the intuitive Chapter 1 ratio; one sentence forward reference to sec:35.2.)

Reasoning outline (Framework 8.1): the 2.6-point base uplift is real but Proposition II says the equity is riskier, so the hurdle should rise too; at 80% the downside cover approaches levels at which lenders block distributions (forward reference to sec:37.4); the bid needs the lower tariff that higher gearing allows only if competitors also gear at 80%. Expert answer: bid at the gearing the lenders will hold through due diligence on the downside case (often below the offered maximum), set the hurdle explicitly for that gearing, and do not use gearing to close a gap between the bid and the hurdle; if the bid fails at 75%, the problem is the bid price or the cost base. Show the lender's view (80% only with tight distribution tests and sweeps), the offtaker's (lower tariff), and the host government's (a thinly capitalized sponsor is a renegotiation risk).

What would change the answer: a fixed-price, long-dated debt market that will hold 80% to maturity with no refinancing (favors 80%); merchant exposure in the tail (favors less); a tax holiday that removes the tax shield (reduces the gain from debt); a competitor field known to bid with 85% gearing (forces a choice between walking away and accepting a lower return).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 9.1 P-value reading checklist** (`fw:pvalue-reading`, box in sec:9.10), original. Purpose: interpret any P-value before using it. Steps: (1) which quantity (energy, revenue, traffic, cost); (2) which period basis (one-year, ten-year, life); (3) which uncertainties are included and which are not (availability, curtailment, degradation, price); (4) which distribution, and whether the P90 and P99 are mutually consistent under it; (5) whether the P50 is a median or a mean (they differ for skewed outcomes); (6) correlation with price and with other assets; (7) who produced it and for whom (bidder, lender, independent). Demonstrated in the walkthrough and the Case R section.
- **Framework 9.2 Choosing the uncertainty tool** (`fw:uncertainty-tool-choice`, box in ssec:9.8.4), original. Purpose: pick sensitivity, scenario or Monte Carlo analysis for a decision. Rules: use sensitivities to find which inputs matter; use scenarios to test coherent stories that lenders and committees can debate; use Monte Carlo when several uncertain inputs interact, correlations are known, and the decision depends on a tail probability; never use Monte Carlo without stating the distributions and correlations, and never present its output as more precise than its inputs. ## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 9.12)

Checklist: Framework 9.1 for every P-value; confirm the period basis on every P90 in a term sheet; confirm independence before combining uncertainties; ask for the correlation assumption in every portfolio number; check that downside scenarios are coherent and labeled; record Monte Carlo seeds, trial counts and distributions.

Red flags: a P90 without a period basis; a P99 inconsistent with the stated P90 and no explanation; uncertainty components summed instead of combined; a portfolio P90 equal to the sum of asset P90s (implies perfect correlation) or far above it (implies independence that weather does not deliver for neighboring wind farms); a Monte Carlo that reports results to four significant figures from guessed inputs; a bid traffic forecast above every independent forecast.

Rules of thumb and their limits: for a normal outcome, P90 ≈ P50 × (1 − 1.28σ) and P99 ≈ P50 × (1 − 2.33σ); ten-year σ is mostly the long-term component once N exceeds about 10; in this book's examples and in Case R's inputs, one-year σ runs at roughly 8% to 10% of P50 for onshore wind and 4% to 5% for tracking solar (the writer presents this as the book's illustrative calibration, not a market statistic, and tells the reader that real values come from each yield report); limits: offshore, complex terrain and short data records widen σ, and none of these rules holds for prices or traffic.

Common mistakes: reading P90 as "90% of P50"; using a ten-year P90 to judge debt service in one year; ignoring curtailment and availability uncertainty; treating the expected value of a skewed cost as the most likely value; adding correlated risks as if independent.

Questions experts ask: "One-year or ten-year?"; "What is in the σ and what is outside it?"; "What correlation did you assume between these assets, and from what data?"; "What happens to revenue in the hours when the wind does not blow?"; "Where does the winning forecast sit among the others?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 9.13)

Situation: You are the analyst at Nordhavn Infrastruktur (fictional sponsor) finalizing a bid model for the fictional 150 MW Skovlund wind farm in Denmark, due to the investment committee in three days (November 2026). Two energy yield assessments disagree: Consultant A gives P50 427.3 GWh with one-year σ 11.2%; Consultant B gives P50 412.0 GWh with one-year σ 8.4%. The bid team wants A's P50 in the price; the lenders' independent engineer has seen both.

Computed figures: one-year P90: A 366.0 GWh, B 367.6 GWh; one-year P99: A 316.0 GWh, B 331.5 GWh. A's higher P50 comes with a wider spread, so the two reports nearly agree on the P90 and A is more pessimistic at the P99.

Reasoning outline: the bid price depends on P50 (equity view); the debt depends on P90 and P99 (lenders' view), where the reports agree or A is worse; so using A's P50 raises the equity value and lowers nothing the lenders will use, which is why bidders like it and why it is the most likely source of a winner's curse (Section 9.9). Expert answer: bid on a P50 between the two only if the difference can be traced to a specific modeling choice that one consultant got right (for example, the long-term correction period or wake from a planned neighbor); otherwise use B's P50 for the price, A's σ for the downside, and ask both consultants to reconcile; tell the committee the price assumes the lower P50.

What would change the answer: an on-site measurement campaign that supports A's long-term correction (move toward A); a planned neighboring wind farm upwind (move toward B and widen σ); a revenue contract paying on deemed energy for curtailment (reduces the volume risk that matters); a fixed-volume hedge (raises the cost of a low-output year: Chapter 20).
