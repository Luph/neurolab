## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every later chapter prices something that happens in the future: a tariff paid for 25 years, a loan repaid over 15, a decommissioning bill due in 2046, an equity stake sold in 2026. This chapter gives the reader the arithmetic that turns future money into present decisions: compounding, discounting, annuities, net present value, internal rate of return and its traps, irregular dates, payback, and the difference between real and nominal money, ending with the mechanics of contract indexation that decide whether a 25-year price keeps its value. It is the reader's first full calculation chapter, so every formula appears with its Excel implementation and every concept with a worked number.

What you will be able to do (2 to 4 items for the chapter opener):

- Discount and compound any cash flow stream, including annuities, growing streams and irregular dates, and implement each calculation in Excel (Capabilities 4 and 12).
- Compute and interpret NPV, IRR, MIRR, XIRR and payback, and recognize the four situations in which an IRR misleads (Capabilities 12 and 15).
- Convert between real and nominal values and trace how full, partial, lagged and currency-converted indexation changes the value of a long-term contract price (Capabilities 3 and 4).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned (taught in full here)

1. Interest as the price of time; simple versus compound interest; compounding frequency; nominal annual rate versus effective annual rate; continuous compounding (one paragraph, for reading only); the rule of 72 and its limit.

3. Annuities (ordinary and due), growing annuities, perpetuities, growing perpetuities; annuity factor; Excel PV, FV, PMT, RATE, NPER.
4. Levelized value: converting an uneven cost or revenue stream into a level (or level-escalating) price per unit; levelized cost and levelized tariff (see change request CR-u02-1).

6. Internal rate of return: definition, iteration, relationship to the NPV profile, Excel IRR and its guess argument.
7. IRR traps: multiple IRRs and no IRR (non-conventional cash flows); the reinvestment assumption and MIRR; scale effects and incremental IRR; timing effects and the crossover rate.
8. XNPV and XIRR for irregular dates; Excel's Actual/365 basis; annualizing periodic IRRs (semiannual IRR times two versus compounded).
9. Payback period and discounted payback; their uses and limits.
10. Inflation; price indices; base dates and rebasing; real versus nominal values and discount rates; the Fisher relation; the consistency rule (nominal with nominal, real with real).
11. Indexation mechanics: full and partial indexation, fixed escalators, indexation lags, reset frequency, multi-index formulas, local-currency shares converted at a base exchange rate and reconverted, caps, floors and collars on indexation, and who bears the residual mismatch between the index and the actual cost.

## 6. Worked examples

All Illustrative unless stated. Numbers below were computed in Python; writers recompute.

- **Example 5.1 Growing a deposit under different compounding** (`ex:5.1`). USD 12.6 million placed for 5 years at 4.85% a year. Compute simple interest and compound values. Results: simple USD 15.66 million; annual compounding USD 15.97 million; semiannual USD 16.01 million; monthly USD 16.05 million; daily USD 16.06 million; continuous USD 16.06 million. Rule of 72: 14.8 years to double versus exact 14.6 years. Excel: `=FV(4.85%/2,10,0,-12.6)`.
- **Example 5.2 Effective and periodic rates** (`ex:5.2`). A loan quoted at 6.42% a year paid semiannually: EAR 6.52% (`=EFFECT(6.42%,2)`); monthly compounding would give 6.61%. A sponsor's 9.25% annual hurdle as a semiannual periodic rate: 4.5227% (not 4.625%); doubled, it quotes as 9.045% "semiannual basis". Lesson: always state the compounding basis of a quoted rate.
- **Example 5.3 Present value of a deferred payment** (`ex:5.3`). USD 18.6 million receivable in 4 years discounted at 9.25%: discount factor 0.70196, PV USD 13.06 million. Exhibit 5.1 lists DFs at 9.25%: 0.91533, 0.83783, 0.76689, 0.70196, 0.64253 (years 1 to 5).
- **Example 5.4 Valuing a land lease** (`ex:5.4`). USD 1.27 million a year for 20 years, paid at year end, discounted at 8.4%: annuity factor 9.53265, PV USD 12.11 million; paid in advance (annuity due) USD 13.12 million; as a perpetuity USD 15.12 million. Excel: `=PV(8.4%,20,-1.27)` and `=PV(8.4%,20,-1.27,0,1)`.
- **Example 5.5 An escalating O&M fee** (`ex:5.5`). USD 3.42 million in year 1 growing 2.5% a year for 18 years, discounted at 8.4%: PV USD 36.80 million (formula and year-by-year sum agree); as a growing perpetuity USD 57.97 million. Shows the closed form and the row-by-row spreadsheet check.
- **Example 5.6 Levelized cost of a solar plant** (`ex:5.6`). Capex USD 41.8 million at year 0; opex USD 0.73 million in year 1 growing 2.5%; output 104.2 GWh in year 1 degrading 0.45% a year; 25 years; nominal discount rate 7.6%. PV of costs USD 51.86 million (of which opex USD 10.06 million); PV of energy 1,109.1 GWh (discounted energy, explained as a device, not a physical quantity); flat levelized price USD 46.76/MWh. If the price instead escalates 2% a year, the year-1 levelized price is USD 39.41/MWh. Teaching point: two bids with the same levelized value can have very different early cash flows.
- **Example 5.7 NPV of a gas-engine life extension** (`ex:5.7`). Outlay USD 31.7 million at year 0; net cash inflows years 1 to 8: 5.84, 6.02, 6.19, 6.37, 6.55, 6.74, 6.93, 7.12, plus salvage of 2.40 in year 8 (USD m). NPV at 9.0%: USD 4.86 million. NPV profile (Exhibit 5.2): 0% 22.46; 5% 11.44; 9% 4.86; 12% 0.88; 15% (2.47). IRR 12.74%.
- **Example 5.8 The Excel NPV first-period trap** (`ex:5.8`). Applying `=NPV(9%,...)` to the whole row including year 0 returns USD 4.46 million, which is the true NPV divided by 1.09. Correct form `=B5+NPV(9%,C5:J5)`.
- **Example 5.9 A mine with a reclamation bill** (`ex:5.9`). Cash flows (USD m): year 0 (52.0); years 1 to 4 +38.0 each; year 5 (105.0) reclamation. Two IRRs: 4.42% and 36.39%; NPV is negative below 4.42% (year 0 to 5 sum is (5.0)), positive between, negative above (NPV at 10% is +3.26; at 40% is (1.25)). MIRR with 8% finance and reinvestment rates: 8.42%. Show the two-sign-change rule (Descartes) in one sentence.
- **Example 5.10 Reinvestment and MIRR** (`ex:5.10`). Cash flows (USD m): (42.5), 19.8, 17.6, 14.1, 9.3. IRR 18.41%. Inflows compounded to year 4 at 8% total USD 70.00 million; MIRR 13.29%. At the IRR as reinvestment rate the terminal value is USD 83.54 million and MIRR equals the IRR.
- **Example 5.11 Scale: the smaller project with the higher IRR** (`ex:5.11`). A: (10.0) then 3.2 a year for 5 years; B: (60.0) then 16.5 a year for 5 years; discount rate 8%. A: NPV 2.78, IRR 18.03%. B: NPV 5.88, IRR 11.65%. Incremental cash flow B minus A: NPV 3.10, IRR 10.33%, above 8%, so B is the better use of capital if the two are exclusive.
- **Example 5.12 Timing and the crossover rate** (`ex:5.12`). C: (50.0), 24.0, 19.0, 14.0, 8.0, 4.0. D: (50.0), 4.0, 9.0, 15.0, 24.0, 31.0. IRR C 16.11%, D 14.54%. NPV at 8%: C 8.23, D 12.07; at 15%: C 1.00, D (0.72). Crossover 12.49%. Exhibit 5.4 plots both profiles (0% to 15%).
- **Example 5.13 XIRR on an equity investor's dated cash flows** (`ex:5.13`). Contributions: 2024-03-15 (7.85); 2024-09-30 (12.40); 2025-06-18 (9.65). Distributions: 2026-06-30 2.10; 2026-12-31 3.35; 2027-06-30 3.48; 2027-12-31 3.61; 2028-12-31 7.42; 2029-12-31 7.70; 2030-12-31 7.96; 2031-12-31 8.25; 2032-12-31 8.51 plus sale proceeds 21.40 (USD m). Net cash USD 43.88 million. XIRR 16.84%; XNPV at 11% USD 9.23 million; the naive IRR that treats each row as one year gives 12.81%, understating by about four percentage points. - **Example 5.14 Payback and discounted payback** (`ex:5.14`). On Example 5.7: cumulative cash turns positive in year 6 (cumulative (0.73) at year 5, +6.01 at year 6), so payback is about 5.1 years by interpolation; discounted at 9% the cumulative turns positive in year 7 (+0.08), discounted payback about 7.0 years.
- **Example 5.15 Real and nominal give the same answer if you are consistent** (`ex:5.15`). Nominal rate 9.4%, inflation 2.6%: exact real rate 6.628% (approximation 6.8%). A cash flow of USD 4.15 million a year in today's prices for 10 years: PV of real flows at the real rate USD 29.66 million equals PV of nominal flows (4.15 × 1.026^t) at 9.4%. Mismatch errors: real flows at the nominal rate USD 26.17 million (understates by 11.8%); nominal flows at the real rate USD 33.79 million (overstates by 13.9%).
- **Example 5.16 Rebasing an index to a contract base date** (`ex:5.16`). Illustrative index values (not real CPI): 255.548 at the base month, 274.310 thirty months later and 323.976 at month 78; rebased to 100.0 at the base month: 107.34 and 126.78. Teach the base-month convention and the three-month lag; Excel `=C7/$C$5*100`. The writer labels the index values Illustrative and must not present them as a real CPI series.
- **Example 5.17 How partial indexation erodes a price** (`ex:5.17`). Capacity charge USD 12.84/kW-month, 25% indexed to CPI, inflation 3.0% a year. Year 10: nominal 13.94, real 10.38 (real loss 19.2%); year 20: nominal 15.43, real 8.54 (real loss 33.5%). Fully indexed: nominal 17.26 (year 10) and 23.19 (year 20), real constant at 12.84. Exhibit 5.6: nominal and real paths for 0%, 25% and 100% indexation at years 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 (writer computes the intermediate points).
- **Example 5.18 A local-currency share under parity and under overshoot** (`ex:5.18`). Fixed charge USD 4.62/kW-month, of which 35% (USD 1.617) is a local share converted at a base rate of 380.0 local units per USD, indexed to local CPI at 12% a year, reconverted after 3 years. Under purchasing-power parity (US inflation 2.5%), the rate moves to 495.75 and the local share is worth USD 1.741, exactly what US-CPI indexation would have given. If the currency instead falls 40% further (rate 694.06), the share is worth USD 1.244, 28.6% less. Lesson: local indexation protects a local-currency cost, not the dollar value.

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 5.1 Four questions before trusting an IRR** (`fw:irr-four-questions`), original. Purpose: decide in one minute whether an IRR can be used to rank or accept. Steps: (1) Do the cash flows change sign more than once? If yes, plot the NPV profile and use NPV. (2) Are the alternatives different in size? If yes, compute the incremental IRR or compare NPVs. (3) Are the alternatives different in timing? If yes, find the crossover rate and compare it with the discount rate. (4) Are large cash flows returned early to an investor who cannot reinvest at the IRR? If yes, report MIRR at a stated reinvestment rate. Demonstrated on Examples 5.9 to 5.12.
- **Framework 5.2 Indexation audit** (`fw:indexation-audit`), original. Purpose: test any indexed price in a contract or model. Steps: (1) Base value and base date. (2) Index name, publisher, region and series; fallback if it changes. (3) Indexed share versus fixed share, and whether that split matches the cost it is meant to track. (4) Lag between index month and application. (5) Reset frequency. (6) Currency of the index versus the currency of the cost and of payment; base exchange rate for any converted share. (7) Caps, floors, collars or fixed escalators. (8) Who bears the residual mismatch: write it down for inflation above and below the base case. Demonstrated on Hinkley Point C (CPI-indexed strike in 2012 prices, restated by the NAO at GBP 128.94/MWh in 2025 prices; fact sheet `hinkley-sizewell`, items 4 and 13; the writer prints amounts as "GBP", never "£") and on Ocean Wind 1 (fixed escalator, no capex or rate indexation).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 5.14)

Checklist:
- State every rate with its compounding basis, day count and whether it is real or nominal, pre- or post-tax.
- Confirm that cash flows and discount rate share currency, inflation basis and risk.
- Check the Excel NPV range excludes year 0.
- Plot the NPV profile whenever cash flows change sign more than once.
- Use XIRR when cash flows fall on real dates; state the annualization convention for periodic IRRs.
- Run the indexation audit (Framework 5.2) on every indexed revenue and cost line.

Red flags:
- An IRR quoted without its cash flow profile or date basis.
- A model that indexes revenue at CPI but holds opex flat (or the reverse) without a contractual reason.
- A real discount rate applied to nominal cash flows in a bid model (overstates value by double-digit percentages; Example 5.15).
- A local-currency indexed share described as "inflation-protected" in USD terms.
- A levelized tariff comparison that hides very different early-year prices.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Rule of 72 for doubling time: accurate within about 0.2 years between 4% and 12%; worse above 20%.
- Fisher approximation (real ≈ nominal − inflation): fine below about 5% inflation; use the exact form in high-inflation economies (Kessara's 2023 inflation of 34.8% is an example from the Case Bible).
- Payback under one third of contract tenor is a comfortable screen for contracted assets; meaningless for comparing projects of different lives.

Common mistakes:
- Mixing annual rates with semiannual periods; doubling a semiannual IRR and calling it annual without saying so.
- Treating a fixed escalator as inflation protection.
- Using the IRR guess argument to "find" the IRR you want when two exist.
- Discounting a growing perpetuity with growth close to the discount rate.

Questions experts ask:
- "Is that IRR on dates or on periods, and nominal or real?"
- "What happens to this price if inflation runs 2 points above the base case for five years?"
- "Which index, which month, which publisher, and what happens if it is rebased?"
- "If you remove the terminal value, what is left?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 5.15)

Situation (second person, deadline): You are the sponsor's financial adviser on a 20-year PPA for an illustrative 96.6 MW wind farm producing 262.4 GWh a year (P50). The offtaker's final offer, due back in 48 hours, gives two options: (A) USD 61.40/MWh in year 1 escalating at a fixed 2.0% a year; (B) USD 57.90/MWh in today's money, 100% indexed to US CPI. The sponsor's required real return is 5.5%. The debt will be fixed-rate in USD; most opex is indexed to CPI.

Computed figures (nominal discount rate = (1.055)(1 + inflation) − 1):
- Inflation 1.5%: PV of revenue A USD 197.13 million, B USD 181.56 million (A ahead by 15.57).
- Inflation 2.5%: A 180.92, B 181.56 (B ahead by 0.64).
- Inflation 4.0%: A 160.19, B 181.56 (B ahead by 21.37).
- Breakeven inflation 2.46%. Option A's year-20 price in real terms: USD 66.41/MWh at 1.5% inflation, 54.59 at 2.5%, 40.82 at 4.0%; Option B stays at USD 57.90/MWh real.

Reasoning outline: B's PV is the same at any inflation because it is fully real; A is a bet that inflation stays below about 2.5%. The sponsor's costs are mostly indexed, so A creates a real margin squeeze in high-inflation years; but fixed-rate nominal debt service is better covered by A's higher early nominal revenue (lenders may prefer A's early years and dislike B's lower year-1 cash). The expert answer: take B unless the debt can be sized and hedged on a nominal basis that B's lower early revenue cannot support, or unless the sponsor holds an explicit view on low inflation; a third way is to ask for a partial indexation (for example 60% CPI with a higher starting price) that matches the indexed share of costs. Present lender, sponsor and offtaker lenses.

What would change the answer: debt that is CPI-linked (favors B); opex fixed under long-term contracts (weakens B's advantage); a sponsor planning to sell in year 5 to a buyer who prices on real cash flows (favors B); an offtaker in a country whose currency is linked to USD inflation differently (Chapter 59).
