## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every later chapter prices something that happens in the future: a tariff paid for 25 years, a loan repaid over 15, a decommissioning bill due in 2046, an equity stake sold in 2026. This chapter gives the reader the arithmetic that turns future money into present decisions: compounding, discounting, annuities, net present value, internal rate of return and its traps, irregular dates, payback, and the difference between real and nominal money, ending with the mechanics of contract indexation that decide whether a 25-year price keeps its value. It is the reader's first full calculation chapter, so every formula appears with its Excel implementation and every concept with a worked number.

What you will be able to do (2 to 4 items for the chapter opener):

- Discount and compound any cash flow stream, including annuities, growing streams and irregular dates, and implement each calculation in Excel (Capabilities 4 and 12).
- Compute and interpret NPV, IRR, MIRR, XIRR and payback, and recognize the four situations in which an IRR misleads (Capabilities 12 and 15).
- Convert between real and nominal values and trace how full, partial, lagged and currency-converted indexation changes the value of a long-term contract price (Capabilities 3 and 4).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned (taught in full here)

1. Interest as the price of time; simple versus compound interest; compounding frequency; nominal annual rate versus effective annual rate; continuous compounding (one paragraph, for reading only); the rule of 72 and its limit.

3. Annuities (ordinary and due), growing annuities, perpetuities, growing perpetuities; annuity factor; Excel PV, FV, PMT, RATE, NPER.
4. Levelized value: converting an uneven cost or revenue stream into a level (or level-escalating) price per unit; levelized cost and levelized tariff (see change request CR-u02-1).

7. IRR traps: multiple IRRs and no IRR (non-conventional cash flows); the reinvestment assumption and MIRR; scale effects and incremental IRR; timing effects and the crossover rate.
8. XNPV and XIRR for irregular dates; Excel's Actual/365 basis; annualizing periodic IRRs (semiannual IRR times two versus compounded).
9. Payback period and discounted payback; their uses and limits.
10. Inflation; price indices; base dates and rebasing; real versus nominal values and discount rates; the Fisher relation; the consistency rule (nominal with nominal, real with real).
11. Indexation mechanics: full and partial indexation, fixed escalators, indexation lags, reset frequency, multi-index formulas, local-currency shares converted at a base exchange rate and reconverted, caps, floors and collars on indexation, and who bears the residual mismatch between the index and the actual cost.

## 6. Worked examples

All Illustrative unless stated. Numbers below were computed in Python; writers recompute.

- **Example 5.1 Growing a deposit under different compounding** (`ex:5.1`). USD 12.6 million placed for 5 years at 4.85% a year. Compute simple interest and compound values. Results: simple USD 15.66 million; annual compounding USD 15.97 million; semiannual USD 16.01 million; monthly USD 16.05 million; daily USD 16.06 million; continuous USD 16.06 million. Rule of 72: 14.8 years to double versus exact 14.6 years. Excel: `=FV(4.85%/2,10,0,-12.6)`.
- **Example 5.2 Effective and periodic rates** (`ex:5.2`). A loan quoted at 6.42% a year paid semiannually: EAR 6.52% (`=EFFECT(6.42%,2)`); monthly compounding would give 6.61%. A sponsor's 9.25% annual hurdle as a semiannual periodic rate: 4.5227% (not 4.625%); doubled, it quotes as 9.045% "semiannual basis". Lesson: always state the compounding basis of a quoted rate.
- **Example 5.3 Present value of a deferred payment** (`ex:5.3`). USD 18.6 million receivable in 4 years discounted at 9.25%: discount factor 0.70196, PV USD 13.06 million. Exhibit 5.1 lists DFs at 9.25%: 0.91533, 0.83783, 0.76689, 0.70196, 0.64253 (years 1 to 5).
- **Example 5.4 Valuing a land lease** (`ex:5.4`). USD 1.27 million a year for 20 years, paid at year end, discounted at 8.4%: annuity factor 9.53265, PV USD 12.11 million; paid in advance (annuity due) USD 13.12 million; as a perpetuity USD 15.12 million. Excel: `=PV(8.4%,20,-1.27)` and `=PV(8.4%,20,-1.27,0,1)`.
- **Example 5.5 An escalating O&M fee** (`ex:5.5`). USD 3.42 million in year 1 growing 2.5% a year for 18 years, discounted at 8.4%: PV USD 36.80 million (formula and year-by-year sum agree); as a growing perpetuity USD 57.97 million. Shows the closed form and the row-by-row spreadsheet check.
- **Example 5.6 Levelized cost of a solar plant** (`ex:5.6`). Capex USD 41.8 million at year 0; opex USD 0.73 million in year 1 growing 2.5%; output 104.2 GWh in year 1 degrading 0.45% a year; 25 years; nominal discount rate 7.6%. PV of costs USD 51.86 million (of which opex USD 10.06 million); PV of energy 1,109.1 GWh (discounted energy, explained as a device, not a physical quantity); flat levelized price USD 46.76/MWh. If the price instead escalates 2% a year, the year-1 levelized price is USD 39.41/MWh. Teaching point: two bids with the same levelized value can have very different early cash flows.
- **Example 5.7 NPV of a gas-engine life extension** (`ex:5.7`). Outlay USD 31.7 million at year 0; net cash inflows years 1 to 8: 5.84, 6.02, 6.19, 6.37, 6.55, 6.74, 6.93, 7.12, plus salvage of 2.40 in year 8 (USD m). NPV at 9.0%: USD 4.86 million. NPV profile (Exhibit 5.2): 0% 22.46; 5% 11.44; 9% 4.86; 12% 0.88; 15% (2.47). IRR 12.74%.
- **Example 5.8 The Excel NPV first-period trap** (`ex:5.8`). Applying `=NPV(9%,...)` to the whole row including year 0 returns USD 4.46 million, which is the true NPV divided by 1.09. Correct form `=B5+NPV(9%,C5:J5)`.
- **Example 5.9 A mine with a reclamation bill** (`ex:5.9`). Cash flows (USD m): year 0 (52.0); years 1 to 4 +38.0 each; year 5 (105.0) reclamation. Two IRRs: 4.42% and 36.39%; NPV is negative below 4.42% (year 0 to 5 sum is (5.0)), positive between, negative above (NPV at 10% is +3.26; at 40% is (1.25)). MIRR with 8% finance and reinvestment rates: 8.42%. Show the two-sign-change rule (Descartes) in one sentence.
- **Example 5.10 Reinvestment and MIRR** (`ex:5.10`). Cash flows (USD m): (42.5), 19.8, 17.6, 14.1, 9.3. IRR 18.41%. Inflows compounded to year 4 at 8% total USD 70.00 million; MIRR 13.29%. At the IRR as reinvestment rate the terminal value is USD 83.54 million and MIRR equals the IRR.
- **Example 5.11 Scale: the smaller project with the higher IRR** (`ex:5.11`). A: (10.0) then 3.2 a year for 5 years; B: (60.0) then 16.5 a year for 5 years; discount rate 8%. A: NPV 2.78, IRR 18.03%. B: NPV 5.88, IRR 11.65%. Incremental cash flow B minus A: NPV 3.10, IRR 10.33%, above 8%, so B is the better use of capital if the two are exclusive.
- **Example 5.12 Timing and the crossover rate** (`ex:5.12`). C: (50.0), 24.0, 19.0, 14.0, 8.0, 4.0. D: (50.0), 4.0, 9.0, 15.0, 24.0, 31.0. IRR C 16.11%, D 14.54%. NPV at 8%: C 8.23, D 12.07; at 15%: C 1.00, D (0.72). Crossover 12.49%. Exhibit 5.4 plots both profiles (0% to 15%).
- **Example 5.13 XIRR on an equity investor's dated cash flows** (`ex:5.13`). Contributions: 2024-03-15 (7.85); 2024-09-30 (12.40); 2025-06-18 (9.65). Distributions: 2026-06-30 2.10; 2026-12-31 3.35; 2027-06-30 3.48; 2027-12-31 3.61; 2028-12-31 7.42; 2029-12-31 7.70; 2030-12-31 7.96; 2031-12-31 8.25; 2032-12-31 8.51 plus sale proceeds 21.40 (USD m). - **Example 5.14 Payback and discounted payback** (`ex:5.14`). On Example 5.7: cumulative cash turns positive in year 6 (cumulative (0.73) at year 5, +6.01 at year 6), so payback is about 5.1 years by interpolation; discounted at 9% the cumulative turns positive in year 7 (+0.08), discounted payback about 7.0 years.
- **Example 5.15 Rebasing an index to a contract base date** (`ex:5.15`). Illustrative index values (not a real CPI series): 142.37 at the base month, 152.81 thirty months later and 180.49 at month 78; rebased to 100.0 at the base month: 107.33 and 126.78. Teach the base-month convention and the three-month lag; Excel `=C7/$C$5*100`. The writer labels the index values Illustrative and must not present them as a real CPI series.
- **Example 5.16 Real and nominal give the same answer if you are consistent** (`ex:5.16`). Nominal rate 9.4%, inflation 2.6%: exact real rate 6.628% (approximation 6.8%). A cash flow of USD 4.15 million a year in today's prices for 10 years: PV of real flows at the real rate USD 29.66 million equals PV of nominal flows (4.15 × 1.026^t) at 9.4%. Mismatch errors: real flows at the nominal rate USD 26.17 million (understates by 11.8%); nominal flows at the real rate USD 33.79 million (overstates by 13.9%).
- **Example 5.17 How partial indexation erodes a price** (`ex:5.17`). Capacity charge USD 12.84/kW-month, 25% indexed to CPI, inflation 3.0% a year. Year 10: nominal 13.94, real 10.38 (real loss 19.2%); year 20: nominal 15.43, real 8.54 (real loss 33.5%). Fully indexed: nominal 17.26 (year 10) and 23.19 (year 20), real constant at 12.84. Exhibit 5.5: nominal and real paths for 0%, 25% and 100% indexation at years 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 (writer computes the intermediate points).
- **Example 5.18 A local-currency share under parity and under overshoot** (`ex:5.18`). Fixed charge USD 4.62/kW-month, of which 35% (USD 1.617) is a local share converted at a base rate of 380.0 local units per USD, indexed to local CPI at 12% a year, reconverted after 3 years. Under purchasing-power parity (US inflation 2.5%), the rate moves to 495.75 and the local share is worth USD 1.741, exactly what US-CPI indexation would have given. If the currency instead falls 40% further (rate 694.06), the share is worth USD 1.244, 28.6% less. Lesson: local indexation protects a local-currency cost, not the dollar value.

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 5.1 Four questions before trusting an IRR** (`fw:irr-four-questions`), original. Purpose: decide in one minute whether an IRR can be used to rank or accept. Steps: (1) Do the cash flows change sign more than once? If yes, plot the NPV profile and use NPV. (2) Are the alternatives different in size? If yes, compute the incremental IRR or compare NPVs. (3) Are the alternatives different in timing? If yes, find the crossover rate and compare it with the discount rate. (4) Are large cash flows returned early to an investor who cannot reinvest at the IRR? If yes, report MIRR at a stated reinvestment rate. Demonstrated on Examples 5.9 to 5.12.
- **Framework 5.2 Indexation audit** (`fw:indexation-audit`), original. Purpose: test any indexed price in a contract or model. Steps: (1) Base value and base date. (2) Index name, publisher, region and series; fallback if it changes. (3) Indexed share versus fixed share, and whether that split matches the cost it is meant to track. (4) Lag between index month and application. (5) Reset frequency. (6) Currency of the index versus the currency of the cost and of payment; base exchange rate for any converted share. (7) Caps, floors, collars or fixed escalators. (8) Who bears the residual mismatch: write it down for inflation above and below the base case. Demonstrated on Hinkley Point C (CPI-indexed strike in 2012 prices, restated by the NAO at GBP 128.94/MWh in 2025 prices; fact sheet `hinkley-sizewell`, items 4 and 13; the writer prints amounts as "GBP", never "£") and on Ocean Wind 1 (fixed escalator, no capex or rate indexation).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 5.14)

Checklist:
- State every rate with its compounding basis, day count and whether it is real or nominal, pre- or post-tax.
- Confirm that cash flows and discount rate share currency, inflation basis and risk.
- Check the Excel NPV range excludes year 0.
- Plot the NPV profile whenever cash flows change sign more than once.
- Use XIRR when cash flows fall on real dates; state the annualization convention for periodic IRRs.
- Run the indexation audit (Framework 5.2) on every indexed revenue and cost line.

Red flags:
- An IRR quoted without its cash flow profile or date basis.
- A model that indexes revenue at CPI but holds opex flat (or the reverse) without a contractual reason.
- A real discount rate applied to nominal cash flows in a bid model (overstates value by double-digit percentages; Example 5.16).
- A local-currency indexed share described as "inflation-protected" in USD terms.
- A levelized tariff comparison that hides very different early-year prices.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Rule of 72 for doubling time: within about 0.35 years of the exact answer between 4% and 12% (at 8% it gives 9.0 years against 9.01); at 20% it gives 3.6 years against 3.8.
- Fisher approximation (real ≈ nominal − inflation): fine below about 5% inflation; use the exact form in high-inflation economies (Kessara's 2023 inflation of 34.8% is an example from the Case Bible).
- Payback under one third of contract tenor is a comfortable screen for contracted assets; meaningless for comparing projects of different lives.

Common mistakes:
- Mixing annual rates with semiannual periods; doubling a semiannual IRR and calling it annual without saying so.
- Treating a fixed escalator as inflation protection.
- Using the IRR guess argument to "find" the IRR you want when two exist.
- Discounting a growing perpetuity with growth close to the discount rate.

Questions experts ask:
- "Is that IRR on dates or on periods, and nominal or real?"
- "What happens to this price if inflation runs 2 points above the base case for five years?"
- "Which index, which month, which publisher, and what happens if it is rebased?"
- "If you remove the terminal value, what is left?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 5.15)

Situation (second person, deadline): You are the sponsor's financial adviser on a 20-year PPA for an illustrative 96.6 MW wind farm producing 262.4 GWh a year (P50). The offtaker's final offer, due back in 48 hours, gives two options: (A) USD 61.40/MWh in year 1 escalating at a fixed 2.0% a year; (B) USD 57.90/MWh in today's money, 100% indexed to US CPI. The sponsor's required real return is 5.5%. The debt will be fixed-rate in USD; most opex is indexed to CPI.

Computed figures (nominal discount rate = (1.055)(1 + inflation) − 1):
- Inflation 1.5%: PV of revenue A USD 197.13 million, B USD 181.56 million (A ahead by 15.57).
- Inflation 2.5%: A 180.92, B 181.56 (B ahead by 0.64).
- Inflation 4.0%: A 160.19, B 181.56 (B ahead by 21.37).
- Breakeven inflation 2.46%. Option A's year-20 price in real terms: USD 66.41/MWh at 1.5% inflation, 54.59 at 2.5%, 40.82 at 4.0%; Option B stays at USD 57.90/MWh real.

Reasoning outline: B's PV is the same at any inflation because it is fully real; A is a bet that inflation stays below about 2.5%. The sponsor's costs are mostly indexed, so A creates a real margin squeeze in high-inflation years; but fixed-rate nominal debt service is better covered by A's higher early nominal revenue (lenders may prefer A's early years and dislike B's lower year-1 cash). The expert answer: take B unless the debt can be sized and hedged on a nominal basis that B's lower early revenue cannot support, or unless the sponsor holds an explicit view on low inflation; a third way is to ask for a partial indexation (for example 60% CPI with a higher starting price) that matches the indexed share of costs. Present lender, sponsor and offtaker lenses.

What would change the answer: debt that is CPI-linked (favors B); opex fixed under long-term contracts (weakens B's advantage); a sponsor planning to sell in year 5 to a buyer who prices on real cash flows (favors B); an offtaker in a country whose currency is linked to USD inflation differently (Chapter 59).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A project financing is mostly debt, and debt is a contract about principal, interest, fees and dates. This chapter teaches the reader how a loan works from the lender's ledger outward: what is borrowed and when, how interest accrues under each day-count convention, how principal comes back (bullet, annuity, straight-line, and in outline sculpted), the difference between fixed and floating rates, what a reference rate is and why LIBOR died, how SOFR, SONIA, €STR and EURIBOR are set and applied (term rates in advance, overnight rates compounded in arrears), what a margin in basis points means, how a bond is priced, and how an interest rate swap converts floating to fixed, what it is worth after rates move, and how it can turn from protection into a liability. The reader leaves able to read the interest and hedging lines of a term sheet and to compute every cash flow they imply.

What you will be able to do:

## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 6.12)

Checklist: base rate, setting method and lookback; credit adjustment spread for legacy tranches; floor; day count; interest period and payment-date alignment with the model; commitment fee base; hedge notional versus projected balance; swap index versus loan index; breakage provisions.

Red flags: a hedge whose notional exceeds the loan balance at any date (over-hedging becomes speculation and a lender-ranked claim); a floored loan hedged with an unfloored swap; an accreting swap against a short bullet loan; a model that applies an annual rate to semiannual periods without a day count; a term sheet quoting "SOFR" without saying which one.

Rules of thumb and their limits: 1 bp a year on USD 100 million is USD 10,000 a year (Actual/360 adds 1.4%); the annuity profile's WAL is a little above half the tenor at mid-single-digit rates (6.0 years on a 10-year loan at 6.35%), and rises with the rate; a swap's MTM is roughly (change in par rate) × remaining annuity × notional, valid for parallel moves only.

Common mistakes: treating a forward curve as a forecast; forgetting the credit and execution charge when comparing swap quotes; quoting all-in cost without fees; mixing Actual/365 and Actual/360 rates in one comparison.

Questions experts ask: "What is the hedge-to-loan basis, and who pays for it?"; "What is the breakage on a prepayment in year 5 if rates fall 150 bps?"; "Is the hedge counterparty pari passu, and does it vote?"; "What happens to this clause if Term SOFR is not published for a week?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 6.13)

Situation: You are CFO of the sponsor of an illustrative 214 MW solar plant in Spain with a EUR 186.4 million, 17-year straight-line amortizing loan at 6M EURIBOR + 1.65%. The term sheet requires at least 75% of the balance hedged. The hedge bank quotes a 17-year amortizing payer swap at a mid rate of 2.71% plus 12 bps (all-in 2.83%). Your board meets Thursday and asks whether to hedge 75%, 90% or 100%. Average outstanding balance over the life: EUR 98.68 million.

Computed figures (average annual base-rate cost on the average balance, EUR million, assuming floating EURIBOR averages the swap mid plus a parallel move): hedge 75%: 2.516 if rates average 1 point lower, 2.763 flat, 3.257 if 2 points higher; hedge 90%: 2.682, 2.781, 2.978; hedge 100%: 2.793 in every case. The extra 15 points of hedging cost about EUR 0.018 million a year in execution charge when rates are flat.

Reasoning: the cost of more hedging is small (the charge), the benefit is protection against a scenario that would also hit inflation-indexed opex; revenue is partly merchant or partly CfD-indexed (state the assumption), so the correlation of revenue with rates decides. Expert answer: hedge 90% for the contracted period and leave the unhedged 10% as headroom for prepayments and sweeps that would otherwise over-hedge; consider a cap on the tail if the loan may be refinanced (breakage risk). Present the lender's view (minimum 75%, prefers higher), the hedge bank's view (credit line usage, ranking), and the sponsor's view (breakage at refinancing).

What would change the answer: a likely refinancing within five years (hedge shorter or less, because breakage could be large); revenue indexed to inflation with rates correlated to inflation (supports less hedging); a cash sweep that accelerates repayment (lower hedge ratio to avoid over-hedging); a negative-rate environment with a loan floor (requires a floored swap).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Lenders lend against cash, but they receive audited accounts, test covenants that reference them, pay tax computed from them, and see distributions blocked by them. The reader must therefore read a project company's financial statements fluently and know exactly where accounting profit and cash part company. This chapter builds the three statements from the accounting equation, links them, and then teaches the items that dominate a project company's accounts: depreciation of a single large asset, interest capitalized during construction, deferred tax (including tax holidays), working capital and receivables from a weak offtaker, provisions such as decommissioning, and the presentation of shareholder loans and restricted cash. It ends with a disciplined method for reading a set of project accounts and with Bélanou Power's first full-year accounts.

What you will be able to do:

- Build and link an income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement for a project company, and reconcile profit to cash (Capabilities 4 and 10).
- Explain and compute depreciation, capitalized interest, deferred tax, working capital and provisions, and say how each affects cash, tax and distributions (Capabilities 4 and 12).
- Read a project company's audited accounts and identify the signals a lender or buyer should act on (Capabilities 10, 11 and 14).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 7.14)

Checklist: Framework 7.1 steps as a checklist; reconcile EBITDA to operating cash flow; reconcile revenue to contract terms; tie debt balances to the facility agent's statements; tie restricted cash to the accounts agreement; confirm the tax holiday dates and deferred deduction balance.

Red flags: receivables days rising faster than revenue; emphasis of matter on going concern or a dispute; capitalized costs continuing after COD; derivative liabilities large relative to equity; related-party fees rising without contractual basis; a deferred tax asset recognized on losses that the tax code lets expire (Case P: five years); auditor change late in a year.

Rules of thumb and their limits: in a stable contracted project, operating cash flow before working capital is close to EBITDA minus cash tax (breaks when receivables move); depreciation of a single-asset company is close to cost over contract life (breaks with component accounting and impairment); EBITDA multiples from corporate deals mislead for finite-life assets.

Common mistakes: reading net income as cash available to shareholders; double-counting IDC as both capitalized and expensed in a model; forgetting that shareholder loan interest is an expense (and may be non-deductible beyond thin-cap limits, Chapter 67); treating restricted cash as available.

Questions experts ask: "Show me receivables by age and by counterparty"; "What is capitalized in PP&E, line by line?"; "When does the tax holiday end and what deferred deductions remain?"; "Where are the swaps on the balance sheet and what did they cost this year?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 7.15)

Situation: You are the credit analyst at the facility agent bank of an illustrative operating gas plant with PP&E of USD 412.6 million depreciated straight-line over 20 years (USD 20.63 million a year). Retained earnings are negative because of early losses, so local company law blocks dividends even though cash is building in the distribution account. The sponsor proposes to extend the depreciation life to 30 years (USD 13.75 million a year, raising book profit by USD 6.88 million a year) and asks the lenders to "note" the change before the board meeting on Friday.

Reasoning outline: the change alters no cash flow and no lender ratio built on cash; it may be legitimate if the useful life really is 30 years (the plant's technical life, contract life and handback terms decide); it may be cosmetic if the PPA ends earlier than 30 years and the asset transfers for USD 1 (a BOOT plant cannot have a book life longer than its use to the owner, unless a residual value is justified). Tax depreciation is separate and unaffected unless local law ties tax to book. The lenders' interest: dividends paid from accounting profit that the plant has not earned in cash terms could breach distribution conditions in spirit; but the lenders' protection is the distribution test (Chapter 37), not depreciation policy. Expert answer: do not object to an auditor-approved change consistent with technical life and contract life; do insist that distributions remain subject to the cash-based tests and that the change is disclosed; if the PPA term is shorter than 30 years, object and propose the shareholder-loan route (forward reference to Chapter 42).

What would change the answer: a PPA term shorter than the new life (object); an IE report supporting a 30-year life and an extension option (accept); local law allowing capital reduction to clear the deficit (prefer that route); a covenant that references accounting net worth (the change then affects compliance, so lenders must consent).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Project finance exists largely because debt is cheaper than equity and a ring-fenced, contracted project can carry far more of it than a company could. This chapter explains why: how debt and equity divide a project's outcomes, how leverage lifts the expected equity return and widens its spread, why in a frictionless world that trade would be a wash (Modigliani and Miller), what breaks the wash (tax, distress costs, agency and information costs), and how investors price risk through the capital asset pricing model, the cost of debt, the weighted average cost of capital and adjusted present value. It ends with why project finance sustains gearing of 70% to 90% and what that leverage costs the sponsor in downside exposure and control. The chapter supplies the discount rates that Chapter 5 took as given.

What you will be able to do:

- Show with numbers how gearing changes the level and the spread of equity returns, and explain the Modigliani-Miller logic and its exceptions (Capabilities 1 and 12).
- Estimate a cost of equity with CAPM (including unlevering and relevering betas and a country risk premium), a cost of debt, and a WACC, and choose between WACC and APV for a project with amortizing debt (Capabilities 12 and 15).
- Explain to a board why project finance achieves high leverage and what the sponsor gives up for it (Capabilities 1 and 5).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned

1. Capital structure: debt and equity as claims with different priority and payoff shapes; limited liability; equity as the residual claim.
2. Leverage (debt's effect on returns; style sheet 8.2 permits "leverage" in this sense in this chapter): the leverage equation for return on equity; unlevered (asset) return versus levered (equity) return as concepts; leverage widening the distribution of equity outcomes (see CR-u02-5 on the boundary with Chapter 46's equity IRR and project IRR).
3. The Modigliani-Miller propositions (I and II) without taxes, as intuition; the interest tax shield; financial distress costs; agency and information costs (cross-reference Chapter 2 for the agency-cost rationale of project finance); the trade-off view of optimal capital structure.
4. Why project finance achieves high leverage: low cash-flow volatility from contracts, ring-fencing, reduced distress costs (going-concern enforcement), monitoring by lenders, matching amortization to asset life, and the debt-capacity constraint set by lenders rather than by the sponsor's choice (forward reference to Chapter 36 for sizing).
5. Risk and return: required return as risk-free rate plus premium; systematic versus diversifiable risk (two-state intuition; formal variance and correlation in Chapter 9).
6. CAPM: beta, market risk premium, risk-free rate (finance sense, distinct from the reference-rate RFR of Chapter 6); asset beta, equity beta, debt beta; unlevering and relevering; country risk premium (the debate, and the book's position).
7. Cost of debt: promised versus expected return, pre-tax and after-tax cost.
8. Weighted average cost of capital (WACC): market-value weights, after-tax debt cost, the constant-gearing assumption and why it fails for amortizing project debt.
9. Adjusted present value (APV): base-case NPV at the unlevered cost of capital plus the PV of financing side effects (tax shields, subsidized debt, issue costs).
10. The positions practitioners take: equity cash flows at a cost of equity for sponsor decisions, WACC for screening and bid comparison, APV for analyzing financing effects; this book's recommendation and why.

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 8.1 Leverage ledger** (`fw:leverage-ledger`), original. Purpose: decide whether more debt is worth it to the sponsor. Steps: (1) equity IRR gained in the base case; (2) equity IRR lost in a defined downside, and the cash shortfall at which equity receives nothing in a year; (3) change in the required return on equity (Proposition II logic), so that the higher IRR is compared with a higher hurdle; (4) tax shield actually usable (holidays, interest limitation); (5) control and flexibility given up (tighter covenants, lock-ups, consent rights, forward reference to Chapters 37 and 51); (6) refinancing and hedge-breakage exposure added; (7) effect on the tariff or bid price. Demonstrated in the judgment drill and in the Case P section.
- **Established tools credited in prose**: Modigliani and Miller's propositions (1958, 1963); the capital asset pricing model (Sharpe, Lintner, Mossin, 1960s); adjusted present value (Myers, 1974). ## 11. Practitioner's notebook outline (Section 8.11)

Checklist: state whether a return is project or equity, pre- or post-tax, nominal or real, and in which currency; check the hurdle rises with gearing; check the tax shield is usable in each year; check the cost-of-equity inputs (risk-free rate tenor and currency, beta source and gearing, market risk premium source and date, country risk premium source); run the base and a downside at each gearing.

Red flags: an equity IRR rising with gearing compared with an unchanged hurdle; a WACC at initial gearing applied to a project whose debt amortizes; a country risk premium added on top of insured political risk; betas taken from diversified utilities and relevered to 80% without a debt beta; a bid that only clears the hurdle at maximum gearing.

Rules of thumb and their limits: each 5 points of gearing added near 75% raises base equity IRR by roughly 1.5 to 2 points in a long contracted asset (Example 8.3: 70% to 75% adds 1.5 points, 75% to 80% adds 2.1) and the effect grows with gearing; the downside IRR rises much less (0.9 and 1.2 points); the rule fails when debt cost rises with gearing.

Common mistakes: discounting equity cash flows at a WACC; using the risk-free rate of one currency with cash flows in another; treating a lower WACC from more debt as free value; forgetting that shareholder loans are equity for return purposes but debt for tax (Chapters 32 and 67).

Questions experts ask: "What gearing does the bid need to win, and what happens to the equity at the downside?"; "Whose risk premium is this: ours, the market's, or the offtaker's?"; "If rates rise 150 bps before close, what gearing do we lose and what return do we keep?"

## 12. Judgment drill (Section 8.12)

Situation: You lead the sponsor team bidding an illustrative 132 MW contracted wind farm costing USD 163.9 million, with pre-financing cash flow of USD 17.42 million in year 1 growing 1.2% a year for 25 years; debt at 6.40% fixed over 17 years (annuity). Your CFO wants 80% gearing to lift the equity IRR above the 16.0% hurdle the investment committee meets on Monday; lenders have offered up to 80%.

Computed figures: project IRR 10.70%. At 70% gearing: debt service USD 11.27 million; equity IRR 15.97% base, 12.88% with cash flows 10% lower, 11.36% with 15% lower; year-1 cash after debt service 6.15 (base), cover of debt service by cash 1.55x. At 80% gearing: debt service USD 12.88 million; equity IRR 18.61%, 14.43%, 12.46%; year-1 cash after debt service 4.54; cover 1.35x (base), 1.22x (10% lower), 1.15x (15% lower). Cash flow can fall 35.3% in year 1 before it fails to cover debt service at 70% gearing, but only 26.1% at 80%.

Reasoning outline (Framework 8.1): the 2.6-point base uplift is real but Proposition II says the equity is riskier, so the hurdle should rise too; at 80% the downside cover approaches levels that lenders will use for lock-ups (forward reference to Chapter 37); the bid needs the lower tariff that higher gearing allows only if competitors also gear at 80%. Expert answer: bid at the gearing the lenders will hold through due diligence on the downside case (often below the offered maximum), set the hurdle explicitly for that gearing, and do not use gearing to close a gap between the bid and the hurdle; if the bid fails at 75%, the problem is the bid price or the cost base. Show the lender's view (80% only with tight lock-ups and sweeps), the offtaker's (lower tariff), and the host government's (a thinly capitalized sponsor is a renegotiation risk).

What would change the answer: a fixed-price, long-dated debt market that will hold 80% to maturity with no refinancing (favors 80%); merchant exposure in the tail (favors less); a tax holiday that removes the tax shield (reduces the gain from debt); a competitor field known to bid with 85% gearing (forces a choice between walking away and accepting a lower return).
