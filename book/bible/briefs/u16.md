## 77.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Industrial plants turn a bought input into a sold output, and they earn the spread between the two. That makes them the hardest assets in the book to fit into project finance, whose tools were built for contracted, availability-style revenue. This chapter teaches the reader to see a refinery, a petrochemical complex and a battery-cell gigafactory as spread or margin businesses; to identify who carries margin risk, ramp-up risk and supply-chain risk in each; to recognize the structures that make such plants financeable (integrated sponsors, feedstock and offtake arrangements, processing fees, sponsor completion guarantees released only on demanding tests, ECA anchoring); and to explain why Sadara and Northvolt Ett, both financed by sophisticated lender groups, ended up where they did.

What you will be able to do:
- Compute a refining crack spread, a cracker cash margin and a factory's breakeven yield, and say which number a lender should size against (Capabilities 4, 13).
- Rank the ways an industrial sponsor can protect a margin, from merchant sale to processing fee, and show how each moves debt capacity (Capabilities 2, 5).
- Read and test a multi-unit completion test and the sponsor guarantee it releases (Capabilities 3, 10).
- Explain, from the Sadara, Duqm, Northvolt and US DOE battery-plant records, why sponsor-supported industrial debt still carries market and ramp-up risk (Capabilities 13, 15).

## 77.3 Concepts owned and assumed

Owned (taught in full here):
1. Spread (margin) business as a revenue type: revenue equals product value less feedstock cost; why lenders treat it as the riskiest revenue class short of pure merchant power.

5. Industrial customer contracts: supply agreements with volume commitments, capacity reservation fees, customer prepayments; why order books are not take-or-pay.
6. Processing agreements (industrial tolling) in industrial plants: the processor earns a fee per tonne and the toller owns input and output (R-054; power tolling is owned by sec:18.7, liquefaction tolling by ssec:21.2.3; this is the industrial form and the glossary home of "processing agreement", ssec:77.5.2).

8. Technology licenses and process performance guarantees from licensors; the gap between a licensor's guarantee and the lenders' loss.
9. 10. Feedstock supply and product marketing agreements with integrated sponsors (sponsor marketing, lifting, marketing fees).
11. Industrial supply-chain and geopolitical risk as a credit risk (single-route feedstock and product logistics; dependence on one country's equipment and materials).
12. Industrial policy finance in manufacturing: public lenders and guarantors (EIB, ECAs, national guarantee offices) and conditional commitments drawn on milestones; the difference between commitments and funding.

Example 77.1. Crack spread and refinery EBITDA (Illustrative). Setting: Sabine Bend Refining LLC (fictional), a 150,000 bbl/d coastal cracking refinery near Port Arthur, Texas, on the US Gulf Coast; its lenders (a fictional club led by Gulfmark Bank) review the 2025 budget in January 2025. Crude USD 76.80/bbl; gasoline USD 2.24/gal; diesel USD 2.52/gal; 42 gal/bbl. Compute: gasoline USD 94.08/bbl, diesel USD 105.84/bbl; 3-2-1 crack = (2 x 94.08 + 105.84 - 3 x 76.80)/3 = USD 21.20/bbl. Utilization 91.5% gives 50,096,250 bbl a year. Capture rate 83% gives a realized margin of USD 17.60/bbl. Cash opex USD 5.20/bbl. EBITDA = 50.10 million bbl x (17.60 - 5.20) = USD 621.0 million. Re-run at a trough crack of USD 12.85/bbl (realized 10.67; EBITDA USD 273.8 million) and USD 8.10/bbl (realized 6.72; EBITDA USD 76.3 million). Breakeven crack = 5.20/0.83 = USD 6.27/bbl. Teaching point: a 62% fall in the benchmark spread (21.20 to 8.10) cuts EBITDA by 88% (621.0 to 76.3); the lender sizes on the trough, not the average. All numbers verified in Python (revision round 1).

Example 77.2. Ethane against naphtha cracker margins (Illustrative). Setting: two 2024 cracker budgets compared by an ECA underwriter: Calcasieu Olefins LLC (fictional), an ethane cracker in Louisiana, and Straits Olefins Pte Ltd (fictional), a naphtha cracker on Jurong Island, Singapore. Per tonne of ethylene, ethylene price USD 1,040/t. Ethane cracker: 1.24 t ethane per t ethylene at USD 245/t (feed cost USD 303.80), co-product credit USD 62, cash conversion cost USD 168: cash margin USD 630.20/t. Naphtha cracker: 3.20 t naphtha per t ethylene at USD 640/t (feed cost USD 2,048.00), co-product credits (propylene, butadiene, aromatics) USD 1,512, conversion cost USD 214: cash margin USD 290.00/t. At 1.5 million t a year of ethylene each (nameplate): USD 945.3 million against USD 435.0 million. Sensitivities: naphtha up 15% turns the naphtha margin to minus USD 17.20/t; ethane doubling to USD 490/t leaves the ethane margin at USD 326.40/t. Teaching point: feedstock advantage, and why a naphtha cracker's credit depends on co-product markets as much as on ethylene.

Example 77.3. Processing fee against merchant sale for a urea plant (Illustrative). Setting: Caroni Nitrogen Company Ltd (fictional), a gas-based urea plant at Point Lisas, Trinidad and Tobago, seeking debt in March 2024; Atlantic Nitrogen Trading SA (fictional, investment grade) offers a processing agreement (industrial tolling) as the alternative to merchant sales. A 1.21 million t a year plant, project cost USD 1,386.4 million, fixed cash costs USD 41.7 million a year borne by the project company in both cases (gas is supplied and paid for by the toller under the processing agreement; under merchant sale, gas cost is inside the margin). Case A, processing fee USD 162.40/t, 12-year amortizing debt at 7.25%, sized at 1.35x: revenue USD 196.5 million; CFADS USD 154.8 million; debt service USD 114.7 million; annuity factor 7.8379; debt USD 898.8 million (64.8% of cost). Case B, merchant: P50 margin USD 214/t and P90 margin USD 131/t over gas cost; lenders size at the lesser of 1.80x on P50 and 1.25x on P90, 10 years at 7.75%: CFADS USD 217.2 million (P50) and USD 116.8 million (P90); debt service USD 120.7 million and USD 93.4 million, so P90 binds at USD 93.4 million; annuity factor 6.7864; debt USD 634.2 million (45.7%). Difference USD 264.6 million of debt. Writer states the DSCR targets as illustrative and does not present them as market norms.

Example 77.4. Gigafactory ramp, yield, and breakeven (Illustrative). Setting: Elbe Cell GmbH (fictional), a lithium-ion cell plant in Saxony-Anhalt, Germany, financed in 2024 by a fictional lender club with an ECA-covered tranche; its lenders test the ramp plan in January 2025. Nameplate 17.6 GWh a year of cells started (deliberately not Northvolt's 16 GWh); good-cell price USD 98.0/kWh; materials USD 58.0 and variable conversion USD 6.5 per kWh started (scrap consumes materials); fixed cash costs USD 342.7 million a year. Years 1 to 4: throughput 25%, 55%, 80%, 92% of nameplate; yield 72%, 84%, 91%, 94%. Results (recomputed in revision round 1): good output 3.168, 8.131, 12.813, 15.220 GWh; revenue USD 310.5, 796.9, 1,255.7, 1,491.6 million; variable cost USD 283.8, 624.4, 908.2, 1,044.4 million; EBITDA (316.0), (170.2), 4.8, 104.5 USD million; cash cost per good kWh USD 197.76, 118.93, 97.63, 91.13. Breakeven yield: at 55% throughput 101.9% (impossible), at 70% throughput 94.2%, at 80% throughput 90.7%, at 85% throughput 89.2%, at 92% throughput 87.4%. Equations: unit cost = (started x (materials + variable conversion) + fixed)/(started x yield); breakeven yield = (started x (m + v) + F)/(started x price). Teaching point: below a throughput threshold no yield saves the plant; the lender's completion test must test throughput and yield together.

Example 77.5. Reading a reliability-run result against a three-envelope completion test (Illustrative). Setting: Rayong Integrated Petrochemical Company Ltd (fictional), a cracker, derivatives and aromatics complex at Map Ta Phut, Thailand; the independent engineer reports on the reliability run in October 2023 to a lender group led by a fictional Japanese bank with an ECA-covered tranche. Test (Clause 77.2): each envelope must average at least 95.0% of nameplate output over 60 consecutive days within a 90-day window, with off-specification product at most 2.0%; financial completion requires historical DSCR of at least 1.25x for two consecutive quarters. Results: envelope A (cracker) 96.8%, off-spec 1.1%; envelope B (derivatives) 93.9%, off-spec 1.6%; envelope C (aromatics and utilities) 97.4%, off-spec 0.7%; quarter 1 CFADS USD 61.4 million against debt service USD 46.2 million (1.329x, prints as 1.33x); quarter 2 USD 58.9 million against USD 47.0 million (1.2532x, passes at 1.25x whether computed to two or three decimals). Outcome: technical completion fails on envelope B; the financial test passes; the guarantee stays in place; the writer shows the retest options (retest window, partial release by envelope if the clause allows it, and why lenders resist partial release when envelopes depend on each other). Teaching point: a ratio this close to a threshold makes the computation and rounding rule decisive; the clause must say how the ratio is computed and whether it is rounded before comparison (a quarter at 1.2496x would fail if truncated and pass if rounded to two decimals).

## 77.8 Frameworks

Framework 77.1, the margin protection ladder (original; label fw:margin-ladder). Purpose: rank how firmly an industrial plant's margin is protected and predict the debt it supports. Rungs, from weakest to strongest:
1. Merchant sale of products and spot purchase of feedstock.
2. Sponsor marketing or lifting agreement without price support (the marketer sells; the project company keeps the price risk).
3. Formula-linked feedstock supply, where feedstock price moves with product price (a partial natural hedge) or a sponsor feedstock agreement with volume commitment.
4. Margin floor or offtake with floor price from a creditworthy party (sponsor or customer), or a customer take-or-pay with capacity reservation fee.
5. Processing fee under a processing agreement (industrial tolling) from a creditworthy toller who owns inputs and outputs.
Steps in use: place each revenue stream on a rung; weight by share of revenue; test the credit of the party on each rung; size debt rung by rung (cross-reference the bucket sizing of Chapter 36). Demonstrated on Example 77.3 and in the Case P installment. When to use: any plant whose revenue is a spread.

## 77.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: identify the rung of every revenue stream on the margin protection ladder; obtain a joint price deck for inputs and outputs through a full cycle; obtain licensor guarantees and compare caps with lenders' exposure; map unit interdependencies before agreeing envelopes; confirm sponsor guarantee scope (debt-wide or cost-overrun only) and release tests; check single-route logistics for feedstock and products; for factories, obtain customer qualification status and contractual remedies for late or off-spec delivery; separate funded from committed capital.

Red flags: debt sized on an average spread; completion tied to mechanical completion only; release of guarantees on a short reliability run; expansion financing raised before phase one stabilizes; "orders" presented as contracted revenue; a marketer that is also a shareholder with discretion over pricing; DSCR thresholds that pass only by rounding.

Rules of thumb with limits: size spread businesses on a trough-of-cycle margin (limit: a long-term processing fee replaces the cycle); two ramp curves multiply (limit: independent if yield and throughput are driven by different constraints); feedstock linked to product price halves margin volatility only if the link is in the formula, not in a renegotiation clause.

Common mistakes: independent sensitivities on correlated prices; treating licensor LDs as a performance backstop; ignoring working capital build during ramp-up; modeling a factory's price flat in real terms when product prices deflate.

Questions experts ask: Which rung is each dollar of revenue on? Who will fund the plant if margins stay at trough for three years after guarantee release? What is the plant's position on its industry cost curve? Which units, if they fail, stop the others? How much of the funding is committed but conditional?

## 77.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are the lead arranger for Wabash Cell Manufacturing LLC (fictional), a 30 GWh lithium-ion cell plant near Kokomo, Indiana (Illustrative), April 2026; senior debt requested USD 2,486.3 million; an automaker customer with a 7-year supply agreement for 70% of nameplate; the sponsor asks that its completion guarantee fall away at mechanical completion plus 90 days at 70% of nameplate throughput and 85% yield, arguing the supply agreement makes the plant "contracted"; credit committee in nine days.

Reasoning outline: the supply agreement is not take-or-pay unless volumes, prices and remedies for the customer's non-purchase are fixed and the customer's right to reject is limited to defined specification failures; Example 77.4 shows breakeven yield at 70% throughput of 94.2% in a similar cost structure (and still 89.2% at 85% throughput), so the proposed test can pass while the plant loses cash; the expert asks for a combined throughput-yield-cash test (for example two consecutive quarters at 85% throughput and 92% yield plus a historical DSCR test), a capacity reservation fee or prepayment from the customer, and a sweep until stabilization; trade-off offered: a stepped release of the guarantee in two tranches.

What would change the answer: an investment-grade customer accepting a take-or-pay with capacity fees covering debt service (moves toward release at mechanical completion plus a short run); a proven repeat design by the same sponsor at another site (shortens the test); public lender cover of the ramp-up risk (changes who bears the risk, not whether it exists); a falling cell price curve faster than the cost curve (tightens the test and lowers debt).

## 78.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A mine is a price taker sitting on a depleting asset in a country that can change the rules after the money is spent. This chapter teaches the reader the economics of mining (cost curves, grade, recovery, by-products, cycles), the special features of critical minerals (thin markets, processing bottlenecks, state-directed finance), and the lender's toolkit that answers them: a bank price deck, a reserve tail, a multi-part completion test, sponsor completion support, multi-source structures with DFI and ECA cover, and streams as construction capital. Oyu Tolgoi and Cobre Panamá show what happens when geology and the host government move after financial close.

What you will be able to do:

- Size mining debt on a bank price deck, test it on a downside deck, and set the maximum tenor from a reserve tail (Capability 5).
- Draft and test a four-part mining completion test and explain what each part protects (Capabilities 3, 10).
- Judge the true cost and risk transfer of a stream against senior debt, and the protection a host agreement gives (Capabilities 8, 15).

## 78.8 Frameworks

Framework 78.1, the four-part completion test (label fw:four-part-completion). Established practice in mining finance, organized here as an original checklist. Parts: (1) mechanical completion: built to specification, certified; (2) physical completion: sustained throughput at a stated share of nameplate over a stated period; (3) metallurgical and operating completion: recovery, product quality and unit cost at or better than the banking case; (4) financial completion: projected cover ratios on the current bank deck and reserve tail still met, reserves funded, no default. Use: drafting and testing the release of sponsor completion support; each part is matched to the risk it retires (construction, ramp-up, process, economics). Demonstrated in Example 78.5 and the walkthrough.

Framework 78.2, the deck-tail-test triangle (original; label fw:deck-tail-test). Purpose: check that the three main mining debt parameters are consistent. Steps: (1) size on the bank deck; (2) set tenor inside the reserve tail; (3) make the financial completion test use the same deck and tail; (4) stress all three together on the downside deck. When the three are set by different teams on different decks, the structure leaks. Demonstrated across Examples 78.2, 78.3 and 78.5.

## 78.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: reserve statement and competent person's report reconciled to the mine plan; bank deck set, with by-products and FX in the same scenario; reserve tail tested in tonnes and in NPV; four-part completion test; host agreement terms on fiscal stability, carried interest funding and dispute resolution; closure plan, cost estimate and bonding; tailings design standard; power, water and logistics contracts; stream and offtake intercreditor positions.

Red flags: sizing on consensus or spot; physical completion over 30 days at peak rates; a single concentrate offtaker with set-off rights; a state partner whose equity is funded by the sponsor with no repayment path; a mining right granted without the process the law required; closure costs unfunded until late in life.

Rules of thumb with limits: the lender's deck sits below consensus by a margin that widens with volatility (limit: price floors from creditworthy parties); the tail is at least a quarter to a third of reserves (indicative, D-011; limit: long-life deposits with stable grade); completion takes longer than the schedule for underground mines.

Common mistakes: treating by-product credits as independent of the main metal; modeling local costs in USD without FX; ignoring care-and-maintenance cost in a downside; treating political risk insurance as cover for creeping fiscal change.

Questions experts ask: Where is the mine on the cost curve at the bank deck? What happens to cover in year 3 if grade comes in 10% below plan? Who funds the state's share? How was the mining right granted, and has its approval been challenged? What does closure cost now, and who holds the bond?

## 78.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are the DFI investment officer on Salar de Pocitos Litio SA (fictional), a lithium brine operation and hydroxide refinery in Salta province, Argentina (Illustrative), June 2026. Senior debt requested USD 1,137.8 million on a USD 1,986.4 million cost. Hanbit Energy Materials Co. (fictional), a Korean battery maker, offers a 10-year offtake for 60% of hydroxide output with a floor of USD 11,500/t and a ceiling of USD 24,000/t and a USD 150.0 million prepayment. The sponsor asks you to size on the floor at 1.30x and to treat the 40% balance as merchant at 2.00x. Board paper due in two weeks.

Reasoning outline: the floor is only as good as the battery maker's credit and its right to reject off-spec product, which bites during qualification; refinery ramp-up and yield are manufacturing risks (Chapter 77) not covered by the floor; the prepayment ranks somewhere against the senior debt and must be subordinated or capped; the expert sizes contracted volume at the floor only after qualification, uses a stricter merchant deck for the rest, adds a four-part completion test with a yield and specification test, and requires sponsor completion support through qualification.

What would change the answer: an investment-grade offtaker with a take-or-pay and a narrow rejection right (raises debt); government price-floor support of the MP Materials kind or import-linked lending from the buyer's state (ssec:78.2.2, ssec:78.4.2; raises debt, adds policy risk); a sponsor with an operating refinery of the same flowsheet (shortens completion); a host fiscal regime under review (lowers debt or requires PRI).

## 79.6 Worked examples

Example 79.1. Willingness to pay and diversion (Illustrative). An 18.7 km tolled bypass saves 14 minutes against the free route for a car; value of time USD 19.40/hour (2025 prices): willingness to pay = 19.40 x 14/60 = USD 4.53. Binary logit share of the tolled route P = 1/(1 + exp(theta x (toll - WTP))) with theta = 0.92 per USD (a calibration parameter, stated as illustrative). Corridor demand 48,200 car trips a day. At toll USD 2.40: share 87.6%, 42,231 trips, toll revenue USD 101,354 a day. At USD 3.20: 77.2%, 37,218 trips, USD 119,098 a day. At USD 4.10: 59.7%, 28,770 trips, USD 117,958 a day. Revenue-maximizing toll in this model: about USD 3.61 (revenue about USD 121,656 a day). Teaching point: the bidder's revenue is most sensitive to the value of time it assumes, which is exactly what Case T's traffic advisor overstated (Case Bible 2.6; 22% above the later survey); the writer makes that link in one sentence without computing anything for Case T.

Example 79.2. A toll cut at two elasticities (Illustrative, set in the Dulles Greenway's numbers but not presented as its data). Toll falls from USD 1.75 to USD 1.00 (a 42.9% cut). Constant-elasticity demand Q1 = Q0 x (P1/P0)^e. At e = -0.30: traffic rises 18.3%, revenue falls to 67.6% of before. At e = -0.60: traffic rises 39.9%, revenue falls to 79.9% of before. Teaching point: with inelastic demand a toll cut buys traffic and loses revenue; the writer labels this as illustrative arithmetic and does not claim what happened to Greenway revenue. Sasha's habit ("minus point six, not minus point three") may be referenced in the Case T installment, not here.

Example 79.3. Minimum revenue guarantee with a revenue-sharing band (Illustrative). Base-case revenue USD 164.8 million a year. State guarantees 80% of base (pays the shortfall below USD 131.84 million) and takes 50% of revenue above 120% of base (USD 197.76 million). Outcomes: revenue at 62% of base (USD 102.18 million): state pays USD 29.66 million; net USD 131.84 million. At 94% (USD 154.91 million): no payments. At 131% (USD 215.89 million): state receives USD 9.06 million; net USD 206.82 million. Debt sizing with operating costs of USD 38.6 million: on the floor, CFADS USD 93.24 million, debt service at 1.30x = USD 71.72 million; on the base case without guarantee at 1.60x, CFADS USD 126.20 million, debt service USD 78.88 million. Teaching point: the guarantee lets lenders size on the floor at a lower ratio and gets close to base-case debt with far less traffic risk; the state carries a contingent liability that Chapter 57 teaches it to count.

Example 79.4. Least present value of revenue (Illustrative). Winning bid PVR USD 1,284.6 million at a 6.5% contractual discount rate; maximum term 45 years. Base: first-year revenue USD 71.4 million growing 3.5%: concession ends after 27.2 years. High: USD 88.9 million growing 4.0%: 18.9 years. Low: USD 58.6 million growing 2.8%: would need 47.1 years; at the 45-year cap the concessionaire has collected PV USD 1,261.2 million (98.2% of the bid), a shortfall of USD 23.4 million in present value. Teaching point: LPVR converts traffic risk into term risk, which suits debt only if lenders can lend to a variable maturity (cross-reference Chapter 36 on tenor and tail); the cap brings some traffic risk back.

Example 79.5. Revenue build and the cost of losing trucks (Illustrative). A 31.6 km road; 38,450 trips a day; average trip 17.4 km; car toll USD 0.168/km; mix 82.0% cars (1.0), 7.5% light commercial (1.5), 10.5% heavy (3.0); weighted multiplier 1.2475; leakage 2.7%; 365 days. Gross toll revenue USD 51.18 million; net revenue USD 49.80 million. If heavy vehicles fall to 6.0% of the original trips (4.5 points lost; trips fall to 36,720 a day; weighted multiplier 1.1649), net revenue falls to USD 44.41 million, a 10.8% loss for a 4.5% loss of trips. Teaching point: trucks are few and carry a large share of revenue; set the heavy-vehicle multiplier against the free route's cost for trucks (the Case T problem, Case Bible 2.6).

Example 79.6. Catch-up arithmetic (Illustrative). Actual traffic in year 1 is 65% of forecast; forecast grows 3.4% a year. If actual grows 6.0% a year, it meets the forecast path after ln(1/0.65)/ln(1.06/1.034) = 17.3 years; at 4.5% growth, 40.7 years. Equation 79.2. Teaching point: a large first-year gap does not close through faster growth within a debt tenor unless the cause is timing (development arriving later); otherwise it is a level shift and the debt must be resized.

## 79.8 Frameworks

Framework 79.1, the demand-risk sharing menu (original; label fw:demand-risk-menu). Purpose: choose how much demand risk the concessionaire should carry. Steps: (1) score traffic predictability (history, free alternative, captive demand); (2) score the government's fiscal capacity and accounting constraints (cross-reference Chapter 57); (3) pick from the menu: full transfer, revenue band or collar, minimum revenue guarantee, flexible-term (LPVR), availability payment with real tolls retained by the state; (4) check bankability with the lenders' sizing logic (Example 79.3); (5) check incentives (who still cares about traffic). When to use: at the procurement design stage and when a bidder proposes changes.

Framework 79.2, the ramp-up diagnosis (original; label fw:ramp-up-diagnosis). Purpose: classify a traffic shortfall. Steps: (1) separate volume from yield; (2) separate by class, by time of day and by origin-destination; (3) test each component against the forecast's assumptions: development timing, value of time, toll by class against the free route, network changes, macro shocks; (4) classify each as timing (closes as the cause arrives), structural (permanent level shift) or policy-fixable (closes only if a tariff or network change is made); (5) translate into debt consequences: wait (timing), resize or restructure (structural), negotiate (policy-fixable). Demonstrated in the walkthrough and in the Case T installment.

## 79.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: free-alternative cost by class and trip length; value of time source and date (stated preference against revealed preference); development assumptions with independent evidence; ramp-up profile against the reference class; toll escalation formula and political tolerance; competing-facility clause; relief and compensation events for demand shocks; back-office leakage and enforcement; lifecycle for tunnel systems; refinancing structure.

Red flags: winning forecast above every other forecast; upfront concession payment tied to the bidder's traffic case; trucks priced above the free route's cost; ramp-up shorter than 18 months in a new corridor; bullet debt or accreting swaps on a demand-risk asset; promised public works not contracted.

Rules of thumb with limits: ramp-up of 18 months to 3 years is normal for a new urban road (NSW Auditor-General; limit: a corridor waiting for development); a first-year gap above about a quarter rarely closes within a debt tenor (Example 79.6 logic; limit: identified timing causes); truck share drives revenue disproportionately.

Common mistakes: holding traffic constant when tolls change; treating ramp-up as a percentage applied to a correct mature level; using peak-hour design models for daily revenue (the RTA's admission); adding growth on top of an over-forecast base.

Questions experts ask: What does a truck pay on the free road? Who decides the toll escalation in practice? Which forecast is the outlier? What happens to the debt if year three looks like year one? What did the government promise to build, and is it in the contract?

## 79.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are credit officer for the senior lenders to a greenfield toll bridge (Illustrative) 14 months after opening; traffic is 71% of the banking case; the sponsor proposes a 30% toll discount for 12 months to "build habit", funded from the DSRA, and asks for the lock-up to be waived during the discount period. Lenders' meeting in five days.

Reasoning outline: test the proposal with elasticity (Example 79.2): at the plausible elasticity, revenue falls during the discount; habit formation is a weak empirical claim; the DSRA is the lenders' liquidity, not marketing money; diagnose the shortfall first (Framework 79.2): if the cause is a missing free-route restriction or development timing, a discount does not help. The expert allows a targeted discount (for example trucks, if the truck toll exceeds the free-route cost) funded by sponsor equity, keeps the lock-up, commissions a ramp-up diagnosis, and agrees in advance the trigger for a re-forecast and resizing.

What would change the answer: evidence from transaction data that price is the main barrier for a class (supports a targeted discount); a contracting authority willing to fund the discount or deliver the promised network change (changes who pays); a sponsor equity injection to fund the trial (removes the DSRA objection).

## 80.6 Worked examples

Example 80.1. Single till against dual till (Illustrative). Airport with regulated asset base USD 2,384.7 million, real pre-tax WACC 5.85%, regulatory depreciation USD 118.4 million, operating costs USD 241.6 million, commercial revenue USD 286.3 million, 31.2 million passengers. Single till: allowed revenue = 2,384.7 x 5.85% + 118.4 + 241.6 = USD 499.5 million (return USD 139.5 million); aeronautical charge = (499.5 - 286.3)/31.2 = USD 6.83 per passenger. Dual till: aeronautical RAB 82% of total; aeronautical depreciation USD 97.1 million; aeronautical opex USD 179.2 million; aeronautical revenue requirement = 0.82 x 139.5 + 97.1 + 179.2 = USD 390.7 million; charge USD 12.52 per passenger. The airport keeps commercial profit of USD 177.5 million under dual till (286.3 less commercial share of return 25.1, commercial depreciation 21.3 and commercial opex 62.4). Teaching point: the till decides whether shops subsidize airlines; airlines lobby for single till, investors for dual till; lenders care about the stability of the regime more than its choice.

Example 80.2. A container terminal's minimum annual guarantee (Illustrative). Concession fee: fixed USD 18.6 million a year plus USD 14.20 per TEU, with a MAG of 850,000 TEU billed if throughput falls short. Terminal tariff USD 182.00 per TEU; variable opex USD 74.50 per TEU; fixed opex USD 46.8 million. At 1,210,000 TEU: fees USD 35.78 million; revenue USD 220.22 million; EBITDA USD 47.49 million (21.6% margin). At 905,000 TEU: fees USD 31.45 million; revenue USD 164.71 million; EBITDA USD 19.04 million. At 620,000 TEU (an alliance moves its transshipment calls): MAG binds, fees USD 30.67 million against USD 27.40 million without the MAG; revenue USD 112.84 million; EBITDA (10.82) million. Teaching point: the MAG transfers volume risk to the operator and makes the downside steeper; lenders ask who brings the volume (anchor-line contracts) before they look at the MAG.

Example 80.3. Farebox recovery of a light-rail line (Illustrative). 61,400 boardings a day, 350 effective days, average fare USD 1.85: fare revenue USD 39.76 million. Operating cost USD 64.3 million: farebox recovery 61.8%. Availability payment to the concessionaire USD 112.7 million a year (capital, maintenance and operations). Net cost to the authority after fares: USD 72.94 million, or USD 3.39 per boarding. Teaching point: a transit line's credit is the authority's budget, not the farebox; lenders analyze appropriation risk and the payment mechanism, not ridership.

Example 80.4. Leasing a fleet with a residual value (Illustrative). Fleet cost USD 412.6 million; 25-year lease; lessor's rate 6.1%; expected residual value 15% of cost at year 25 (useful life 35 years). PV of residual = 61.89/(1.061^25) = USD 14.08 million. Annual rental (in arrears) = (412.6 - 14.08)/annuity factor = USD 31.47 million; without a residual assumption USD 32.58 million. Teaching point: the lessor's residual assumption lowers the rent by USD 1.11 million a year and puts technology and re-lease risk on the lessor; a lender to the lessor tests whether trains of that type can be re-leased.

Example 80.5. An airport through a passenger shock (Illustrative). 18.4 million passengers; aeronautical revenue USD 14.60 and commercial USD 11.85 per passenger; variable cost USD 4.10 per passenger; fixed costs USD 168.3 million; debt service USD 112.6 million. Base: revenue USD 486.7 million; CFADS USD 242.9 million; DSCR 2.16x. At 38% of base traffic: CFADS (12.0) million; debt service unpaid without liquidity. At 71% of base: CFADS USD 123.7 million; DSCR 1.10x. Breakeven traffic for 1.00x: 12.57 million passengers, 68.3% of base. Teaching point: airports with high fixed costs need liquidity (DSRA, revolver, covenant holidays) more than high base cover; tie to the pandemic responses in 80.5.1.

## 80.8 Frameworks

Framework 80.1, the footloose-demand test (original; label fw:footloose-demand). Purpose: judge how easily a transport asset's users can take their demand elsewhere. Steps: (1) identify the decision-maker for demand (passenger, airline, shipping line, authority); (2) identify the alternatives and the switching cost and time; (3) identify contractual locks (anchor-line agreements, airline use agreements, concession exclusivity); (4) score as captive, contestable or footloose; (5) link the score to structure (availability or regulation for captive; contracted anchor volumes and higher cover for contestable; short tenor, high cover or no project debt for footloose). ## 80.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: who sets fares and charges; demand decision-maker and alternatives (Framework 80.1); utilities and third-party interface register; systems integration responsibility; operator interface agreement; anchor-user contracts and their tenor against the debt; regulatory regime and review cycle; liquidity for demand shocks; supply-chain governance where shareholders are contractors.

Red flags: shareholders who are also the main contractors with no independent cost control; an extended-delay termination right without owner-delay carve-outs; a transshipment terminal financed on throughput without line commitments; airport debt sized on peak-year passengers; periodic reviews without clear "efficient cost" tests.

Rules of thumb with limits: transit is a budget credit (limit: where fare revenue is pledged); captive gateway ports are more bankable than transshipment hubs (limit: alliance contracts with take-or-pay); high fixed-cost airports need liquidity sized for a severe shock.

Common mistakes: treating ridership as the transit lender's risk under availability payment; modeling MAG as revenue rather than cost; ignoring commercial revenue's dependence on passenger mix; equating a public guarantee with risk transfer.

Questions experts ask: Who chooses whether this demand comes here? How long does it take them to leave? What does the state do when a shock hits, and what did it do last time? Which interface is not in anyone's contract? Who owns the supply chain?

## 80.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are a lender asked to refinance a container terminal (Illustrative) with 12 years of throughput history; 46% of volume comes from one alliance whose terminal services agreement expires in three years; the sponsor wants debt sized on the 12-year average with a 15-year tenor. Credit committee in two weeks.

Reasoning outline: score the terminal on Framework 80.1 (transshipment share and alliance concentration make it contestable or footloose); the history proves the alliance chose the terminal, not that it will stay; the expert sizes bankable volume on gateway cargo plus contracted alliance volume, adds a cash sweep and a tenor step-down if the alliance agreement is not renewed by a set date, or requires renewal before close; considers MAG exposure in the downside (Example 80.2).

What would change the answer: an alliance shareholding in the terminal or a renewal with take-or-pay to beyond the debt tenor (raises bankable volume); no competing terminal within the region (moves toward captive); port authority willingness to adjust the MAG in a downturn (reduces downside).

## 81.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. In these sectors the payer is usually a public body or a utility, not a market: a health authority pays for a hospital being available, a single buyer pays for desalinated water, a municipality pays a gate fee for waste. That makes the revenue look safe and the debt look cheap, which is why gearing is high and cover thin. This chapter teaches where the risk goes when payment is near-certain: into construction and contractor credit (Carillion), into lifecycle and service performance, into the payer's own collection chain (Tideway and Thames Water), into feedstock quality and quantity (waste), and into tariffs that a public must be able to afford (water). It covers four subsectors and the record of Carillion's hospitals, Tideway and the Gulf's desalination tenders.

What you will be able to do:
- Explain why availability-based social infrastructure supports high gearing and thin cover, and test a hospital's cover against a deduction (Capabilities 5, 9).
- Build a desalination tariff from capacity and output charges and compare it with real Gulf tariffs (Capabilities 4, 13).
- Analyze a waste-to-energy plant's revenue stack and its sensitivity to waste quantity and calorific value (Capabilities 2, 13).

## 81.6 Worked examples

Example 81.1. Gearing and cover in a hospital availability PPP (Illustrative). Capital cost USD 486.2 million; debt 90% (USD 437.6 million) at 5.4% over 27 years, level annuity debt service USD 31.16 million; capital element of the unitary charge set at 1.20x debt service (USD 37.39 million); hard FM USD 21.4 million, lifecycle USD 6.8 million, project company costs USD 2.3 million; unitary charge USD 67.89 million. A bad year brings deductions of 3.1% of the unitary charge (USD 2.10 million). If deductions are not passed down, DSCR falls from 1.20x to 1.13x. If the FM contract passes deductions down with a cap at 10% of the FM fee (USD 2.14 million), the full deduction is passed down and DSCR stays at 1.20x. Teaching point: thin cover is safe only if deductions and cost overruns are passed down to creditworthy subcontractors; the passdown cap is the real risk buffer.

Example 81.2. Building a desalination tariff (Illustrative). A 600,000 m3/day RO plant; capital cost USD 640.3 million; 85% debt (USD 544.3 million) at 5.85% over 25 years: debt service USD 41.97 million; equity USD 96.0 million recovered at 10% over 25 years: USD 10.58 million; fixed O&M USD 14.6 million. Capacity charge USD 67.15 million a year; at 95% availability (208.05 million m3 a year) USD 0.3228/m3. Implied DSCR on the capacity charge net of fixed O&M: 1.25x. Output charge: energy 3.4 kWh/m3 x USD 0.052/kWh = USD 0.1768, chemicals USD 0.031, membranes USD 0.018, variable O&M USD 0.012: USD 0.2378/m3. Total tariff USD 0.5606/m3. Teaching point: compare with Jubail 3A (SAR 1.55/m3, about USD 0.41 at the peg, computed) and Hassyan (USD 0.365/m3): the exercise set asks which inputs a winning bidder must have changed (tenor, cost of debt, energy price, capex per m3).

Example 81.3. A waste-to-energy plant's revenue stack (Illustrative). 412,000 t a year of residual waste; gate fee USD 96.40/t: USD 39.72 million. Net calorific value 9.8 MJ/kg; net electrical efficiency 26.5%: electricity 297,212 MWh; at USD 71.20/MWh: USD 21.16 million. Residue costs: bottom ash 21% of input at USD 18.50/t (USD 1.60 million); air-pollution-control residues 3.5% at USD 142.00/t (USD 2.05 million). If NCV falls to 8.6 MJ/kg, the plant can burn up to its 440,000 t mechanical limit: gate fees USD 42.42 million, electricity 278,544 MWh (USD 19.83 million). If NCV rises to 11.2 MJ/kg (more plastics), thermal capacity caps tonnage at 360,500 t: gate fees fall to USD 34.75 million (a USD 4.96 million loss) while electricity stays at USD 21.16 million. Teaching point: in WtE "better" waste can cost money; the model must cap throughput by thermal capacity.

Example 81.4. Non-revenue water and cash revenue (Illustrative). A municipal concession produces 412.6 million m3 a year; collection rate 87%; average tariff USD 0.94/m3. At 42% non-revenue water, billed volume 239.3 million m3 and cash revenue USD 195.7 million. At 30%, billed volume 288.8 million m3 and cash revenue USD 236.2 million: USD 40.5 million more without new production. Teaching point: in water concessions, operational performance on losses and collection is worth more than tariff increases that politics may refuse.

Example 81.5. Cost to complete after a contractor fails (Illustrative). A hospital project company has USD 184.6 million of work left under a USD 412.0 million fixed-price contract when the contractor enters liquidation. A replacement contractor prices the remaining work 62% higher, at USD 299.1 million, and excludes latent defects in completed work. Resources: undrawn debt equal to the original remaining cost (USD 184.6 million); contingency USD 18.3 million; performance bond 10% of contract price (USD 41.2 million); retention and LDs held USD 12.4 million; total extra resources USD 71.9 million. Shortfall USD 42.6 million (299.1 - 184.6 - 71.9). Teaching point: the bond and contingency rarely cover the replacement premium; the shortfall falls on equity, then on lenders through termination compensation (cross-reference Chapter 58 for compensation on project company default and Chapter 61 for the process).

## 81.8 Frameworks

Framework 81.1, the payment-chain trace (original; label fw:payment-chain). Purpose: find the weak link between the end-payer and the project company when revenue looks certain. Steps: (1) name the end-payer (taxpayer through budget, water customer through bill, municipality through gate fee, power market); (2) list each intermediary that collects or appropriates the money (ministry, utility, single buyer, municipality); (3) for each link, state the credit, the legal obligation and the collection or appropriation risk; (4) identify the protections (guarantees, escrow, licence obligations, reconciliation mechanisms); (5) identify the link most likely to break in a downturn and size liquidity for it. Demonstrated on Exhibit 81.3 and in Section 81.10 (Tideway's collection by Thames Water). Distinct from Chapter 28's "Who pays if...?" trace, which follows a loss through contracts; this traces the revenue to its source.

## 81.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: payer and appropriation process; deduction regime and passdown caps; contractor credit, bonds, parent guarantees, replacement-contractor plan; latent-defect allocation; lifecycle plan and reserve; for water, tariff-setting process, collection rates and non-revenue water plan; for desal, energy supply and price, membrane plan, intake risk; for WtE, waste supply contracts, composition forecasts, thermal capacity, residue outlets, emissions permits.

Red flags: a single contractor for D&C and FM who is also the main shareholder; passdown caps far below plausible deductions; WtE sized on tonnage without a thermal-capacity cap; a water concession dependent on tariff increases not yet approved; a carve-out whose revenue is collected by a stressed incumbent.

Rules of thumb with limits: availability PPP debt is a construction credit first (limit: brownfield refinancings); WtE gate fees from long municipal contracts support more debt than merchant gate fees; desal tariffs are mostly capital and energy (limit: tenor and cost of debt drive the capital part).

Common mistakes: treating unitary charge as risk-free revenue during construction; modeling deductions without passdown; ignoring that recycling changes waste composition; assuming a utility collector's credit equals the regulator's protection.

Questions experts ask: Who actually writes the check, and from which budget line? What happens if the builder disappears tomorrow? What deductions does the FM contractor bear, and can it pay them? What does a tonne of waste look like in ten years? Who pays for energy above the deemed price?

## 82.8 Frameworks

Framework 82.1, the lease-life gap test (original; label fw:lease-life-gap). Purpose: expose mismatches between the lives that matter in a digital asset. Steps: (1) list four lives: firm lease term, debt tenor, technology life of what the tenant uses the asset for, and power-contract or interconnection life; (2) plot them on one timeline (Exhibit 82.2); (3) for each gap where debt outlives the firm lease, name what covers it (renewal economics, RVG, re-leasing market, amortization before the gap); (4) for each gap where technology life is shorter than the lease, test the tenant's incentive to renew; (5) set sizing, amortization and sweeps so that debt outstanding at each gap is covered by the named support. Demonstrated in Example 82.3, the walkthrough and Section 82.9.

## 82.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: tenant list and credit; lease or MLA terms (term, escalator, cancellation, renewal); power arrangements and interconnection status; lease-life gap timeline; for towers, tenancy and amendment pipeline, ground lease terms; for fiber, homes passed, penetration curve, churn, competing networks, wholesale contracts; for data centers, MW energized against contracted, PUE, cooling water, RVG terms.

Red flags: debt tenor beyond firm lease without RVG or proven renewal; a single neo-cloud tenant on a short lease; penetration assumptions above the operator's existing footprint; power interconnection "expected" but not contracted; towers dependent on an operator that is merging.

Rules of thumb with limits: data-center debt is tenant debt (limit: multi-tenant colocation with diversified short leases); tower credit improves with each tenant; fiber is an equity risk until penetration is proven.

Common mistakes: lending to building life when the tenant's equipment lives five years; modeling power as a pass-through when the lease does not say so; counting signed letters of intent as leases; ignoring operator consolidation.

Questions experts ask: When does the firm lease end, and what is owed then? Who pays for power, and what if it is not available on time? What does the tenant use the building for, and how long will that technology last? Who else could build a competing network or campus here?

## 82.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are a credit officer asked to lead a USD 1,140.0 million senior loan (Illustrative) for a 120 MW AI data center leased for five years to a single neo-cloud tenant (non-investment-grade, contract with a large AI lab behind it), April 2026. What would change the answer: an investment-grade parent guarantee or RVG (moves toward hyperscale terms); a 15-year lease (reduces the gap); a second tenant pre-let for part of the capacity; evidence of strong re-leasing demand at the site because of scarce power (supports a balloon, but only with conservative sizing).

## 83.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Hydrogen, captured carbon and sustainable fuels are products whose demand is made by policy and whose price has no deep market. A lender cannot size debt on a price curve that does not exist, so every financeable project in this chapter rests on a contract or a state instrument that creates the price: a long take-if-tendered offtake from a creditworthy sponsor (NEOM), a state that pays most of the capital and absorbs the cross-chain risk (Northern Lights), a licensed transport-and-storage business with revenue support (the UK clusters), or a contract for difference. This chapter teaches the cost economics of each pathway, the revenue models that make them bankable, the risks particular to first-of-a-kind molecule chains, and how to trace who really holds the market risk.

What you will be able to do:
- Compute a levelized cost of hydrogen and its sensitivity to capacity factor and power price, and convert hydrogen into ammonia volume and revenue (Capabilities 4, 13).
- Compare take-or-pay, take-if-tendered and CfD-style revenue support for a molecule project and size the risk each leaves with lenders (Capabilities 2, 5).
- Analyze cross-chain risk in carbon capture and storage and the state's role in absorbing it (Capabilities 8, 9).
- Trace who holds the market risk in any new-molecule project, and judge whether it is bankable (Capabilities 14, 15).

Levelized cost of hydrogen (Illustrative). 400 MW electrolyzer; installed system cost USD 2,250/kW (USD 900.0 million); efficiency 53.0 kWh/kg; capacity factor 58%; power at USD 34.60/MWh; fixed O&M 3% of capex a year (USD 27.0 million); stack replacement of 25% of capex in year 10 (PV USD 103.3 million at 8.1%); water 9 liters per kg at USD 2.10/m3; WACC 8.1%; 25 years (capital recovery factor 0.09448). Output 38,346 t a year. Annualized capital USD 94.79 million; power USD 70.32 million; O&M USD 27.0 million; water USD 0.72 million. LCOH USD 5.03/kg: capital USD 2.47, power USD 1.83, O&M USD 0.70, water USD 0.02. Sensitivities: capacity factor 42% gives USD 6.24/kg; 70% gives USD 4.48/kg; power at USD 24.00/MWh gives USD 4.47/kg; at USD 48.00/MWh, USD 5.74/kg. Teaching point: capital and utilization dominate; cheap power at low capacity factor can cost more than dearer power at high capacity factor.

Example 83.2. From hydrogen to ammonia (Illustrative). Using Example 83.1's 38,346 t of hydrogen and a conversion of 0.1795 t of hydrogen per tonne of ammonia (stoichiometric 3/17 = 0.1765 plus losses): 213,625 t of ammonia a year. At USD 640/t, revenue USD 136.7 million. Teaching point: the derivative's price, not hydrogen's, sets revenue; ammonia's existing market gives a reference price but not a premium for low emissions. (Cross-check for the reader: NEOM's up to 600 t a day of hydrogen and up to about 1.2 million t a year of ammonia are consistent with a similar ratio; the writer states this only as a consistency check, not as a NEOM fact beyond the fact sheet.)

Example 83.3. A hydrogen CfD and volume risk (Illustrative). Strike price USD 7.15/kg on 24,800 t a year; reference price (a natural-gas-linked benchmark) at USD 2.10, USD 3.40 and USD 5.25/kg. Difference payments: USD 125.24 million, USD 93.00 million and USD 47.12 million; the producer's total (sale at reference plus difference) stays USD 177.32 million. If the offtaker takes only 70% of volume and the CfD pays only on sold volume, revenue falls to USD 124.12 million. Teaching point: a CfD removes price risk but not volume risk; lenders still need an offtaker for the molecules (cross-reference Chapter 19 for CfD mechanics).

Example 83.4. Cross-chain stranding in CCS (Illustrative). A transport-and-storage system with fixed costs of USD 94.7 million a year and variable costs of USD 6.80/t, built for 1.5 Mtpa: unit cost USD 69.93/t. If one emitter's 0.8 Mtpa capture plant fails and only 0.7 Mtpa flows: unit cost USD 142.09/t. Teaching point: under cost-of-service pricing remaining emitters pay double; under fixed tariffs the T&S owner loses; someone (often the state) must absorb the stranding risk; this is why Northern Lights' state aid and the UK's revenue support exist.

Example 83.5. A SAF plant's revenue stack (Illustrative). HEFA plant processing 350,000 t a year of used cooking oil at USD 1,120/t; product yield 0.83 t per t of feed; processing cost USD 265/t of feed; conventional jet value USD 742/t; airline green premium USD 610/t; policy credit value USD 690/t of fuel (illustrative, not a statement of any law's value). Margin per tonne of feed: with credits USD 309.86 (USD 108.45 million a year); without credits (USD 262.84) (USD 91.99 million loss); jet value only (USD 769.14). Breakevens: feedstock at USD 1,429.86/t with credits; credit value of USD 316.67/t of fuel. Teaching point: the plant is a spread business (Framework 77.1) whose spread is mostly policy; lenders size only on contracted premium and credits with a term matching the debt.

## 83.7 Walkthrough

Walkthrough: marking up a hydrogen offtake term sheet (Section 83.11). Exhibit 83.5 (Illustrative term sheet for a 15-year green ammonia offtake).
1. Volume: annual contract quantity, take-or-pay or take-if-tendered, make-up and carry-forward.
2. Specification and certification: product spec, emissions-intensity certification, consequences of failing either.
3. Price: fixed, indexed (to what), floor; who bears the green premium if policy support changes.
4. Delivery and shipping: point of delivery, shipping responsibility, demurrage.
5. Credit support: parent guarantee, letter of credit, rating triggers.
6. Termination: offtaker's rights (change in law, project delay), termination payments and whether they cover debt.
7. Assignment and direct agreement for lenders (cross-reference Chapter 28).
8. Mark up as lenders' counsel (Clause 83.1b logic) and apply Framework 83.1 to the result.

## 83.8 Frameworks

Framework 83.1, the market-risk holder trace (original; label fw:market-risk-trace). Purpose: find who ultimately bears the price and volume risk of a new molecule. Steps: (1) state the product and its nearest liquid market; (2) list each contract or instrument between the plant and the end-user, and for each say whether it fixes price, volume, both or neither; (3) identify the party that bears the residual (offtaker, state, consumer, lenders, equity); (4) test that party's credit and its incentive to keep performing if the market moves against it; (5) test the term of the protection against the debt tenor; (6) decide bankable volume and price. Demonstrated on NEOM, Northern Lights, Example 83.3 and the Case R installment. Distinct from Chapter 88's new-structure framework, which evaluates whole structures; this trace answers the narrower lender question for molecule projects.

## 83.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: identify the nearest liquid market and the price gap; list every price- or volume-fixing contract and its counterparty credit; check policy support terms against debt tenor and change-in-law protection; check power supply shape against electrolyzer or plant needs; check technology guarantees (efficiency, degradation) and integrator wrap; for CCS, map every link's owner and who absorbs stranding; for SAF, check feedstock contracts, certification and credit eligibility.

Red flags: memoranda of understanding presented as offtake; offtake shorter than debt; a sponsor who is offtaker and contractor with no independent enforcement path; CCS links contracted bilaterally with no party absorbing interface risk; revenue that depends on credits with a policy deadline inside the construction period.

Rules of thumb with limits: no contract, no debt (limit: state capital grants and regulated revenue); capacity factor beats power price for LCOH within the normal range; in CCS the capture plant is the weakest link.

Common mistakes: treating a CfD as removing volume risk; modeling electrolyzers at nameplate with flat power; ignoring stack replacement; assuming a green premium persists after policy changes.

Questions experts ask: Who pays if the green premium disappears? What is the bankable volume if the offtaker takes only the minimum? Who absorbs the cost if one link of the chain fails? When does the support end compared with the debt? What did the last first-of-a-kind plant of this type cost against its budget?

## 83.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are a DFI investment officer (Illustrative), March 2026, considering senior debt for a 120,000 t a year e-methanol plant in a North African country. A container shipping line offers a 10-year offtake for 50% of output at a fixed price with CPI indexation; the remaining 50% would be sold on spot to bunkering markets. Debt tenor requested 15 years; sponsor asks for 70% gearing on a USD 1,214.6 million cost. Board in three weeks.

Reasoning outline: apply Framework 83.1: the shipping line holds price risk for half the volume for ten years; the rest and the tail are merchant in a market without depth; t-market-norms' practitioner ceiling of 25% to 30% merchant suggests the merchant half is not bankable on its own; size on contracted volume over the contract term, treat the rest as equity upside or require a second offtaker, consider blended finance (Chapter 34) for the tail, require completion support given first-of-a-kind integration, and test the shipping line's credit and termination rights.

What would change the answer: a second offtake for the remaining volume; state support (CfD or auction) covering the tail; a sponsor with operating experience of the technology; a shorter tenor matching the contract.
