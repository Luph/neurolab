# Chapter 77. Refining, petrochemicals and manufacturing (gigafactory) finance

## 77.1 Header

- Chapter: 77
- Title: Refining, petrochemicals and manufacturing (gigafactory) finance
- File: `chapters/77-industrial-projects.tex`
- Target length: 9,000 to 10,000 words
- Dominant lens: a senior project finance banker who has run ECA-heavy industrial financings and sat on the credit committee for a manufacturing deal, with the independent engineer's view of multi-unit completion testing.

## 77.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Industrial plants turn a bought input into a sold output, and they earn the spread between the two. That makes them the hardest assets in the book to fit into project finance, whose tools were built for contracted, availability-style revenue. This chapter teaches the reader to see a refinery, a petrochemical complex and a battery-cell gigafactory as spread or margin businesses; to identify who carries margin risk, ramp-up risk and supply-chain risk in each; to recognize the structures that make such plants financeable (integrated sponsors, feedstock and offtake arrangements, processing fees, sponsor completion guarantees released only on demanding tests, ECA anchoring); and to explain why Sadara and Northvolt Ett, both financed by sophisticated lender groups, ended up where they did.

What you will be able to do:
- Compute a refining crack spread, a cracker cash margin and a factory's breakeven yield, and say which number a lender should size against (Capabilities 4, 13).
- Rank the ways an industrial sponsor can protect a margin, from merchant sale to processing fee, and show how each moves debt capacity (Capabilities 2, 5).
- Read and test a multi-unit completion test and the sponsor guarantee it releases (Capabilities 3, 10).
- Explain, from the Sadara and Northvolt records, why sponsor-supported industrial debt still carries market and ramp-up risk (Capabilities 13, 15).

## 77.3 Concepts owned and assumed

Owned (taught in full here):
1. Spread (margin) business as a revenue type: revenue equals product value less feedstock cost; why lenders treat it as the riskiest revenue class short of pure merchant power.
2. Refining economics at financier depth: crack spreads (3-2-1 as the teaching benchmark), gross refining margin, capture rate, configuration and the Nelson complexity index (credited to W. L. Nelson, as an established measure), crude differentials, turnarounds.
3. Petrochemical economics: feedstock advantage (ethane against naphtha), co-product credits, cash cost margin per tonne of ethylene, integration with refining and with feedstock suppliers, the capacity-wave cycle.
4. Manufacturing (gigafactory) economics: nameplate, throughput ramp, yield and scrap, unit cost per good unit, breakeven yield, learning curves and product-price deflation.
5. Industrial customer contracts: supply agreements with volume commitments, capacity reservation fees, customer prepayments; why order books are not take-or-pay.
6. Processing (tolling) arrangements in industrial plants: the processor earns a fee per tonne and the toller owns input and output. (Power tolling agreements are owned by Chapter 18; this is the industrial form.)
7. Framework 77.1, the margin protection ladder (original).
8. Technology licenses and process performance guarantees from licensors; the gap between a licensor's guarantee and the lenders' loss.
9. Multi-unit completion tests for industrial complexes: envelopes of units, reliability runs, product specification tests, and financial completion tests that release sponsor guarantees.
10. Feedstock supply and product marketing agreements with integrated sponsors (sponsor marketing, lifting, marketing fees).
11. Industrial supply-chain and geopolitical risk as a credit risk (single-route feedstock and product logistics; dependence on one country's equipment and materials).
12. Industrial policy finance in manufacturing: public lenders and guarantors (EIB, ECAs, national guarantee offices) and conditional commitments drawn on milestones; the difference between commitments and funding.
13. Modeling specifics for industrial plants: joint price decks for inputs and outputs, margin correlation, yield and throughput ramp curves, inventory-heavy working capital, turnaround downtime.

## 77.6 Worked examples

Example 77.1. Crack spread and refinery EBITDA (Illustrative). A 150,000 bbl/d coastal cracking refinery, 2025 prices. Crude USD 76.80/bbl; gasoline USD 2.24/gal; diesel USD 2.52/gal; 42 gal/bbl. Compute: gasoline USD 94.08/bbl, diesel USD 105.84/bbl; 3-2-1 crack = (2 x 94.08 + 105.84 - 3 x 76.80)/3 = USD 21.20/bbl. Utilization 91.5% gives 50,096,250 bbl a year. Capture rate 83% gives a realized margin of USD 17.60/bbl. Cash opex USD 5.20/bbl. EBITDA = 50.10 million bbl x (17.60 - 5.20) = USD 621.0 million. Re-run at a trough crack of USD 12.85/bbl (realized 10.67; EBITDA USD 273.8 million) and USD 8.10/bbl (realized 6.72; EBITDA USD 76.3 million). Breakeven crack = 5.20/0.83 = USD 6.27/bbl. Teaching point: a 62% fall in the benchmark spread cuts EBITDA by 88%; the lender sizes on the trough, not the average.

Example 77.2. Ethane against naphtha cracker margins (Illustrative). Per tonne of ethylene, ethylene price USD 1,040/t. Ethane cracker: 1.24 t ethane per t ethylene at USD 245/t (feed cost USD 303.80), co-product credit USD 62, cash conversion cost USD 168: cash margin USD 630.20/t. Naphtha cracker: 3.20 t naphtha per t ethylene at USD 640/t (feed cost USD 2,048.00), co-product credits (propylene, butadiene, aromatics) USD 1,512, conversion cost USD 214: cash margin USD 290.00/t. At 1.5 million t a year of ethylene: USD 945.3 million against USD 435.0 million. Sensitivities: naphtha up 15% turns the naphtha margin to minus USD 17.20/t; ethane doubling to USD 490/t leaves the ethane margin at USD 326.40/t. Teaching point: feedstock advantage, and why a naphtha cracker's credit depends on co-product markets as much as on ethylene.

Example 77.3. Processing fee against merchant sale for a urea plant (Illustrative). A 1.21 million t a year gas-based urea plant, project cost USD 1,386.4 million, fixed cash costs USD 41.7 million a year borne by the project company in both cases (gas is supplied and paid for by the toller under the processing agreement; under merchant sale, gas cost is inside the margin). Case A, processing fee USD 162.40/t from an investment-grade toller, 12-year amortizing debt at 7.25%, sized at 1.35x: revenue USD 196.5 million; CFADS USD 154.8 million; debt service USD 114.7 million; annuity factor 7.8379; debt USD 898.8 million (64.8% of cost). Case B, merchant: P50 margin USD 214/t and P90 margin USD 131/t over gas cost; lenders size at the lesser of 1.80x on P50 and 1.25x on P90, 10 years at 7.75%: CFADS USD 217.2 million (P50) and USD 116.8 million (P90); debt service USD 120.7 million and USD 93.4 million, so P90 binds at USD 93.4 million; annuity factor 6.7864; debt USD 634.2 million (45.7%). Difference USD 264.6 million of debt. Writer states the DSCR targets as illustrative and does not present them as market norms.

Example 77.4. Gigafactory ramp, yield and breakeven (Illustrative). A 16.0 GWh (nameplate, cells started) plant; good-cell price USD 98.0/kWh; materials USD 58.0 and variable conversion USD 6.5 per kWh started (scrap consumes materials); fixed cash costs USD 318.4 million a year. Year 1 to 4: throughput 25%, 55%, 80%, 92% of nameplate; yield 72%, 84%, 91%, 94%. Results: good output 2.880, 7.392, 11.648, 13.837 GWh; revenue USD 282.2, 724.4, 1,141.5, 1,356.0 million; variable cost USD 258.0, 567.6, 825.6, 949.4 million; EBITDA (294.2), (161.6), (2.5), 88.2 USD million; cash cost per good kWh USD 200.14, 119.86, 98.21, 91.63. Breakeven yield: at 55% throughput 102.7% (impossible), at 80% throughput 91.2%, at 92% throughput 87.9%. Equations: unit cost = (started x (materials + variable conversion) + fixed)/(started x yield); breakeven yield = (started x (m + v) + F)/(started x price). Teaching point: below a throughput threshold no yield saves the plant; the lender's completion test must test throughput and yield together.

Example 77.5. Reading a reliability-run result against a three-envelope completion test (Illustrative). Test (Clause 77.2): each envelope must average at least 95.0% of nameplate output over 60 consecutive days within a 90-day window, with off-specification product at most 2.0%; financial completion requires historical DSCR of at least 1.25x for two consecutive quarters. Results: envelope A (cracker) 96.8%, off-spec 1.1%; envelope B (derivatives) 93.9%, off-spec 1.6%; envelope C (aromatics and utilities) 97.4%, off-spec 0.7%; quarter 1 CFADS USD 61.4 million against debt service USD 46.2 million (1.33x); quarter 2 USD 58.9 million against USD 47.0 million (1.253x, passes at 1.25x when computed to three decimals, 1.25x at two). Outcome: technical completion fails on envelope B; the financial test passes; the guarantee stays in place; the writer shows the retest options (retest window, partial release by envelope if the clause allows it, and why lenders resist partial release when envelopes depend on each other). Teaching point: two-decimal rounding of a ratio at a threshold is a drafting issue; the clause must say how the ratio is computed and rounded.

## 77.8 Frameworks

Framework 77.1, the margin protection ladder (original; label fw:margin-ladder). Purpose: rank how firmly an industrial plant's margin is protected and predict the debt it supports. Rungs, from weakest to strongest:
1. Merchant sale of products and spot purchase of feedstock.
2. Sponsor marketing or lifting agreement without price support (the marketer sells; the project company keeps the price risk).
3. Formula-linked feedstock supply, where feedstock price moves with product price (a partial natural hedge) or a sponsor feedstock agreement with volume commitment.
4. Margin floor or offtake with floor price from a creditworthy party (sponsor or customer), or a customer take-or-pay with capacity reservation fee.
5. Processing fee (tolling) from a creditworthy toller who owns inputs and outputs.
Steps in use: place each revenue stream on a rung; weight by share of revenue; test the credit of the party on each rung; size debt rung by rung (cross-reference the bucket sizing of Chapter 36). Demonstrated on Example 77.3 and in the Case P installment. When to use: any plant whose revenue is a spread.

## 77.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: identify the rung of every revenue stream on the margin protection ladder; obtain a joint price deck for inputs and outputs through a full cycle; obtain licensor guarantees and compare caps with lenders' exposure; map unit interdependencies before agreeing envelopes; confirm sponsor guarantee scope (debt-wide or cost-overrun only) and release tests; check single-route logistics for feedstock and products; for factories, obtain customer qualification status and contractual remedies for late or off-spec delivery; separate funded from committed capital.

Red flags: debt sized on an average spread; completion tied to mechanical completion only; release of guarantees on a short reliability run; expansion financing raised before phase one stabilizes; "orders" presented as contracted revenue; a marketer that is also a shareholder with discretion over pricing; DSCR thresholds that pass only by rounding.

Rules of thumb with limits: size spread businesses on a trough-of-cycle margin (limit: a long-term processing fee replaces the cycle); two ramp curves multiply (limit: independent if yield and throughput are driven by different constraints); feedstock linked to product price halves margin volatility only if the link is in the formula, not in a renegotiation clause.

Common mistakes: independent sensitivities on correlated prices; treating licensor LDs as a performance backstop; ignoring working capital build during ramp-up; modeling a factory's price flat in real terms when product prices deflate.

Questions experts ask: Which rung is each dollar of revenue on? Who will fund the plant if margins stay at trough for three years after guarantee release? What is the plant's position on its industry cost curve? Which units, if they fail, stop the others? How much of the funding is committed but conditional?

## 77.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are the lead arranger for a 30 GWh lithium-ion cell plant in the US Midwest (Illustrative), April 2026; senior debt requested USD 2,480.0 million; an automaker customer with a 7-year supply agreement for 70% of nameplate; the sponsor asks that its completion guarantee fall away at mechanical completion plus 90 days at 70% of nameplate throughput and 85% yield, arguing the supply agreement makes the plant "contracted"; credit committee in nine days.

Reasoning outline: the supply agreement is not take-or-pay unless volumes, prices and remedies for the customer's non-purchase are fixed and the customer's right to reject is limited to defined specification failures; Example 77.4 shows breakeven yield at 70% throughput is above 85% in a similar cost structure, so the proposed test can pass while the plant loses cash; the expert asks for a combined throughput-yield-cash test (for example two consecutive quarters at 85% throughput and 92% yield plus a historical DSCR test), a capacity reservation fee or prepayment from the customer, and a sweep until stabilization; trade-off offered: a stepped release of the guarantee in two tranches.

What would change the answer: an investment-grade customer accepting a take-or-pay with capacity fees covering debt service (moves toward release at mechanical completion plus a short run); a proven repeat design by the same sponsor at another site (shortens the test); public lender cover of the ramp-up risk (changes who bears the risk, not whether it exists); a falling cell price curve faster than the cost curve (tightens the test and lowers debt).

## 78.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A mine is a price taker sitting on a depleting asset in a country that can change the rules after the money is spent. This chapter teaches the reader the economics of mining (cost curves, grade, recovery, by-products, cycles), the special features of critical minerals (thin markets, processing bottlenecks, state-directed finance), and the lender's toolkit that answers them: a bank price deck, a reserve tail, a multi-part completion test, sponsor completion support, multi-source structures with DFI and ECA cover, and streams as construction capital. Oyu Tolgoi and Cobre Panamá show what happens when geology and the host government move after financial close.

Ore milled 30.5 Mt a year at 0.58% Cu; recovery 88.4%: copper in concentrate 156,379.6 t; payable 96.5%: 150,906.3 t payable copper = 332.69 million lb. Site costs: mining USD 3.95/t ore, processing USD 8.40/t ore, G&A USD 61.8 million: site total USD 438.5 million. Concentrate at 26% Cu: 601,460 dmt; TC USD 80/dmt: USD 48.1 million; RC USD 0.08/lb payable: USD 26.6 million; freight 1.08 wmt per dmt at USD 61/wmt: USD 39.6 million; TC/RC and freight USD 114.4 million. Gold by-product: 96,300 oz at 92% payable and USD 2,350/oz: credit USD 208.2 million. C1 = (438.5 + 114.4 - 208.2)/332.69 = USD 1.04/lb. Sustaining capex USD 212.4 million; royalty 5% of net revenue at copper USD 4.15/lb (5% x (332.69 x 4.15 + 208.2) = USD 79.4 million). AISC = (438.5 + 114.4 - 208.2 + 212.4 + 79.4)/332.69 = USD 1.91/lb; margin at USD 4.15/lb = USD 2.24/lb. Writer places the mine on Exhibit 78.1 (second quartile in the illustrative curve).

Example 78.2. Reserve tail and maximum tenor (Illustrative). Proven and probable reserves 412.6 Mt; throughput 30.5 Mtpa: reserve life 13.53 years. Lenders require at least 30% of reserves to remain at final maturity: maximum tenor from start of operations = 0.70 x 13.53 = 9.47 years. At a 9-year tenor, 33.5% of reserves remain. Equation 78.1: maximum tenor = (1 - tail) x reserves / annual throughput. Extension: grade decline (front-loaded high-grade) means a tonnage tail overstates the cash-flow tail; the writer shows the fix (tail measured on NPV of remaining cash flows on the bank deck), as a principle with no numbers beyond this example.

Example 78.3. Sizing on a bank price deck and testing a downside (Illustrative). The Example 78.1 mine, using its volumes and costs; CFADS after a 25% tax on EBITDA above USD 310 million of tax depreciation. Consensus deck (copper USD 4.30/lb, gold USD 2,350/oz): CFADS USD 671.2 million. Bank deck (USD 3.85/lb, USD 1,950/oz): CFADS USD 539.3 million. Downside deck (USD 3.20/lb, USD 1,650/oz): CFADS USD 366.3 million. Size at 1.50x on the bank deck: debt service USD 359.5 million a year; 8 years at 8.12%: annuity factor 5.7206; debt USD 2,056.7 million (42.3% of development capital of USD 4,862.0 million). DSCR on consensus deck 1.87x; on downside deck 1.02x. Teaching point: the sizing DSCR on a bank deck is chosen so that a plausible downside still meets debt service; level debt service is used for simplicity (sculpting is Chapter 36).

Example 78.4. The cost of a gold stream as construction capital (Illustrative). Stream deposit USD 318.6 million paid 30%, 40%, 30% over three construction years; streamer receives 25% of payable gold (96,300 oz x 92% x 25% = 22,149 oz a year) for 13 years and pays an ongoing 20% of spot per ounce. Net value to the streamer each year at USD 2,350/oz: USD 41.64 million; at USD 1,950/oz: USD 34.55 million; at USD 1,650/oz: USD 29.24 million. Streamer's IRR (timing: deposit years 0 to 2, deliveries years 3 to 15): 7.29%, 4.56%, 2.27%. Compare with senior debt at 8.12% in Example 78.3: the stream is cheaper than debt in the base case and the streamer, not the lenders, takes gold price risk; the writer explains what the miner gives up (gold upside) and how the stream ranks against senior lenders (intercreditor terms, cross-reference Chapter 53).

Example 78.5. Testing a completion result (Illustrative). Clause 78.2 tests: mechanical completion certified; physical completion: mill throughput at least 90% of nameplate (83,560 t/d) averaged over 90 consecutive days; metallurgical: copper recovery at least 86.0%; operating: C1 at most USD 1.25/lb over the test period; financial: projected DSCR on the bank deck at least 1.40x for each remaining period, with reserve tail still met. Results: throughput 77,410 t/d (92.6%: pass); recovery 86.9% (pass); C1 USD 1.18/lb (pass); projected minimum DSCR 1.37x (fail). Outcome: completion not achieved; options: prepayment to restore 1.40x (the writer computes the prepayment only as a method, without new numbers, or the exercise set does it), sponsor cure, retest after a deck update; the writer explains why lenders insist on the financial test in mining (the plant can work and the mine still not support the debt).

## 78.7 Walkthrough

Walkthrough: marking up a mining completion test (Section 78.11).
1. Read the sponsor's draft schedule: list each test and its measurement period.
2. Check that physical tests use sustained periods (90 days rather than 30) and average rather than peak rates.
3. Tie the metallurgical test to the recovery used in the banking case.
4. Add an operating cost test and define which costs count (C1 versus AISC).
5. Add the financial test on the bank deck in force at the test date, with the reserve tail re-tested on the updated mine plan.
6. Specify the IE's certificate, the long-stop date and the consequences of failure (guarantee call, cash trap, mandatory prepayment).
7. Apply the marked-up schedule to the Example 78.5 results and write the facility agent's notice.

## 78.8 Frameworks

Framework 78.1, the four-part completion test (label fw:four-part-completion). Established practice in mining finance, organized here as an original checklist. Parts: (1) mechanical completion: built to specification, certified; (2) physical completion: sustained throughput at a stated share of nameplate over a stated period; (3) metallurgical and operating completion: recovery, product quality and unit cost at or better than the banking case; (4) financial completion: projected cover ratios on the current bank deck and reserve tail still met, reserves funded, no default. Use: drafting and testing the release of sponsor completion support; each part is matched to the risk it retires (construction, ramp-up, process, economics). Demonstrated in Example 78.5 and the walkthrough.

Framework 78.2, the deck-tail-test triangle (original; label fw:deck-tail-test). Purpose: check that the three main mining debt parameters are consistent. Steps: (1) size on the bank deck; (2) set tenor inside the reserve tail; (3) make the financial completion test use the same deck and tail; (4) stress all three together on the downside deck. When the three are set by different teams on different decks, the structure leaks. Demonstrated across Examples 78.2, 78.3 and 78.5.

## 78.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: reserve statement and competent person's report reconciled to the mine plan; bank deck set, with by-products and FX in the same scenario; reserve tail tested in tonnes and in NPV; four-part completion test; host agreement terms on fiscal stability, carried interest funding and dispute resolution; closure plan, cost estimate and bonding; tailings design standard; power, water and logistics contracts; stream and offtake intercreditor positions.

Red flags: sizing on consensus or spot; physical completion over 30 days at peak rates; a single concentrate offtaker with set-off rights; a state partner whose equity is funded by the sponsor with no repayment path; a mining right granted without the process the law required; closure costs unfunded until late in life.

Rules of thumb with limits: the lender's deck sits below consensus by a margin that widens with volatility (limit: price floors from creditworthy parties); the tail is at least a quarter to a third of reserves (indicative, D-011; limit: long-life deposits with stable grade); completion takes longer than the schedule for underground mines.

Common mistakes: treating by-product credits as independent of the main metal; modeling local costs in USD without FX; ignoring care-and-maintenance cost in a downside; treating political risk insurance as cover for creeping fiscal change.

Questions experts ask: Where is the mine on the cost curve at the bank deck? What happens to cover in year 3 if grade comes in 10% below plan? Who funds the state's share? How was the mining right granted, and has its approval been challenged? What does closure cost now, and who holds the bond?

## 78.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are the DFI investment officer on a lithium mine and hydroxide refinery in a South American country (Illustrative), June 2026. Senior debt requested USD 1,140.0 million on a USD 1,986.4 million cost. A Korean battery maker offers a 10-year offtake for 60% of hydroxide output with a floor of USD 11,500/t and a ceiling of USD 24,000/t and a USD 150.0 million prepayment. The sponsor asks you to size on the floor at 1.30x and to treat the 40% balance as merchant at 2.00x. Board paper due in two weeks.

Reasoning outline: the floor is only as good as the battery maker's credit and its right to reject off-spec product, which bites during qualification; refinery ramp-up and yield are manufacturing risks (Chapter 77) not covered by the floor; the prepayment ranks somewhere against the senior debt and must be subordinated or capped; the expert sizes contracted volume at the floor only after qualification, uses a stricter merchant deck for the rest, adds a four-part completion test with a yield and specification test, and requires sponsor completion support through qualification.

What would change the answer: an investment-grade offtaker with a take-or-pay and a narrow rejection right (raises debt); government price-floor support (raises debt, adds policy risk); a sponsor with an operating refinery of the same flowsheet (shortens completion); a host fiscal regime under review (lowers debt or requires PRI).

## 79.6 Worked examples

Example 79.1. Willingness to pay and diversion (Illustrative). An 18.7 km tolled bypass saves 14 minutes against the free route for a car; value of time USD 19.40/hour (2025 prices): willingness to pay = 19.40 x 14/60 = USD 4.53. Binary logit share of the tolled route P = 1/(1 + exp(theta x (toll - WTP))) with theta = 0.92 per USD (a calibration parameter, stated as illustrative). Corridor demand 48,200 car trips a day. At toll USD 2.40: share 87.6%, 42,231 trips, toll revenue USD 101,354 a day. At USD 3.20: 77.2%, 37,218 trips, USD 119,098 a day. At USD 4.10: 59.7%, 28,770 trips, USD 117,958 a day. Revenue-maximizing toll in this model: about USD 3.61 (revenue about USD 121,656 a day). Teaching point: the bidder's revenue is most sensitive to the value of time it assumes, which is exactly what Case T's traffic advisor overstated (Case Bible 2.6; 22% above the later survey); the writer makes that link in one sentence without computing anything for Case T.

Example 79.2. A toll cut at two elasticities (Illustrative, set in the Dulles Greenway's numbers but not presented as its data). Toll falls from USD 1.75 to USD 1.00 (a 42.9% cut). Constant-elasticity demand Q1 = Q0 x (P1/P0)^e. At e = -0.30: traffic rises 18.3%, revenue falls to 67.6% of before. At e = -0.60: traffic rises 39.9%, revenue falls to 79.9% of before. Teaching point: with inelastic demand a toll cut buys traffic and loses revenue; the writer labels this as illustrative arithmetic and does not claim what happened to Greenway revenue. Sasha's habit ("minus point six, not minus point three") may be referenced in the Case T installment, not here.

Example 79.3. Minimum revenue guarantee with a revenue-sharing band (Illustrative). Base-case revenue USD 164.8 million a year. State guarantees 80% of base (pays the shortfall below USD 131.84 million) and takes 50% of revenue above 120% of base (USD 197.76 million). Outcomes: revenue at 62% of base (USD 102.18 million): state pays USD 29.66 million; net USD 131.84 million. At 94% (USD 154.91 million): no payments. At 131% (USD 215.89 million): state receives USD 9.06 million; net USD 206.82 million. Debt sizing with operating costs of USD 38.6 million: on the floor, CFADS USD 93.24 million, debt service at 1.30x = USD 71.72 million; on the base case without guarantee at 1.60x, CFADS USD 126.20 million, debt service USD 78.88 million. Teaching point: the guarantee lets lenders size on the floor at a lower ratio and gets close to base-case debt with far less traffic risk; the state carries a contingent liability that Chapter 57 teaches it to count.

Example 79.4. Least present value of revenue (Illustrative). Winning bid PVR USD 1,284.6 million at a 6.5% contractual discount rate; maximum term 45 years. Base: first-year revenue USD 71.4 million growing 3.5%: concession ends after 27.2 years. High: USD 88.9 million growing 4.0%: 18.9 years. Low: USD 58.6 million growing 2.8%: would need 47.1 years; at the 45-year cap the concessionaire has collected PV USD 1,261.2 million (98.2% of the bid), a shortfall of USD 23.4 million in present value. Teaching point: LPVR converts traffic risk into term risk, which suits debt only if lenders can lend to a variable maturity (cross-reference Chapter 36 on tenor and tail); the cap brings some traffic risk back.

Example 79.5. Revenue build and the cost of losing trucks (Illustrative). A 31.6 km road; 38,450 trips a day; average trip 17.4 km; car toll USD 0.168/km; mix 82.0% cars (1.0), 7.5% light commercial (1.5), 10.5% heavy (3.0); weighted multiplier 1.2475; leakage 2.7%; 365 days. Gross toll revenue USD 51.18 million; net revenue USD 49.80 million. If heavy vehicles fall to 6.0% of the original trips (4.5 points lost; trips fall to 36,720 a day; weighted multiplier 1.1649), net revenue falls to USD 44.41 million, a 10.8% loss for a 4.5% loss of trips. Teaching point: trucks are few and carry a large share of revenue; set the heavy-vehicle multiplier against the free route's cost for trucks (the Case T problem, Case Bible 2.6).

Example 79.6. Catch-up arithmetic (Illustrative). Actual traffic in year 1 is 65% of forecast; forecast grows 3.4% a year. If actual grows 6.0% a year, it meets the forecast path after ln(1/0.65)/ln(1.06/1.034) = 17.3 years; at 4.5% growth, 40.7 years. Equation 79.2. Teaching point: a large first-year gap does not close through faster growth within a debt tenor unless the cause is timing (development arriving later); otherwise it is a level shift and the debt must be resized.

## 79.8 Frameworks

Framework 79.1, the demand-risk sharing menu (original; label fw:demand-risk-menu). Purpose: choose how much demand risk the concessionaire should carry. Steps: (1) score traffic predictability (history, free alternative, captive demand); (2) score the government's fiscal capacity and accounting constraints (cross-reference Chapter 57); (3) pick from the menu: full transfer, revenue band or collar, minimum revenue guarantee, flexible-term (LPVR), availability payment with real tolls retained by the state; (4) check bankability with the lenders' sizing logic (Example 79.3); (5) check incentives (who still cares about traffic). When to use: at the procurement design stage and when a bidder proposes changes.

Framework 79.2, the ramp-up diagnosis (original; label fw:ramp-up-diagnosis). Purpose: classify a traffic shortfall. Steps: (1) separate volume from yield; (2) separate by class, by time of day and by origin-destination; (3) test each component against the forecast's assumptions: development timing, value of time, toll by class against the free route, network changes, macro shocks; (4) classify each as timing (closes as the cause arrives), structural (permanent level shift) or policy-fixable (closes only if a tariff or network change is made); (5) translate into debt consequences: wait (timing), resize or restructure (structural), negotiate (policy-fixable). Demonstrated in the walkthrough and in the Case T installment.

## 79.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: free-alternative cost by class and trip length; value of time source and date (stated preference against revealed preference); development assumptions with independent evidence; ramp-up profile against the reference class; toll escalation formula and political tolerance; competing-facility clause; relief and compensation events for demand shocks; back-office leakage and enforcement; lifecycle for tunnel systems; refinancing structure.

Red flags: winning forecast above every other forecast; upfront concession payment tied to the bidder's traffic case; trucks priced above the free route's cost; ramp-up shorter than 18 months in a new corridor; bullet debt or accreting swaps on a demand-risk asset; promised public works not contracted.

Rules of thumb with limits: ramp-up of 18 months to 3 years is normal for a new urban road (NSW Auditor-General; limit: a corridor waiting for development); a first-year gap above about a quarter rarely closes within a debt tenor (Example 79.6 logic; limit: identified timing causes); truck share drives revenue disproportionately.

Common mistakes: holding traffic constant when tolls change; treating ramp-up as a percentage applied to a correct mature level; using peak-hour design models for daily revenue (the RTA's admission); adding growth on top of an over-forecast base.

Questions experts ask: What does a truck pay on the free road? Who decides the toll escalation in practice? Which forecast is the outlier? What happens to the debt if year three looks like year one? What did the government promise to build, and is it in the contract?

## 79.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are credit officer for the senior lenders to a greenfield toll bridge (Illustrative) 14 months after opening; traffic is 71% of the banking case; the sponsor proposes a 30% toll discount for 12 months to "build habit", funded from the DSRA, and asks for the lock-up to be waived during the discount period. Lenders' meeting in five days.

Reasoning outline: test the proposal with elasticity (Example 79.2): at the plausible elasticity, revenue falls during the discount; habit formation is a weak empirical claim; the DSRA is the lenders' liquidity, not marketing money; diagnose the shortfall first (Framework 79.2): if the cause is a missing free-route restriction or development timing, a discount does not help. The expert allows a targeted discount (for example trucks, if the truck toll exceeds the free-route cost) funded by sponsor equity, keeps the lock-up, commissions a ramp-up diagnosis, and agrees in advance the trigger for a re-forecast and resizing.

What would change the answer: evidence from transaction data that price is the main barrier for a class (supports a targeted discount); a contracting authority willing to fund the discount or deliver the promised network change (changes who pays); a sponsor equity injection to fund the trial (removes the DSRA objection).

## 80.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Rail, airports and ports all move people or goods, but their revenue comes through other organizations: transit authorities that set fares, airlines that choose hubs, shipping lines that choose ports. This chapter teaches the economics of each subsector, the revenue and regulatory models that make them financeable (availability-paid transit, regulated airport tills and concessions, landlord-port terminal concessions with minimum annual guarantees), the risks that are particular to them (interfaces, supply-chain governance, footloose demand, pandemics), and the record of Metronet, the Purple Line and the pandemic rail contracts.

## 80.6 Worked examples

Example 80.1. Single till against dual till (Illustrative). Airport with regulated asset base USD 2,384.7 million, real pre-tax WACC 5.85%, regulatory depreciation USD 118.4 million, operating costs USD 241.6 million, commercial revenue USD 286.3 million, 31.2 million passengers. Single till: allowed revenue = 2,384.7 x 5.85% + 118.4 + 241.6 = USD 499.5 million (return USD 139.5 million); aeronautical charge = (499.5 - 286.3)/31.2 = USD 6.83 per passenger. Dual till: aeronautical RAB 82% of total; aeronautical depreciation USD 97.1 million; aeronautical opex USD 179.2 million; aeronautical revenue requirement = 0.82 x 139.5 + 97.1 + 179.2 = USD 390.7 million; charge USD 12.52 per passenger. The airport keeps commercial profit of USD 177.5 million under dual till (286.3 less commercial share of return 25.1, commercial depreciation 21.3 and commercial opex 62.4). Teaching point: the till decides whether shops subsidize airlines; airlines lobby for single till, investors for dual till; lenders care about the stability of the regime more than its choice.

Example 80.2. A container terminal's minimum annual guarantee (Illustrative). Concession fee: fixed USD 18.6 million a year plus USD 14.20 per TEU, with a MAG of 850,000 TEU billed if throughput falls short. Terminal tariff USD 182.00 per TEU; variable opex USD 74.50 per TEU; fixed opex USD 46.8 million. At 1,210,000 TEU: fees USD 35.78 million; revenue USD 220.22 million; EBITDA USD 47.49 million (21.6% margin). At 905,000 TEU: fees USD 31.45 million; revenue USD 164.71 million; EBITDA USD 19.04 million. At 620,000 TEU (an alliance moves its transshipment calls): MAG binds, fees USD 30.67 million against USD 27.40 million without the MAG; revenue USD 112.84 million; EBITDA (10.82) million. Teaching point: the MAG transfers volume risk to the operator and makes the downside steeper; lenders ask who brings the volume (anchor-line contracts) before they look at the MAG.

Example 80.3. Farebox recovery of a light-rail line (Illustrative). 61,400 boardings a day, 350 effective days, average fare USD 1.85: fare revenue USD 39.76 million. Operating cost USD 64.3 million: farebox recovery 61.8%. Availability payment to the concessionaire USD 112.7 million a year (capital, maintenance and operations). Net cost to the authority after fares: USD 72.94 million, or USD 3.39 per boarding. Teaching point: a transit line's credit is the authority's budget, not the farebox; lenders analyze appropriation risk and the payment mechanism, not ridership.

Example 80.4. Leasing a fleet with a residual value (Illustrative). Fleet cost USD 412.6 million; 25-year lease; lessor's rate 6.1%; expected residual value 15% of cost at year 25 (useful life 35 years). PV of residual = 61.89/(1.061^25) = USD 14.08 million. Annual rental (in arrears) = (412.6 - 14.08)/annuity factor = USD 31.47 million; without a residual assumption USD 32.58 million. Teaching point: the lessor's residual assumption lowers the rent by USD 1.11 million a year and puts technology and re-lease risk on the lessor; a lender to the lessor tests whether trains of that type can be re-leased.

Example 80.5. An airport through a passenger shock (Illustrative). 18.4 million passengers; aeronautical revenue USD 14.60 and commercial USD 11.85 per passenger; variable cost USD 4.10 per passenger; fixed costs USD 168.3 million; debt service USD 112.6 million. Base: revenue USD 486.7 million; CFADS USD 242.9 million; DSCR 2.16x. At 38% of base traffic: CFADS (12.0) million; debt service unpaid without liquidity. At 71% of base: CFADS USD 123.7 million; DSCR 1.10x. Breakeven traffic for 1.00x: 12.57 million passengers, 68.3% of base. Teaching point: airports with high fixed costs need liquidity (DSRA, revolver, covenant holidays) more than high base cover; tie to the pandemic responses in 80.5.1.

## 80.8 Frameworks

Framework 80.1, the footloose-demand test (original; label fw:footloose-demand). Purpose: judge how easily a transport asset's users can take their demand elsewhere. Steps: (1) identify the decision-maker for demand (passenger, airline, shipping line, authority); (2) identify the alternatives and the switching cost and time; (3) identify contractual locks (anchor-line agreements, airline use agreements, concession exclusivity); (4) score as captive, contestable or footloose; (5) link the score to structure (availability or regulation for captive; contracted anchor volumes and higher cover for contestable; short tenor, high cover or no project debt for footloose). Demonstrated in Exhibit 80.2 and the walkthrough.

## 80.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist: who sets fares and charges; demand decision-maker and alternatives (Framework 80.1); utilities and third-party interface register; systems integration responsibility; operator interface agreement; anchor-user contracts and their tenor against the debt; regulatory regime and review cycle; liquidity for demand shocks; supply-chain governance where shareholders are contractors.

Red flags: shareholders who are also the main contractors with no independent cost control; an extended-delay termination right without owner-delay carve-outs; a transshipment terminal financed on throughput without line commitments; airport debt sized on peak-year passengers; periodic reviews without clear "efficient cost" tests.

Rules of thumb with limits: transit is a budget credit (limit: where fare revenue is pledged); captive gateway ports are more bankable than transshipment hubs (limit: alliance contracts with take-or-pay); high fixed-cost airports need liquidity sized for a severe shock.

Common mistakes: treating ridership as the transit lender's risk under availability payment; modeling MAG as revenue rather than cost; ignoring commercial revenue's dependence on passenger mix; equating a public guarantee with risk transfer.

Questions experts ask: Who chooses whether this demand comes here? How long does it take them to leave? What does the state do when a shock hits, and what did it do last time? Which interface is not in anyone's contract? Who owns the supply chain?

## 80.12 Judgment drill

The situation: you are a lender asked to refinance a container terminal (Illustrative) with 12 years of throughput history; 46% of volume comes from one alliance whose terminal services agreement expires in three years; the sponsor wants debt sized on the 12-year average with a 15-year tenor. Credit committee in two weeks.

Reasoning outline: score the terminal on Framework 80.1 (transshipment share and alliance concentration make it contestable or footloose); the history proves the alliance chose the terminal, not that it will stay; the expert sizes bankable volume on gateway cargo plus contracted alliance volume, adds a cash sweep and a tenor step-down if the alliance agreement is not renewed by a set date, or requires renewal before close; considers MAG exposure in the downside (Example 80.2).

What would change the answer: an alliance shareholding in the terminal or a renewal with take-or-pay to beyond the debt tenor (raises bankable volume); no competing terminal within the region (moves toward captive); port authority willingness to adjust the MAG in a downturn (reduces downside).

|---|---|---|---|
| farebox recovery ratio | – | Fare revenue as a share of a transit service's operating cost. | 80.1.1 |
| vertical separation | – | The division of a railway into separate infrastructure and train-operating businesses. | 80.1.2 |
| gross-cost contract | – | A transit operating contract in which the authority keeps fare revenue and pays the operator its costs plus a fee. | 80.1.2 |
| rolling stock lease | – | A lease of trains from a lessor that owns them, with rentals set over a term shorter than the trains' life. | 80.1.4 |
| aeronautical revenue | – | An airport's revenue from charges on airlines and passengers for using runways, terminals and related services. | 80.2.1 |
| commercial revenue (airport) | – | An airport's revenue from retail, food, parking, property and other non-aeronautical sources. | 80.2.1 |
| single till | – | Airport regulation that counts commercial revenue toward the allowed revenue, lowering aeronautical charges. | 80.2.2 |
| dual till | – | Airport regulation that sets aeronautical charges on aeronautical assets and costs only, leaving commercial profit to the airport. | 80.2.2 |
| landlord port | – | A port model in which a public authority owns land and basic infrastructure and leases terminals to private operators. | 80.3.1 |
| twenty-foot equivalent unit | TEU | The standard measure of container volume, equal to one 20-foot container. | 80.3.2 |
| transshipment | – | Moving containers between ships at an intermediate port without their entering the port's hinterland. | 80.3.2 |
| minimum annual guarantee | MAG | A concessionaire's undertaking to pay concession fees on at least a stated throughput or amount each year. | 80.3.3 |
| footloose-demand test | – | Framework 80.1: a scoring of how easily a transport asset's demand decision-makers can move elsewhere. | 80.4.1 |
| anchor-line agreement | – | A terminal services agreement under which a shipping line commits volumes to a terminal, often in return for tariffs or berthing priority. | 80.6.3 |
