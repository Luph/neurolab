# Chapter 23. Construction structures beyond the single EPC

## 23.1 Header

- Chapter: 23
- Title: Construction structures beyond the single EPC contract
- File: `chapters/23-construction-structures-beyond-epc.tex`
- Target length: 10,000 to 11,500 words
- Dominant expert lens: construction and project finance lawyer, with the independent engineer as second voice (the lender's view of interface risk and completion)

## 23.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapter 22 taught the lump-sum turnkey, date-certain EPC contract as the lenders' preferred answer to construction risk: one contractor, one price, one date, one set of guarantees. This chapter teaches what happens when that answer is unavailable or too expensive, which is the norm in offshore wind, LNG, mining, large transport PPPs and much of the energy transition. The reader learns how split and multi-contract structures, EPCM, wraps, interface agreements and joint-venture contractors redistribute construction risk; how the FIDIC and NEC standard forms allocate it and why lenders amend them; and how supply-chain concentration and the credit of equipment suppliers create exposures that last long after handover. Each structure is priced: the reader computes a wrap premium against expected interface losses, the cascade cost of one late handover, the owner's retained overrun under EPCM, a commodity price adjustment and a supplier's uncovered credit exposure.

What you will be able to do:

- Choose among single EPC, split, multi-contract and EPCM delivery for a given project and price the construction risk the owner keeps under each (Capabilities 3, 15).
- Map the interfaces in a multi-contract package and draft the interface and wrap provisions that close them (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Mark up a FIDIC- or NEC-based construction contract for a project financing (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Measure an equipment supplier's credit exposure over time and size the security that covers it (Capabilities 3, 7).

**23.1 What a single EPC contract buys** (`sec:23.1`)
- 23.1.1 One point of responsibility and its price (`ssec:23.1.1`). The single EPC contractor absorbs interface, coordination and integration risk and charges for it through contingency and margin: the wrap premium. Explains the premium's components (interface contingency, integration margin, cost of the contractor's own bonds and capital) and why it rises with project size and novelty.
- 23.1.2 Why sponsors break the wrap (`ssec:23.1.2`). Five drivers, ranked: no contractor will wrap at any price (offshore wind at gigawatt scale, LNG in some markets, block-cave mines); the premium exceeds the sponsor's estimate of the risk; the OEM controls the critical equipment and will not sit under a civil contractor; local content and tax push work onshore; schedule (early procurement of long-lead items before an EPC price is fixed).
- 23.1.3 What lenders ask for in exchange (`ssec:23.1.3`). When the wrap goes, lenders replace it with larger contingency, an owner's engineer and an independent engineer review of the interfaces, and sponsor completion support. One-sentence forward reference to Ch 26 for the support documents.

**23.2 Split and multi-contract structures** (`sec:23.2`)

**23.4 Wraps** (`sec:23.4`)
- 23.4.1 Full, partial and performance wraps (`ssec:23.4.1`). A full wrap puts one entity on the hook for the whole works; a partial wrap covers a subset (for example, the power island); a performance wrap guarantees output and heat rate across packages without taking delay risk. Wrap guarantee from a lead contractor, its parent, or a specialist wrapper.
- 23.4.2 Wrap caps and the sum-of-caps problem (`ssec:23.4.2`). The wrapper's cap is usually below the sum of the package caps it sits over, so the owner's recovery for a multi-package failure is the lower of the two. Clause 23.2 variants (wrap liability cap: sponsor-friendly, lender-friendly, contractor-friendly) with the landing paragraph (indicative, labeled with market and period, signed off by the domain reviewer under D-011).
- 23.4.3 Who can give a wrap (`ssec:23.4.3`). The wrapper needs technical competence over every package and a balance sheet large enough to make the cap credible. Why OEMs rarely wrap civil works and civil contractors rarely wrap turbines.

**23.5 EPCM and owner-managed delivery** (`sec:23.5`)

The EPCM contractor designs, procures and manages construction as the owner's agent for a fee; the owner signs every trade and supply contract and carries price and schedule risk. ## 23.6 Worked examples

**Example 23.1. Wrapped or multi-contract offshore wind (Illustrative).** Label `ex:23.1`. Scenario: a 924 MW offshore wind farm in northern European waters, 2025 prices, EUR. Packages (EUR million): turbine supply 1,148.6; turbine transport and installation 151.6; foundations supply 412.3; foundations installation 186.9; array and export cables, supply and install 296.8; offshore substation 251.9; onshore works 97.4. Sum of packages EUR 2,545.5 million. A single-contractor wrapped bid prices at a 12.5% premium: EUR 318.2 million, for a wrapped price of EUR 2,863.7 million. The sponsor's owner's engineer assesses interface losses under multi-contracting as a discrete distribution: 50% probability of none; 30% of EUR 48.7 million (vessel standby and resequencing); 15% of EUR 163.2 million (a foundation-installation season lost); 5% of EUR 391.5 million (cable failure discovered at energization plus season loss). Compute: expected interface loss EUR 58.7 million (58.66); P90 interface loss EUR 163.2 million (cumulative probability 80% at 48.7 and 95% at 163.2); expected saving from multi-contracting EUR 259.5 million (318.2 minus 58.7); saving at P90 EUR 155.0 million. The sponsor proposes an interface contingency of 4% of package cost (EUR 101.8 million); lenders size to the P90 interface loss, so they ask for EUR 61.4 million more contingency or sponsor support (163.2 minus 101.8). Key results: the wrap is expensive in expectation, the tail is what lenders finance against, and the decision turns on who funds the gap between the sponsor's contingency and the lenders' downside. State the simplification: a four-point distribution stands in for the independent engineer's full risk model (Ch 9 for distributions).

**Example 23.2. One late handover in a split hydro contract (Illustrative).** Label `ex:23.2`. Scenario: a 96 MW run-of-river hydro plant in the Himalayan foothills (country unnamed), USD. Civil contract price USD 87.3 million; civil delay LDs USD 96,000 per day capped at 10% of the civil price (USD 8.73 million). The civil contractor hands over the powerhouse 112 days late. The E&M supplier claims standby of USD 61,300 per day plus remobilization of USD 1.86 million. The owner's own costs of delay (extended owner's team, insurance extension, interest during the extended construction period, taken here as a blended daily figure) are USD 118,500 per day. The PPA charges delay LDs of USD 42,000 per day after a 30-day grace period. Compute: E&M standby USD 6.87 million (6.8656); remobilization USD 1.86 million; owner's costs USD 13.27 million (13.272); PPA LDs USD 3.44 million (82 days, 3.444); total loss USD 25.44 million (25.442). Civil LDs uncapped would be USD 10.75 million (10.752) but the cap bites after 90.9 days, so recovery is USD 8.73 million; net loss to the owner USD 16.71 million (16.712). Then rerun under an interface agreement that makes the civil contractor liable to the E&M supplier for standby within a separate sub-cap, and show which lines move. Simplification stated: revenue lost during the delay is excluded because the PPA term runs from COD.

**Example 23.3. EPCM against lump-sum turnkey for a copper concentrator (Illustrative).** Label `ex:23.3`. Scenario: a 40,000 t/d copper concentrator expansion in northern Chile, USD, 2026. Direct cost estimate USD 1,284.7 million; EPCM fee 9.6% of actual direct cost; owner's costs USD 87.4 million; owner's contingency 8.5% of direct cost estimate (USD 109.2 million). EPCM budget USD 1,604.6 million. Outturn direct cost growth: P50 +4%, P90 +26%. Compute: at P50, direct USD 1,336.1 million, fee USD 128.3 million, total USD 1,551.8 million (USD 52.9 million inside budget); at P90, direct USD 1,618.7 million, fee USD 155.4 million, total USD 1,861.5 million (USD 256.9 million over budget). An LSTK bid at 21.2% over the direct estimate is USD 1,557.1 million; with owner's costs and a 3% owner's contingency (USD 46.7 million) the LSTK route costs USD 1,691.2 million. Key results: EPCM is cheaper at P50 by USD 139.4 million and dearer at P90 by USD 170.3 million (1,861.5 minus 1,691.2); under EPCM, lenders will ask the sponsors for completion support of about USD 256.9 million (the P90 overrun), which is the price of the saving. Note the fee rises with the overrun because it is a percentage of actual cost, a point the writer turns into a red flag.

**Example 23.4. A steel price adjustment with a band and sharing (Illustrative).** Label `ex:23.4`. Scenario: a transmission tower supply-and-erect contract for a 400 kV line, price USD 143.8 million, of which 38% is subject to adjustment against a published steel index; base index 112.4, index at the adjustment date 139.7. Equation (23.1), `eq:23.1`: adjustment = price × indexed share × (I_t / I_0 − 1), with Excel implementation. Compute: index change 24.29%; full adjustment USD 13.27 million (13.272). With a ±5% band and 50:50 sharing beyond the band: USD 5.27 million paid to the contractor, USD 8.00 million kept by the contractor. Show who the lenders want to bear it (the contractor or a funded contingency) and why an uncapped escalation clause breaks a fixed-price construction budget.

**Example 23.5. A battery supplier's credit exposure over ten years (Illustrative).** Label `ex:23.5`; supported by Exhibit 23.5. Scenario: a 200 MW / 800 MWh battery project in Australia buys its battery system directly from an integrator rated BB (illustrative), supply price USD 214.6 million, with 10-year capacity and availability guarantees. The owner's engineer values the remaining warranty and guarantee obligations (the exposure) at USD 18.4, 17.1, 15.7, 14.2, 12.6, 10.9, 9.1, 7.2, 5.1 and 2.9 million in Years 1 to 10. Security: a warranty bond of 5% of price (USD 10.73 million) for Years 1 and 2, 2.5% (USD 5.37 million) for Years 3 to 5, nothing after. Assume an annual default probability of 2.1% (labeled as an assumption, not a market statistic). Compute uncovered exposure by year (7.67, 6.37, 10.33, 8.83, 7.23, 10.90, 9.10, 7.20, 5.10, 2.90), the marginal default probability by year (survival-weighted), and the expected uncovered loss: USD 1.46 million (1.459); cumulative 10-year default probability 19.1%. Key results: the peak uncovered exposure falls in Year 6 when the bond expires, not at COD; the fix is a bond profile that tracks exposure or an escrow and parent guarantee.

## 23.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: marking up a standard-form contract for a project financing (sec 23.8).** The artifact is a contractor's design-build or EPC/turnkey standard form proposed by a bidder with "particular conditions" left blank. Steps:

1. Read the general conditions for the risk allocation baseline: who designs, who bears unforeseen ground, what counts as an employer's risk event.
2. List every employer's risk event and compare it with the offtake contract's relief events (Ch 18) and the concession's relief and compensation events (Ch 17). Any event that gives the contractor time and money but gives the project company nothing under the offtake contract is a gap; mark it.
3. Replace the completion and testing provisions with tests matching the lenders' completion definition (forward reference Ch 26) and the offtake contract's commissioning tests.
4. Calibrate delay LDs and caps to the debt service and fixed costs they must cover (method from Ch 22), and check the aggregate cap against the sum of LD sub-caps.
5. Insert the security package: performance security, advance payment guarantee, retention, parent guarantee (Ch 22 terms).
6. Insert the finance provisions the standard form lacks: assignment by way of security, consent to a direct agreement (forward reference sec 28.2), step-in, novation to a substitute project company, lenders' and IE's access rights.
7. Check the disputes clause against the other contracts so a multi-party interface dispute can be heard in one forum.
8. Record every change in a particular-conditions table that the lenders' counsel and IE sign off.

## 23.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 23.1, Construction structure selector** (original; label `fw:construction-structure-selector`; home `ssec:23.1.3` or end of sec 23.5, writer's choice, placed once). Purpose: choose a delivery structure and state the support lenders will require. Steps: (1) Is a creditworthy contractor willing to wrap the whole works at a premium the project can afford? ## 23.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: package list with scope boundaries; interface matrix signed by every contractor; single integrated schedule with float ownership stated; aggregate LD and cap map across packages; wrap or performance wrap coverage; free-issue equipment warranties; supplier exposure profile against security; standard-form amendments table; disputes joinder.
- Red flags: interface contingency set as a round percentage with no interface register behind it; an EPCM fee calculated on actual cost with no cap or target; a JV whose partners are severally, not jointly, liable; a wrap cap below the largest single package cap; warranty bonds that expire before the guarantees they secure; a sole-source transformer with no reserved slot.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: wrap premia, EPCM fee ranges and lead times only as indicative ranges with market and period (D-011), each with its driver and the condition under which it fails.
- Common mistakes: treating a split contract as two contracts rather than one risk; assuming back-to-back means identical words; letting the owner become the integrator by default through free-issue equipment.
- Questions experts ask: Who is the integrator? Whose cap responds first? Which package's delay moves COD? What happens to the warranty if the supplier is acquired or fails?

## 23.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are the lead arranger on a 480 MW solar-plus-storage project in Morocco (Illustrative), four weeks from credit committee. The EPC contractor's price includes a battery system it buys from an integrator. The sponsor proposes to take the battery system out of the EPC wrap and buy it directly from a BB-rated integrator, saving USD 14.6 million. The EPC contractor will then give no performance guarantee on the combined plant's dispatch. Reasoning: what the saving buys versus the interface and supplier exposure created (use Examples 23.2 and 23.5 logic); what the IE must certify; which of the following the lenders demand: a partial performance wrap from the EPC contractor at a fee, a supplier bond profile tracking exposure, an interface agreement, extra contingency, sponsor support for battery delay. Expert answer: accept the split only with an interface agreement, a bond profile that follows the exposure, and contingency sized to the P90 interface loss, and require the sponsor to keep part of the saving in contingency. What would change the answer: an investment-grade integrator or a parent guarantee from one (moves toward accepting); an EPC contractor willing to give a performance wrap for less than the saving (keep the wrap); a PPA with tight battery availability LDs payable from COD (moves against the split).

## 24.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Once the EPC contractor hands over, the project company spends 20 to 30 years buying three things: people who run the plant, an equipment manufacturer who keeps the critical machines alive, and someone who manages the whole on the owners' behalf. This chapter teaches the O&M agreement, the long-term service agreement (LTSA) and the asset management agreement as risk-transfer instruments: what each covers, how its fee is built (fixed, cost-plus, per equivalent operating hour), how its performance regime and liability cap compare with the revenue the project loses when the plant underperforms, and how major maintenance is planned and paid for. The reader computes the expected cost of fixed and cost-plus fee structures, the share of an availability shortfall an operator bears, an LTSA's cost per MWh under peaking and baseload dispatch, and the cash effect of lumpy major maintenance; then applies an original test of whether operating incentives line up with the offtake contract.

What you will be able to do:

- Read an O&M agreement, an LTSA and an asset management agreement and state which operating risks each transfers and which stay with the project company (Capability 3).
- Compute and compare fee structures and performance incentives against the revenue at risk under the offtake contract (Capabilities 3, 4).
- Plan major maintenance and its funding over the life of the debt (Capabilities 4, 10).

## 24.6 Worked examples

**Example 24.1. Fixed fee or cost-plus for a geothermal plant (Illustrative).** Label `ex:24.1`. Scenario: a 126 MW geothermal plant in Kenya's Rift Valley (Illustrative), USD, first operating year. Fixed-fee bid USD 9.37 million a year. Cost-plus alternative: reimbursed actual cost plus a 12% fee. Actual cost outcomes: USD 7.21 million (25%), USD 7.95 million (50%), USD 9.46 million (25%). Compute: owner's cost under cost-plus USD 8.075, 8.904 and 10.595 million; expected cost-plus cost USD 9.12 million against USD 9.37 million fixed, so the owner pays USD 0.25 million a year in expectation for certainty; the operator's margin under the fixed fee is USD 2.16, 1.42 and (0.09) million in the three outcomes. Key result: the fixed fee buys a cap on a cost that lenders size against in the downside; the right comparison is the downside (USD 10.595 million against 9.37), not the mean.

**Example 24.2. How much of an availability shortfall the operator bears (Illustrative).** Label `ex:24.2`. Scenario: a 220 MW open-cycle peaking plant in Bangladesh (Illustrative) whose PPA pays a capacity charge of USD 9.85/kW-month on contracted capacity times the lesser of 1 and availability divided by a 92% target (tariff formula from Ch 18, cited). The O&M operator's annual fee is USD 6.3 million; its availability malus is USD 45,000 per point below 92%, capped at 20% of the fee. Compute: capacity payment at target USD 26.0 million a year (26.004); revenue lost per availability point below target USD 282,652; the operator bears 15.9% of each point; the cap (USD 1.26 million) is reached only at 28 points below target. At 86% availability the project loses USD 1.70 million (1.696) of capacity payments and the operator pays USD 0.27 million. Key result: an O&M performance regime aligns incentives at the margin but never transfers the revenue risk; the residual sits with equity and, below that, with lenders.

**Example 24.3. LTSA cost per MWh for a peaker and a baseload unit (Illustrative).** Label `ex:24.3`; uses Equation (24.1). ## 24.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 24.1, Operating incentive alignment test** (original; label `fw:operating-incentive-alignment`; home sec 24.6). Purpose: test whether the operating contracts make each operator want what the offtake contract pays for. Steps: (1) List what the offtake contract pays for and penalizes (availability, heat rate, response, starts). (2) For each, find the operator or LTSA provider incentive and its measurement period. ## 24.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: scope matrix across O&M, LTSA, asset manager and owner; operating budget approval process; performance measurement definitions identical to the offtake contract's; operator liability cap against revenue at risk; LTSA term against debt and offtake term under each dispatch case; exclusions mapped to the major maintenance plan; replacement triggers and transition duties; direct agreements.
- Red flags: availability measured on a different period or definition from the PPA; an O&M bonus earned above the PPA target; an LTSA that ends on EOH before the debt matures; unapproved-parts clauses that void warranties; an affiliated operator whose claims the sponsor decides.
- Rules of thumb with limits: operator caps as a multiple of annual fee, LTSA fee ranges, typical inspection intervals, all only as dated indicative ranges (D-011) with the driver of each.
- Common mistakes: comparing fixed and cost-plus on the mean rather than the downside; ignoring starts; double-counting maintenance in the LTSA fee and the major maintenance line; letting the O&M agreement expire with the first renewal at the operator's option only.
- Questions experts ask: Who decides a claim against the operator? What happens on the day the LTSA ends? What does a start cost, and who pays for it?

## 24.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are head of asset management for a sponsor that owns a 252 MW onshore wind farm in Poland (Illustrative), Year 5 of operation, with 9 years of debt left. ## 25.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A power plant with a perfect PPA and a perfect EPC contract still earns nothing if gas does not arrive, the cooling water is too warm, the transmission line is late, the land lease can be terminated or a permit dies with a change of owner. This chapter teaches the contracts and rights that bring inputs in and give the project access: fuel and feedstock supply agreements (with the gas sale agreement as the model), transportation from the shipper's side, water, grid connection and interconnection, land rights and permits. Each is taught as a link in a chain that must line up with the offtake contract in term, quantity, quality, price, delivery point, relief events and termination. The reader computes a plant's gas requirement and sets DCQ and MDQ, runs a take-or-pay and make-up account over four years, prices a fuel-offtake mismatch, values a grid delay with and without deemed energy, and tests land tenure against the debt; then applies an original alignment grid to a whole input chain.

- Read a gas sale agreement and set its quantities, take-or-pay and make-up terms against the plant's dispatch and the PPA (Capabilities 3, 4).

- Diligence grid connection, land rights and permits for a lender, including transferability on enforcement (Capabilities 7, 10).

## 25.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: fuel requirement under every dispatch case; ACQ, DCQ, MDQ consistent with plant and pipeline; take-or-pay level against downside dispatch; make-up recovery rule and window; deliver-or-pay remedies matched to PPA deemed availability; price pass-through and its caps; gas quality spec matched to the turbine's fuel spec; connection agreement dates against the PPA's long-stop dates; land tenure against debt plus tail and PPA plus decommissioning; permit register with transfer rules.
- Red flags: take-or-pay above the downside dispatch with no pass-through; a supplier whose upstream field lacks certified reserves for the term; a fuel price below market enforceable only under the supplier's law; a connection built by a state entity with no deemed-energy cover; an unregistered lease; permits held by a sponsor affiliate rather than the project company.
- Rules of thumb with limits: take-or-pay levels, make-up windows and connection lead times only as dated indicative ranges (D-011).
- Common mistakes: matching DCQ to full load instead of expected dispatch; treating make-up as free money; reading deemed energy as unconditional; ignoring decommissioning in the land term.
- Questions experts ask: What happens to the gas price when the market moves 50% against the seller? Who builds the last kilometer of line and who pays if it is late? Can the lenders hold the permits after enforcement?

## 25.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are counsel to the project company for a 450 MW CCGT in a Southeast Asian market (Illustrative). Three weeks before signing, the state gas seller offers a USD 0.38/MMBtu price reduction if take-or-pay rises from 75% to 90% of ACQ and the make-up window shortens from three years to one. The PPA passes through only 50% of dispatch-caused take-or-pay. Reasoning: compute the annual saving at expected dispatch, the extra deficiency exposure at downside dispatch (method of Examples 25.2 and 25.3), the lenders' view (downside case), and the offtaker's reaction. Expert answer: decline unless the PPA pass-through rises to 100% or the take-or-pay level stays at or below the lenders' downside dispatch; offer a smaller price cut for a modest take-or-pay increase with a three-year make-up window. What would change the answer: a capacity-constrained system where dispatch is reliably high; an offtaker that agrees full pass-through; a gas market with ready resale (secondary market for surplus gas).

## 26.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every other contract in Part IV binds the project company to an outsider. The documents in this chapter bind the owners: to each other while the project is an idea (development and co-development agreements), to each other once it is a company (the shareholders' or joint venture agreement), and to the lenders until the project has proven itself (the equity contribution agreement and sponsor support, above all the completion guarantee). The reader learns how sponsors share development cost and dilute a partner who stops paying; how boards, reserved matters and deadlock work; how transfer rights (lock-in, ROFR, ROFO, tag-along, drag-along) decide who can leave and on what terms; how an equity contribution agreement makes an equity promise bankable; and how lenders define the completion that releases sponsors. The reader computes dilution, voting blocks, an equity commitment's LC profile and acceleration, and a capped, several completion guarantee's expected cost to sponsors and residual risk to lenders.

What you will be able to do:

- Draft and negotiate the key terms of a co-development agreement and a shareholders' agreement from a majority and a minority seat (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Compute dilution, voting blocks and transfer outcomes under a shareholders' agreement (Capability 3).
- Structure an equity contribution agreement and a completion guarantee that lenders will accept and sponsors can afford (Capabilities 5, 6).
- Decide how much sponsor support a project needs and when it should fall away (Capabilities 10, 15).

## 26.6 Worked examples

**Example 26.1. Diluting a partner who stops paying (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.1`; Equation (26.1), `eq:26.1`: defaulting partner's share = its credited contributions ÷ total credited contributions, where the non-defaulting partner's cover of the defaulted call is credited at a multiplier m (m = 1 for straight dilution). Scenario: two developers of a 300 MW wind project in Vietnam (Illustrative), A 60% and B 40%, have spent USD 8.62 million (A 5.172, B 3.448). A cash call of USD 2.75 million follows (A 1.65, B 1.10); B fails to fund and A funds the whole call. Compute: straight dilution leaves B with 30.33% (3.448 ÷ 11.37) and A with 69.67%; punitive dilution crediting A's cover at 150% leaves B with 28.93% (A credited 8.472, total 11.92). Key result: punitive dilution is a deterrent, not compensation; minority partners negotiate the multiplier and a cure period.

**Example 26.2. Who can block a reserved matter (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.2`. Scenario: three shareholders of a 380 MW hydro project company in Georgia (the country; Illustrative) hold 45%, 30% and 25%; reserved matters need 75%. Compute every coalition: 45 + 30 = 75 passes; 45 + 25 = 70 fails; 30 + 25 = 55 fails. Result: the 45% and 30% holders each have a veto; the 25% holder has none. Then show the effect of moving the threshold to 70% (45 + 25 passes; the 30% holder loses its veto) and to 80% (only unanimity passes). Key result: thresholds are negotiated against stakes; a minority bargains for the threshold, not for seats.

**Example 26.3. An equity commitment backed by letters of credit (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.3`. Scenario: a 240 MW solar project in the Philippines (Illustrative), total funding requirement USD 248.7 million, equity 27% (USD 67.15 million, 67.149), contributed pro rata with debt over 18 months on an S-curve (monthly weights 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 8, 7, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 2, summing to 94); each sponsor's undrawn commitment is backed by an LC at 1.15% a year. Compute: average LC balance USD 34.29 million (34.289); total LC fees over construction USD 0.59 million (0.5915); an event of default at the start of Month 8 accelerates the undrawn USD 42.86 million (42.861), payable under the LCs. Key results: the LC converts an equity promise into bank credit the lenders can draw; its cost is small against the gearing it supports; Ch 32 compares this with equity first.

**Example 26.4. A capped, several completion guarantee (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.4`. Scenario: a USD 612.0 million chemicals plant in the US Gulf Coast (Illustrative); sponsors A (70%) and B (30%) give several completion guarantees capped in aggregate at USD 85.0 million for cost overruns. Overrun outcomes: none (55%), USD 41.3 million (25%), USD 96.8 million (12%), USD 158.4 million (8%). Compute: expected cost to sponsors USD 27.33 million (27.325); expected overrun beyond the cap, left to lenders and the project, USD 7.29 million (7.288). If the overrun is USD 96.8 million and B is insolvent: A pays USD 59.5 million, B owes USD 25.5 million it cannot pay, and USD 37.3 million is unfunded (25.5 plus the 11.8 above the cap). Key results: several liability and a cap both cut the lenders' protection exactly in the cases that matter; lenders respond with rating triggers, LC backing for weaker sponsors, or a joint guarantee for part of the cap.

## 26.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: negotiating a reserved-matters schedule (sec 26.7).** Artifact: a draft SHA schedule of reserved matters circulated by a majority sponsor to a 25% local partner and a 15% DFI-backed fund (Illustrative names and stakes, not Case P). Steps:

1. Sort the draft list into four groups: constitutional (articles, share capital, new classes), financial (budgets, debt, guarantees, distributions policy), commercial (material contracts, related-party contracts, disposals), and personnel (CEO, CFO, auditors).
2. For each item, ask which party's money or reputation it puts at risk and set the threshold accordingly (Example 26.2 method).
3. Add the minority's protections: related-party contracts and claims decided without the conflicted shareholder; anti-dilution on new issues; information rights.
4. Add the DFI's policy requirements (E&S, integrity, sanctions), forward-referenced to Ch 50 and Ch 60.
5. Check against the finance documents: anything the lenders already control (debt, distributions while locked up) need not be a reserved matter, and anything requiring lender consent must not need a shareholder vote that a minority can block after enforcement.
6. Write the deadlock path: escalation, mediation, expert determination for technical matters, buy-sell (shotgun) only where both sides can fund it.
7. Produce the final schedule with thresholds and an annotation of why each item sits where it does.

## 26.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 26.1, Sponsor support spectrum** (original; label `fw:sponsor-support-spectrum`; home ssec 26.5.1). Purpose: choose the level and form of sponsor support for a project and the trigger that releases it. Rungs, from strongest to weakest: full recourse guarantee of debt until completion; capped completion guarantee (cost and delay); cost-overrun undertaking (capped equity commitment); contingent equity commitment with LC; equity contribution agreement only (base equity); operating or offtake support from affiliates; keepwell and comfort letters. Steps to use: (1) identify the risks not transferred by contract or insurance (Chapters 22 to 25, 27); (2) price them at P50 and P90 (Examples 23.3, 26.4); (3) choose the rung that covers the P90 residual at the lowest cost to sponsors; (4) set the release trigger (financial completion definition, sec 26.5.2); (5) test guarantor credit and add rating triggers. Demonstrated on Example 26.4 and the Case P installment (Case P sits at the contingent-equity rung: no completion guarantee, contingent equity plus a standby facility).

## 26.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: co-development budget, cash-call and dilution terms; ownership of development assets on withdrawal; SHA board, quorum, reserved matters, deadlock; transfer rights with lender minimum holdings; related-party conflict rules; equity contribution agreement amount, timing, conditions, acceleration, LC rating triggers; completion definition with long-stop; guarantee cap, joint or several, guarantor credit, release mechanics.
- Red flags: a dilution multiplier with no cure period; reserved matters that let a minority block a lender-required action; a ROFR with no time limit; tag-along missing for a minority; several guarantees from sponsors of very different credit; completion definitions that a sponsor can satisfy before the plant performs; comfort letters presented as support.
- Rules of thumb with limits: indicative completion guarantee caps and LC rating thresholds only as dated ranges (D-011).
- Common mistakes: drafting the SHA without reading the finance documents; using "lock-up" for transfer restrictions; assuming a parent's keepwell is a guarantee; forgetting that share pledges transfer voting rights on enforcement.
- Questions experts ask: Who decides a claim against a sponsor's affiliate? What happens to my stake if the majority sells? When exactly are the sponsors released, and who certifies it?

## 26.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are the lead arranger for a 1.1 mtpa green ammonia plant in Oman (Illustrative), first-of-a-kind electrolyzer scale. Sponsors are a Gulf utility (A-rated, 55%) and a European developer (unrated, 45%). They offer a several completion guarantee capped at USD 210 million, released at mechanical completion plus a 30-day run. The IE's P90 overrun is USD 340 million and the offtake contract's long-stop date is 18 months after scheduled completion. Credit committee is in ten days. Reasoning: compare the cap with P90; the release trigger against what the lenders need proven (sustained output at contracted specification for 90 days, offtake contract in effect); the unrated sponsor's several share (Example 26.4 logic); options: LC backing for the developer's share, a higher cap, joint liability for part of the cap, a cost-overrun facility from a DFI, a later release. Expert answer: accept a cap at or near P90 only with LC backing for the developer's share and a release tied to a 90-day reliability and performance test plus a funded DSRA; otherwise ask for joint liability up to the cap. What would change the answer: a proven technology (lower P90, earlier release); an investment-grade offtaker taking delay risk through a long-stop extension; ECA cover on the electrolyzer supplier.

## 27.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Contracts move risk between the parties to a project; insurance moves some of it outside the project altogether, for a premium, on terms the market sets and resets every year. This chapter teaches the insurance program a project finance lender expects: what each construction and operational cover pays for and excludes; how delay in start-up and business interruption are sized to debt service and fixed costs; what lenders require so that the policies pay them and cannot be voided by the project company's conduct; how insurance proceeds are applied; what happens when cover becomes unavailable or unaffordable; and how the insurance market cycle reaches the cash flow. The reader sizes a DSU daily indemnity, computes construction and operational claims with time deductibles, tests limit adequacy against a real loss, works a proceeds-application decision and prices a hard-market renewal; then applies an original program adequacy test.

What you will be able to do:

- Specify a construction and operational insurance program for a project and read the policies against the risks they are meant to cover (Capabilities 3, 7).
- Size DSU and BI covers and compute claims net of deductibles (Capabilities 3, 4).
- Draft and negotiate lenders' insurance requirements, including loss payee, non-vitiation, cut-through and proceeds application (Capabilities 6, 10).
- Judge limit adequacy and respond to a hardening market or unavailable cover (Capabilities 11, 15).

## 27.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: reading the lenders' insurance advisor's report (sec 27.9).** Artifact: the report a lenders' insurance advisor delivers before financial close (Case P's is by Marchbank Risk Advisory, Case Bible 1.4; the walkthrough uses an illustrative report, not Case P's). Steps:

1. Read the risk profile section: the hazards the advisor identifies (natural catastrophe, fire, machinery, transit, political) and whether each maps to a cover.
2. Check each cover's sum insured or limit against the basis stated (reinstatement value, maximum foreseeable loss) and the advisor's view of accumulation.
3. Check DSU and BI: indemnity basis, daily amount against debt service and fixed costs, indemnity period against the longest lead-time item, time deductible against the project's liquidity.
4. Check deductibles against the finance documents' caps.
5. Read the exclusions and extensions list for gaps that matter for this technology (defects exclusions, testing and commissioning, battery thermal runaway, cyber, strikes, terrorism).
6. Check the lenders' clauses: loss payee, non-vitiation, waiver of subrogation, cut-through and reinsurance assignment, notice of cancellation, broker's undertaking.
7. Check insurer security: ratings, fronting, reinsurer list, concentration.
8. Read the market commentary and the renewal risk; note which covers the advisor says may become unavailable.
9. Record open items as conditions precedent or conditions subsequent.

## 27.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 27.1, Insurance program adequacy test** (original; label `fw:insurance-adequacy-test`; home sec 27.8). Purpose: decide whether an insurance program protects the lenders and the sponsors against the losses that matter. Steps: (1) list loss scenarios from the risk register (Ch 14) by phase; (2) for each, identify the cover, the trigger and the exclusions; (3) compare the limit with the scenario's maximum foreseeable loss, including accumulation across co-located assets; (4) compare DSU or BI daily amounts and indemnity periods with debt service, fixed costs and the longest repair; (5) compute retained loss (deductibles, time deductibles, uninsured items) and test it against liquidity (contingency, reserves, sponsor support); (6) check lenders' protections (loss payee, non-vitiation, cut-through, subrogation waivers); (7) check insurer security and renewal risk; (8) record residual risk and its bearer. Demonstrated on Example 27.1 and in the Case P installment.

## 27.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: covers by phase; limits and basis; DSU and BI daily amount, indemnity period, time deductible; deductible caps; exclusions that matter for the technology; lenders' clauses; insurer ratings and reinsurance; broker's undertaking; renewal calendar and unavailability clause; who insures in a multi-contract structure.
- Red flags: DSU indemnity period shorter than the longest lead time; property limit set per facility on a co-located site; contractor-controlled program with no lenders' clauses; fronting insurer without cut-through; waiver of subrogation that excludes or includes the wrong counterparties; unavailability clause with no consent right for lenders.
- Rules of thumb with limits: deductible and indemnity period norms only as dated indicative ranges (D-011) with the driver.
- Common mistakes: sizing DSU on revenue when debt service is the point (or the reverse when equity is the client); forgetting that DSU responds only to insured physical damage; treating PRI as property insurance; assuming a policy that paid in full was adequate.
- Questions experts ask: What is the longest repair, and is the indemnity period longer? What does one event at this site cost if it spreads? Who can void this cover, and do the lenders survive it?

## 27.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are the facility agent on an operating 600 MW CCGT portfolio in Turkey (Illustrative). At renewal, the broker reports that property and BI cover with the required USD 900 million limit is available only with deductibles of USD 10 million (finance documents cap them at USD 5 million) and a premium up 71%; a USD 700 million limit is available at the old deductible for a 38% increase. The sponsors ask the lenders to waive the deductible cap. Renewal is in 12 days. Reasoning: apply Framework 27.1 (maximum foreseeable loss against both limits; retained loss against DSRA and liquidity); the unavailability clause's test; the insurance advisor's confirmation; options such as a waiver with a sponsor-funded deductible reserve, a temporary lower limit with a cure plan, or a captive layer. Expert answer: grant a 12-month waiver of the deductible cap conditional on a sponsor-funded deductible reserve equal to the extra retained amount and a re-marketing plan, rather than accepting a limit below the maximum foreseeable loss. What would change the answer: an MFL study showing USD 700 million is enough (take the lower limit); weak sponsors (insist on the cap or prepayment); a single-site asset (accumulation lower).

## 28.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapters 17 to 27 taught each contract on its own. Lenders lend against all of them at once, and a project fails at the joins: where one contract's relief event is another's breach, where a term ends before the debt does, where a counterparty can terminate before anyone can cure. This chapter teaches the two instruments that make the contract set work as a system for the lenders, and the two frameworks that test it. Direct agreements give the lenders their own contract with each key counterparty: notice, cure periods, step-in, step-out and novation to a new owner. The contract map shows the whole set; the contract gap scan (Framework 28.1) finds mismatches across it; the "Who pays if...?" trace (Framework 28.2) follows one adverse event through every contract and policy to whoever finally bears the loss. The reader sizes a cure period from the lenders' decision timeline, runs a gap scan on an illustrative solar-plus-storage project, traces a transformer failure in numbers, and watches the Case P legal and technical team run the same tools on the Bélanou contract set in June 2018.

What you will be able to do:

- Draft and negotiate a direct agreement, including cure periods, step-in and novation, from the lenders', the sponsor's and the counterparty's seats (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Draw a project's contract map and run a contract gap scan across it (Capabilities 3, 7).
- Trace any adverse event through the contracts and insurances to the party that finally bears the loss, in numbers (Capabilities 3, 11, 15).
- Present the residual risks the gap scan and trace reveal to a credit committee (Capabilities 14, 15).

## 28.5 Opening

May 1, 2020: the Fluor-led design-build joint venture on Maryland's Purple Line gives notice of its intent to terminate its contract, citing more than a year of qualifying delay; on June 23, 2020 the concessionaire, Purple Line Transit Partners, terminates the P3 agreement with the state under a matching 365-day right (facts: `purple-line`). The state sues to stop the exit and loses in September 2020; by December it has settled for USD 250 million, Fluor has left entirely, and the concessionaire must find a new builder. Two contracts, drafted to mirror each other, had turned one party's exit right into the whole project's. The opening ends on the question every lender should ask of a contract set before close: what happens to everything else when one contract moves?

## 28.6 Worked examples

**Example 28.1. How long a cure period must be (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.1`. Scenario: a 210 MW geothermal project in Indonesia (Illustrative) whose steam supply agreement lets the steam field operator terminate 60 days after a notice of payment default. The lenders' realistic decision path: notice reaches the security agent and is circulated (5 days); facility agent convenes lenders (3 days); IE and insurance advisor assess the cause (21 days); lenders' credit approvals for step-in (30 days); step-in entity set up and appointed (14 days). Compute: 73 days, 13 more than the counterparty offers. Show the negotiated outcome: a 90-day cure period for non-payment defaults (30 for payment defaults, with lenders able to pay arrears), extendable by up to 90 more days while the lenders diligently pursue a cure. Explain why counterparties accept it: their alternative is a terminated contract and a stranded plant.

**Example 28.2. A gap scan on a solar-plus-storage project (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.2`; supported by Exhibit 28.4. Scenario: a 150 MW solar plus 75 MW / 300 MWh battery project in Jordan (Illustrative), COD January 1, 2027; PPA 20 years to December 31, 2046; senior debt final maturity December 31, 2044. Instruments and expiry dates: generation license 15 years to October 31, 2041; environmental license to May 31, 2045; O&M agreement initial term to December 31, 2031, renewable at the operator's option only; battery capacity warranty to December 31, 2036, while the PPA requires capacity to be maintained through 2046; land lease to February 28, 2049; grid connection agreement to June 30, 2051. Compute (by date arithmetic): the generation license ends 3.17 years before debt maturity and 5.17 years before PPA expiry; the environmental license ends 0.41 years after debt maturity but 1.59 years before PPA expiry; the O&M agreement's committed term leaves 13.0 years of the debt and 15.0 years of the PPA without a committed operator; the battery warranty leaves 8.0 years of debt and 10.0 years of PPA capacity obligations to the owner; land and grid are long enough (land 4.16 years beyond debt and 2.16 years beyond the PPA; grid 6.49 and 4.50 years). Score each gap and assign fixes: renewal undertaking from the regulator and a CP on legal opinion for the generation license; owner's renewal option for O&M; an augmentation reserve and budget for the battery (forward reference Ch 37). Key result: the dangerous gap is a short-dated license, not the commercial contracts.

**Example 28.3. Who pays when a wind farm's main transformer fails (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.3`; supported by Exhibit 28.5. Scenario: a 168 MW onshore wind farm in Mexico (Illustrative), Year 3 of operation; P50 553.8 GWh a year (net capacity factor 37.6%); as-produced PPA at USD 48.65/MWh (the offtaker pays only for energy delivered); the main step-up transformer fails after a switching error by the O&M operator and is out for 128 days. Property damage USD 3.86 million, deductible USD 0.75 million; BI insured on gross profit at 94% of revenue (variable costs saved are 6%), 60-day time deductible; transformer outside the OEM's defects period; O&M operator's liability for its negligence capped at 25% of its USD 9.36 million annual fee (USD 2.34 million); insurers have waived subrogation against the O&M operator as a co-insured. Compute: revenue per day USD 73,815; revenue lost USD 9.45 million (9.448); BI paid USD 4.72 million (68 × 69,386 = 4.718); property paid USD 3.11 million; insurers' total USD 7.83 million (7.828); project's net loss before the operator USD 4.91 million (lost revenue 9.448 minus BI 4.718 minus variable costs saved 0.567 plus the 0.75 deductible = 4.913); operator pays its cap of USD 2.34 million; equity bears USD 2.57 million (2.573) through lower distributions; offtaker and lenders bear nothing unless distributions cannot absorb it. Then vary one link at a time (no subrogation waiver; operator cap at 10% of fee; a 90-day transformer lead time instead of 128 days) and show how the residual bearer changes.

## 28.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: reviewing a direct agreement line by line (sec 28.3).** Artifact: an illustrative tripartite direct agreement among a state-owned offtaker, a project company and a security agent (drafted originally for the book, about 12 clauses, presented in a `clause` box in excerpts). Steps:

1. Parties and recitals: confirm the security agent signs for all secured parties and that the counterparty is the contracting entity, not a parent.
2. Consent to security and acknowledgment of assignment: confirm it covers the contract, its proceeds and any replacement contract; under civil law, check the notice and acceptance formalities.
3. Payment direction: payments into the account the lenders control (Ch 52).
4. Notice of default: what the counterparty must send, to whom, and within how many days.
5. Cure periods: separate payment and non-payment defaults; extension while lenders diligently pursue a cure (Example 28.1).
6. Standstill: no termination or suspension during the cure and step-in periods.
7. Step-in: who may step in, liability for past arrears (usually limited to amounts the lenders choose to pay), length of step-in, step-out on notice.
8. Novation: transferee criteria and the counterparty's consent standard; release of the original project company.
9. Termination compensation and its payment to the lenders (government counterparties, Ch 17).
10. Counterparty's own protections: information, no amendment without its consent, limits on lenders' liability.
11. Governing law and forum, aligned with the main contract (Ch 54).
12. Record each deviation from the lenders' standard position and its price in the term sheet negotiation log.

## 28.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 28.1, Contract gap scan** (original, named in standards Section 7; label `fw:contract-gap-scan`; home sec 28.5). Purpose: find every mismatch across a project's contract set before financial close. Steps: (1) Draw the contract map (sec 28.1). (2) Build the grid: contracts down, dimensions across (Exhibit 28.3). (3) Fill each cell from the documents, quoting clause references. (4) For each dimension, compare every contract with the offtake contract and the finance documents' tenor, and mark each mismatch. (5) Classify each mismatch: relief in time but not money; relief in one contract but breach in another; term shorter than debt or offtake; cap below exposure; termination without cure. (6) Score severity and likelihood. (7) Assign a fix and its owner, or name the residual bearer. (8) Feed the log into the CP checklist, the model (cost or reserve) and the credit paper. Use it at term sheet, before signing, and on any amendment.
- **Framework 28.2, "Who pays if...?" trace** (original, named in standards Section 7; label `fw:who-pays-if`; home sec 28.6, numbered 28.2 consistent with the style sheet example). Purpose: follow one adverse event through every contract and policy to whoever finally bears the loss. Steps: (1) State the event precisely (cause, timing, duration, physical damage or not). (2) Quantify the gross loss by component (repair, lost revenue, extra cost, penalties payable). (3) Walk the contracts in order of response: the counterparty that caused it (indemnity, LDs, caps), the insurances (trigger, deductibles, limits, subrogation), the offtake contract (deemed payments, relief, penalties), the input contracts, the sponsor support documents. (4) Net each component through each layer. (5) Name the residual bearer and its capacity (equity distributions, reserves, debt). (6) Vary one link at a time to find the weakest. (7) Record the result in the gap log. Demonstrated on Example 28.3, on Lake Turkana (sec 28.6.3) and in the Case P installment.

## 28.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: contract map current; direct agreement or acknowledgment for every material contract; cure periods against the lenders' decision timeline; step-in liability limited; novation criteria workable; government direct agreement routes compensation to lenders; gap scan grid complete with clause references; trace run for the top five risks; gap log feeds CPs, model and credit paper.
- Red flags: a counterparty refusing any direct agreement; cure periods shorter than 30 days for non-payment defaults; step-in that makes lenders liable for all past arrears; relief that is time-only in one contract and time-and-money in its mirror; a license or lease that ends before the debt; dispute forums that cannot join the parties to an interface dispute; a residual bearer with no money.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: cure and step-in period norms only as dated indicative ranges (D-011), with the driver (counterparty type, asset criticality).
- Common mistakes: scanning only commercial contracts and missing permits and leases; tracing a loss through the contracts but not the insurances, or the reverse; assuming back-to-back words produce back-to-back outcomes; treating a waiver of subrogation as harmless.
- Questions experts ask: Which contract moves first when something breaks? Who pays the first 45 days? If we stepped in tomorrow, what would we owe?

## 28.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are lenders' counsel on a 360 MW solar-plus-storage project in Uzbekistan (Illustrative), two weeks before signing. The state-owned O&M company that will operate the substation (a separate contract from the plant's O&M) refuses to sign a direct agreement; it offers a comfort letter and a 30-day cure period for non-payment defaults. The substation is a single point of failure on the contract map. Reasoning: what the lenders lose without a direct agreement (no notice, no cure, no step-in or novation); alternatives (ministry-level acknowledgment under the implementation agreement, a government direct agreement covering the substation contract, an obligation in the PPA that the offtaker procures substation performance, a deemed-energy remedy for substation failure); run the gap scan and a trace for a substation outage. Expert answer: sign only if the implementation agreement or PPA makes substation failure a government or offtaker risk event with deemed energy and the government direct agreement covers it; otherwise make the direct agreement a CP. What would change the answer: an investment-grade offtaker taking substation risk under the PPA (accept); a short tenor; a DFI or PRI cover that responds to non-performance of state entities.
