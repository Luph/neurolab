## 23.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapter 22 taught the lump-sum turnkey, date-certain EPC contract as the lenders' preferred answer to construction risk: one contractor, one price, one date, one set of guarantees. This chapter teaches what happens when that answer is unavailable or too expensive, which is the norm in offshore wind, LNG, mining, large transport PPPs and much of the energy transition. The reader learns how split and multi-contract structures, EPCM, wraps, interface agreements and joint-venture contractors redistribute construction risk; how the FIDIC and NEC standard forms allocate it and why lenders amend them; and how supply-chain concentration and the credit of equipment suppliers create exposures that last long after handover. Each structure is priced: the reader computes a wrap premium against expected interface losses, the cascade cost of one late handover, the owner's retained overrun under EPCM, a commodity price adjustment and a supplier's uncovered credit exposure, and drafts the interface, wrap-cap and EPCM liability provisions that close the gaps.

What you will be able to do:

- Choose among single EPC, split, multi-contract and EPCM delivery for a given project and price the construction risk the owner keeps under each (Capabilities 3, 15).
- Map the interfaces in a multi-contract package and draft the interface, wrap and EPCM liability provisions that close them (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Mark up a FIDIC- or NEC-based construction contract for a project financing (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Measure an equipment supplier's credit exposure over time and size the security that covers it (Capabilities 3, 7).

**23.4 Wraps** (`sec:23.4`)
- 23.4.1 Full, partial, and performance wraps (`ssec:23.4.1`). A full wrap puts one entity on the hook for the whole works; a partial wrap covers a subset (for example, the power island); a performance wrap guarantees output and heat rate across packages without taking delay risk. Wrap guarantee from a lead contractor, its parent, or a specialist wrapper.
- 23.4.2 Wrap caps and the sum-of-caps problem (`ssec:23.4.2`). The wrapper's cap is usually below the sum of the package caps it sits over, so the owner's recovery for a multi-package failure is the lower of the two. Worked in prose with Example 23.1's packages: if each package carries a 100% cap and the wrapper caps at 30% of the wrapped price (Illustrative), a cascade loss above EUR 859.1 million (30% of EUR 2,863.7 million) falls back on the package contracts and the owner. Clause 23.2 variants (wrap guarantee liability cap: 23.2a sponsor-friendly, 23.2b lender-friendly, 23.2c contractor-friendly) with the landing paragraph, which the writer states as a D-011 indicative position with market and period (for example, European offshore wind partial wraps, 2020 to 2025), signed off by the domain reviewer; no percentage norm is stated.
- 23.4.3 Who can give a wrap (`ssec:23.4.3`). The wrapper needs technical competence over every package and a balance sheet large enough to make the cap credible. Why OEMs rarely wrap civil works and civil contractors rarely wrap turbines. Bonding capacity as a constraint: Siemens Energy described advance payment, performance and warranty guarantees as "usually between 5% and 25% of the order value" and in November 2023 needed a EUR 12 billion guarantee facility, EUR 7.5 billion of it counter-guaranteed by the German government (facts: `t-construction-norms`, quote only as the sheet gives it).

## 23.6 Worked examples

**Example 23.1. Wrapped or multi-contract offshore wind (Illustrative).** Label `ex:23.1`. Setting: Dünenfeld Offshore GmbH (fictional project company) is taking a 924 MW offshore wind farm in the German North Sea to final investment decision in March 2025, with prices in EUR at 2025 levels; its owner's engineer is Marlow Holt Engineering (fictional), and one European EPCI consortium (unnamed) has offered a wrapped bid. Packages (EUR million): turbine supply 1,148.6; turbine transport and installation 151.6; foundations supply 412.3; foundations installation 186.9; array and export cables, supply and install 296.8; offshore substation 251.9; onshore works 97.4. Sum of packages EUR 2,545.5 million. The single-contractor wrapped bid prices at a 12.5% premium (Illustrative; the chapter states no market wrap premium): EUR 318.2 million, for a wrapped price of EUR 2,863.7 million. The owner's engineer assesses interface losses under multi-contracting as a discrete distribution: 50% probability of none; 30% of EUR 48.7 million (vessel standby and resequencing); 15% of EUR 163.2 million (a foundation-installation season lost); 5% of EUR 391.5 million (cable failure discovered at energization plus season loss). Compute: expected interface loss EUR 58.7 million (58.665); P90 interface loss EUR 163.2 million (cumulative probability 80% at 48.7 and 95% at 163.2); expected saving from multi-contracting EUR 259.5 million (318.19 minus 58.665); saving at P90 EUR 155.0 million. The sponsor proposes an interface contingency of 4% of package cost (EUR 101.8 million); lenders size to the P90 interface loss, so they ask for EUR 61.4 million more contingency or sponsor support (163.2 minus 101.82). Key results: the wrap is expensive in expectation, the tail is what lenders finance against, and the decision turns on who funds the gap between the sponsor's contingency and the lenders' downside. State the simplification: a four-point distribution stands in for the independent engineer's full risk model (Ch 9 for distributions).

**Example 23.2. One late handover in a split hydro contract (Illustrative).** Label `ex:23.2`. Setting: Kaligandaki Rapti Hydro Ltd (fictional) owns a 96 MW run-of-river plant in Gandaki Province, Nepal; the civil contract and the electromechanical (E&M) supply-and-install contract were signed in 2023 with two fictional contractors, Siwalik Civil Works and Danube Hydro Equipment, and the powerhouse handover falls in 2025; amounts in USD. Civil contract price USD 87.3 million; civil delay LDs USD 96,000 per day capped at 10% of the civil price (USD 8.73 million). The civil contractor hands over the powerhouse 112 days late. The E&M supplier claims standby of USD 61,300 per day plus remobilization of USD 1.86 million. The owner's own costs of delay (extended owner's team, insurance extension, interest during the extended construction period, taken here as a blended daily figure) are USD 118,500 per day. The PPA with the state utility (unnamed) charges delay LDs of USD 42,000 per day after a 30-day grace period. ## 23.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: marking up a standard-form contract for a project financing (sec:23.8).** The artifact is a contractor's design-build or EPC/turnkey standard form proposed by a bidder with "particular conditions" left blank (an Illustrative bid for a 180 MW solar-plus-storage plant in Morocco's Drâa-Tafilalet region, 2026). Steps:

1. Identify the book, edition, reprint and amendment set, and read the general conditions for the risk allocation baseline: who designs, who bears unforeseen ground, what counts as an employer's risk event. Check the project against FIDIC's own Silver Book unsuitability criteria (`t-contract-law`).
2. List every employer's risk event and compare it with the offtake contract's relief events (Ch 18) and the concession's relief and compensation events (ssec:17.3.2). Any event that gives the contractor time and money but gives the project company nothing under the offtake contract is a gap; mark it.
3. Replace the completion and testing provisions with tests matching the lenders' financial completion definition (forward reference ssec:26.5.2) and the offtake contract's commissioning tests.
4. Calibrate delay LDs and caps to the debt service and fixed costs they must cover (method from Ch 22), and check the aggregate cap against the sum of LD sub-caps.
5. Insert the security package: performance security, advance payment guarantee, retention, parent company guarantee (Ch 22 terms).
6. Insert the finance provisions the standard form lacks: assignment by way of security, consent to a direct agreement (forward reference sec:28.2), step-in, novation to a substitute project company, lenders' and IE's access rights.
7. Check the disputes clause against the other contracts so a multi-party interface dispute can be heard in one forum (FIDIC's DAAB under Clause 21 against the other contracts' forums).
8. Record every change in a particular-conditions table that the lenders' counsel and IE sign off.

## 23.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 23.1, Construction structure selector** (original; label `fw:construction-structure-selector`; home `ssec:23.1.3` as registered). Purpose: choose a delivery structure and state the support lenders will require. Steps: (1) Is a creditworthy contractor willing to wrap the whole works at a premium the project can afford? If yes, single EPC. (2) If not, count the interfaces and rate each by consequence (Example 23.2 method). (3) Test the owner's capacity to manage them (owner's engineer, track record). (4) Choose split, multi-contract or EPCM accordingly. (5) Price the retained risk at P50 and P90 (Examples 23.1 and 23.3). (6) Set the lenders' price: contingency to P90, interface agreement, partial or performance wrap where available, sponsor completion support for the rest. Demonstrated on Examples 23.1 and 23.3, in Exercise 23.17 and in the judgment drill.

## 23.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: package list with scope boundaries; interface matrix signed by every contractor; single integrated schedule with float ownership stated; aggregate LD and cap map across packages; wrap or performance wrap coverage; free-issue equipment warranties; supplier exposure profile against security; serial-defect clause in every major supply contract; standard-form edition, reprint and amendment set named; standard-form amendments table; disputes joinder.
- Red flags: interface contingency set as a round percentage with no interface register behind it; an EPCM fee calculated on actual cost with no cap or target; an EPCM liability cap set as a small multiple of fees with the owner's cost of correction uncapped; a JV whose partners are severally, not jointly, liable; a wrap cap below the largest single package cap; warranty bonds that expire before the guarantees they secure; a sole-source transformer with no reserved slot; a Silver Book used for uninspectable ground.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: only what the sheets verify (owner-procured LDs of up to 10% of construction cost at Venture Global, 2024; OEM guarantees "usually between 5% and 25% of the order value" per Siemens Energy, 2023; transformer lead times of two to three years, IEA 2026), each with its market and date and the condition under which it fails. No wrap-premium or EPCM-fee rule of thumb is printed.
- Common mistakes: treating a split contract as two contracts rather than one risk; assuming back-to-back means identical words; letting the owner become the integrator by default through free-issue equipment; citing a FIDIC clause number without checking the edition.
- Questions experts ask: Who is the integrator? Whose cap responds first? Which package's delay moves COD? What happens to the warranty if the supplier is acquired or fails, or the defect turns out to be fleet-wide?

## 23.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are the lead arranger on a 480 MW solar-plus-storage project near Ouarzazate in Morocco's Drâa-Tafilalet region (Illustrative; sponsor Atlas Sol Énergie SA, fictional), four weeks from your credit committee on November 12, 2026. The EPC contractor's price includes a battery system it buys from an integrator. The sponsor proposes to take the battery system out of the EPC wrap and buy it directly from a BB-rated integrator, saving USD 14.6 million. The EPC contractor will then give no performance guarantee on the combined plant's dispatch. Reasoning: what the saving buys versus the interface and supplier exposure created (use Examples 23.2 and 23.5 logic); what the IE must certify; which of the following the lenders demand: a partial performance wrap from the EPC contractor at a fee, a supplier bond profile tracking exposure, an interface agreement, extra contingency, sponsor support for battery delay. Expert answer: accept the split only with an interface agreement, a bond profile that follows the exposure, and contingency sized to the P90 interface loss, and require the sponsor to keep part of the saving in contingency. What would change the answer: an investment-grade integrator or a parent company guarantee from one (moves toward accepting); an EPC contractor willing to give a performance wrap for less than the saving (keep the wrap); a PPA with tight battery availability LDs payable from COD (moves against the split); a battery model with a fleet-wide defect history (moves against the split).

## 24.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Once the EPC contractor hands over, the project company spends 20 to 30 years buying three things: people who run the plant, an equipment manufacturer who keeps the critical machines alive, and someone who manages the whole on the owners' behalf. This chapter teaches the O&M agreement, the long-term service agreement (LTSA) and the asset management agreement as risk-transfer instruments: what each covers, how its fee is built (fixed, cost-plus, per equivalent operating hour), how its performance regime and liability cap compare with the revenue the project loses when the plant underperforms, and how major maintenance is planned and paid for. The reader computes the expected cost of fixed and cost-plus fee structures, the share of an availability shortfall an operator bears, an LTSA's cost per MWh under peaking and baseload dispatch, and the cash effect of lumpy major maintenance; drafts the availability guarantee, the LTSA fee-and-guarantee schedule and the asset manager's termination right; then applies an original test of whether operating incentives line up with the offtake contract.

What you will be able to do:

- Read an O&M agreement, an LTSA and an asset management agreement and state which operating risks each transfers and which stay with the project company (Capability 3).
- Compute and compare fee structures and performance incentives against the revenue at risk under the offtake contract (Capabilities 3, 4).
- Plan major maintenance and its funding over the life of the debt (Capabilities 4, 10).
- Design and draft an operating incentive package that a lender, a sponsor and an operator will each sign (Capabilities 6, 15).

## 24.6 Worked examples

**Example 24.1. Fixed fee or cost-plus for a geothermal plant (Illustrative).** Label `ex:24.1`. Setting: Rift Thermal Power Ltd (fictional) owns a 126 MW geothermal plant in Nakuru County, Kenya, and in 2026 compares two bids for its first operating year (2027) from two fictional O&M firms; amounts in USD. Fixed-fee bid USD 9.37 million a year. Cost-plus alternative: reimbursed actual cost plus a 12% fee. Actual cost outcomes: USD 7.21 million (25%), USD 7.95 million (50%), USD 9.46 million (25%). Compute: owner's cost under cost-plus USD 8.075, 8.904 and 10.595 million; expected cost-plus cost USD 9.12 million (9.1196) against USD 9.37 million fixed, so the owner pays USD 0.25 million a year in expectation for certainty; the operator's margin under the fixed fee is USD 2.16, 1.42 and (0.09) million in the three outcomes. Key result: the fixed fee buys a cap on a cost that lenders size against in the downside; the right comparison is the downside (USD 10.595 million against 9.37), not the mean.

**Example 24.2. How much of an availability shortfall the operator bears (Illustrative).** Label `ex:24.2`. Setting: Meghna Peaking Power Ltd (fictional) owns a 220 MW open-cycle peaking plant in Bangladesh whose 2024 PPA with the state power buyer (unnamed) pays a capacity charge of USD 9.85/kW-month on contracted capacity times the lesser of 1 and availability divided by a 92% target (an application of eq:18.1, cited, not re-derived); the 2026 operating year is the case. The O&M operator (fictional) earns an annual fee of USD 6.3 million; its availability malus is USD 45,000 per point below 92%, capped at 20% of the fee (contract terms of this deal, not a market norm). Compute: capacity payment at target USD 26.0 million a year (26.004); revenue lost per availability point below target USD 282,652; the operator bears 15.9% of each point; the cap (USD 1.26 million) is reached only at 28 points below target. At 86% availability the project loses USD 1.70 million (1.696) of capacity payments and the operator pays USD 0.27 million. Key result: an O&M performance regime aligns incentives at the margin but never transfers the revenue risk; the residual sits with equity and, below that, with lenders.

## 24.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 24.1, Operating incentive alignment test** (original; label `fw:operating-incentive-alignment`; home sec:24.6). Purpose: test whether the operating contracts make each operator want what the offtake contract pays for. Steps: (1) List what the offtake contract pays for and penalizes (availability, heat rate, response, starts). (2) For each, find the operator or LTSA provider incentive and its measurement period. (3) Compute the operator's share of revenue at risk per unit of shortfall (Example 24.2 method). ## 24.9 Real cases

## 24.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: scope matrix across O&M, LTSA, asset manager and owner; operating budget approval process; performance measurement definitions identical to the offtake contract's; operator liability cap against revenue at risk; LTSA term against debt and offtake term under each dispatch case; exclusions mapped to the major maintenance plan; replacement triggers and transition duties; renewal options and whose they are; direct agreements.
- Red flags: availability measured on a different period or definition from the PPA; an O&M bonus earned above the PPA target; an LTSA that ends on EOH before the debt matures; unapproved-parts clauses that void warranties; an affiliated operator whose claims the sponsor decides; an operator's right to decline renewal with notice shorter than a replacement takes.
- Rules of thumb with limits: only verified fee shapes (Guernsey O&M and CSA, 2019; Hadera Expansion CSA, 2026; Ellomay solar O&M) with market and date; no operator or OEM cap levels and no guaranteed availability percentages are printed as norms (`t-om-ltsa-norms` "Do not state"); typical inspection intervals only as the Case P contract states them or as D-011 indicative ranges with their driver.
- Common mistakes: comparing fixed and cost-plus on the mean rather than the downside; ignoring starts; double-counting maintenance in the LTSA fee and the major maintenance line; letting the O&M agreement expire with the first renewal at the operator's option only.
- Questions experts ask: Who decides a claim against the operator? What happens on the day the LTSA ends? What does a start cost, and who pays for it?

## 24.12 Judgment drill

## 25.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A power plant with a perfect PPA and a perfect EPC contract still earns nothing if gas does not arrive, the cooling water is too warm, the transmission line is late, the land lease can be terminated or a permit dies with a change of owner. This chapter teaches the contracts and rights that bring inputs in and give the project access: fuel and feedstock supply agreements (with the gas sale agreement as the model), transportation from the shipper's side, water, grid connection and interconnection, land rights and permits. Each is taught as a link in a chain that must line up with the offtake contract in term, quantity, quality, price, delivery point, relief events and termination. The reader computes a plant's gas requirement and sets DCQ and MDQ, runs a take-or-pay and make-up account over four years, prices a fuel-offtake mismatch, values a grid delay with and without deemed energy, and tests land tenure against the debt; drafts the transportation, water, grid and land provisions lenders need; then applies an original alignment grid to a whole input chain.

What you will be able to do:

- Read a gas sale agreement and set its quantities, take-or-pay and make-up terms against the plant's dispatch and the PPA (Capabilities 3, 4).
- Test every input and access contract for alignment with the offtake contract and the debt, and price the gaps (Capabilities 3, 7).
- Diligence grid connection, land rights and permits for a lender, including transferability on enforcement (Capabilities 7, 10).
- Draft and negotiate fuel, transport, water, grid and land terms from the project company's, the lenders' and the counterparty's chairs (Capabilities 6, 15).

## 25.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: fuel requirement under every dispatch case; ACQ, DCQ, MDQ consistent with plant and pipeline; take-or-pay level against downside dispatch; make-up recovery rule and window; deliver-or-pay remedies matched to PPA deemed availability; off-spec gas treated as seller shortfall; price pass-through and its caps; gas quality spec matched to the turbine's fuel spec; transport capacity and term coterminous with supply; water priority class and curtailment remedy; connection agreement dates against the PPA's long-stop dates; land tenure against debt plus tail and PPA plus decommissioning; permit register with transfer rules.
- Red flags: take-or-pay above the downside dispatch with no pass-through; a supplier whose upstream field lacks certified reserves for the term; a fuel price below market enforceable only under the supplier's law; seller force majeure that includes reservoir underperformance; a connection built by a state entity with no deemed-energy cover; an unregistered lease; permits held by a sponsor affiliate rather than the project company.
- Rules of thumb with limits: take-or-pay levels, make-up windows and connection lead times only as dated indicative ranges (D-011), with the driver of each.
- Common mistakes: matching DCQ to full load instead of expected dispatch; treating make-up as free money; reading deemed energy as unconditional; ignoring decommissioning in the land term; assuming a take-or-pay term will be enforced against a sole regional supplier (WAGP).
- Questions experts ask: What happens to the gas price when the market moves 50% against the seller? Who builds the last kilometer of line and who pays if it is late? Can the lenders hold the permits after enforcement?

## 26.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every other contract in Part IV binds the project company to an outsider. The documents in this chapter bind the owners: to each other while the project is an idea (development and co-development agreements), to each other once it is a company (the shareholders' or joint venture agreement), and to the lenders until the project has proven itself (the equity contribution agreement and sponsor support, above all the completion guarantee). The reader learns how sponsors share development cost and dilute a partner who stops paying; how boards, reserved matters and deadlock work; how transfer rights (lock-in, ROFR, ROFO, tag-along, drag-along) decide who can leave and on what terms; how an equity contribution agreement makes an equity promise bankable; and how lenders define the completion that releases sponsors. The reader computes dilution, voting blocks, an equity commitment's LC profile and acceleration, and a capped, several completion guarantee's expected cost to sponsors and residual risk to lenders, and drafts the funding-default, transfer, equity-acceleration, completion-definition and guarantee provisions.

What you will be able to do:

- Draft and negotiate the key terms of a co-development agreement and a shareholders' agreement from a majority and a minority seat (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Compute dilution, voting blocks and transfer outcomes under a shareholders' agreement (Capability 3).
- Structure and draft an equity contribution agreement and a completion guarantee that lenders will accept and sponsors can afford (Capabilities 5, 6).
- Decide how much sponsor support a project needs and when it should fall away (Capabilities 10, 15).

## 26.6 Worked examples

**Example 26.1. Diluting a partner who stops paying (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.1`; Equation (26.1), `eq:26.1`: defaulting partner's share = its credited contributions ÷ total credited contributions, where the non-defaulting partner is credited with its own share of the call plus m times the defaulter's share it covered (m = 1 for straight dilution). Excel: with prior contributions of A and B in F5 and F6, the call in F7, A's share in F8 and the multiplier in F9, B's diluted share `\xl{=F6/(F5+F7*F8+F7*(1-F8)*F9+F6)}`. Setting: two developers of a 300 MW wind project in Ninh Thuan province, Vietnam, Lotus Coast Renewables (A, 60%) and Haldane Wind Developments (B, 40%) (both fictional), have spent USD 8.62 million by March 2024 (A 5.172, B 3.448). A cash call of USD 2.75 million follows (A 1.65, B 1.10); B fails to fund and A funds the whole call. Compute: straight dilution leaves B with 30.33% (3.448 ÷ 11.37) and A with 69.67%; punitive dilution crediting A's cover of B's USD 1.10 million at 150% (A credited 5.172 + 1.65 + 1.65 = 8.472, total 11.92) leaves B with 28.93%. Key result: punitive dilution is a deterrent, not compensation; minority partners negotiate the multiplier and a cure period.

**Example 26.2. Who can block a reserved matter (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.2`. Setting: Enguri Highlands Hydro LLC (fictional) is building a 380 MW hydro plant in the Samegrelo-Zemo Svaneti region of Georgia (the country); its three shareholders signed their SHA in 2025 and hold 45%, 30% and 25%; reserved matters need 75%. Compute every coalition: 45 + 30 = 75 passes; 45 + 25 = 70 fails; 30 + 25 = 55 fails. Result: the 45% and 30% holders each have a veto; the 25% holder has none. Then show the effect of moving the threshold to 70% (45 + 25 passes; the 30% holder loses its veto) and to 80% (only unanimity passes, so every holder has a veto). Key result: thresholds are negotiated against stakes; a minority bargains for the threshold, not for seats. (Thresholds are contract terms and are round by convention, which is the teaching point.)

**Example 26.3. An equity commitment backed by letters of credit (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.3`. Setting: Nueva Ecija Sunfields Inc. (fictional) is building a 240 MW solar project in Luzon, the Philippines, with construction from January 2026 to June 2027; total funding requirement USD 248.7 million, equity 27% (USD 67.15 million, 67.149), contributed pro rata with debt over 18 months on an S-curve (monthly weights 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 8, 7, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 2, summing to 94); each sponsor's undrawn commitment is backed by an LC at 1.15% a year. Compute: average undrawn LC balance USD 34.29 million (34.289); total LC fees over construction USD 0.59 million (0.5915); an event of default at the start of Month 8 accelerates the undrawn USD 42.86 million (42.861), payable under the LCs. Key results: the LC converts an equity promise into bank credit the lenders can draw; its cost is small against the gearing it supports; Ch 32 compares this with equity first.

**Example 26.4. A capped, several completion guarantee (Illustrative).** Label `ex:26.4`. Setting: Calcasieu Polymers LLC (fictional) is building a USD 617.4 million chemicals plant in Louisiana on the US Gulf Coast, financed in 2025; sponsors A (70%) and B (30%) (fictional) give several completion guarantees capped in aggregate at USD 85.0 million for cost overruns (a negotiated round cap, as such caps usually are). Overrun outcomes: none (55%), USD 41.3 million (25%), USD 96.8 million (12%), USD 158.4 million (8%). Compute: expected cost to sponsors USD 27.33 million (27.325); expected overrun beyond the cap, left to lenders and the project, USD 7.29 million (7.288). If the overrun is USD 96.8 million and B is insolvent: A pays USD 59.5 million, B owes USD 25.5 million it cannot pay, and USD 37.3 million is unfunded (25.5 plus the 11.8 above the cap). Key results: several liability and a cap both cut the lenders' protection exactly in the cases that matter; lenders respond with rating triggers, LC backing for weaker sponsors, or a joint guarantee for part of the cap.

## 26.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: negotiating a reserved-matters schedule (sec:26.7).** Artifact: a draft SHA schedule of reserved matters circulated in 2026 by a majority sponsor (60%) to a 25% local partner and a 15% DFI-backed fund for a 410 MW gas-fired plant in Côte d'Ivoire (Illustrative names and stakes, not Case P; parties fictional). Steps:

1. Sort the draft list into four groups: constitutional (articles, share capital, new classes), financial (budgets, debt, guarantees, distributions policy), commercial (material contracts, related-party contracts, disposals), and personnel (CEO, CFO, auditors).
2. For each item, ask which party's money or reputation it puts at risk and set the threshold accordingly (Example 26.2 method).
3. Add the minority's protections: related-party contracts and claims decided without the conflicted shareholder; anti-dilution on new issues; information rights.
4. Add the DFI's policy requirements (E&S, integrity, sanctions) as individual consent rights, forward-referenced to Ch 50 and Ch 60.
5. Check against the finance documents: anything the lenders already control (debt, distributions while a distribution lock-up applies) need not be a reserved matter, and anything requiring lender consent must not need a shareholder vote that a minority can block after enforcement.
6. Write the deadlock path: escalation, mediation, expert determination for technical matters, buy-sell (shotgun) only where both sides can fund it and the lenders' transfer restrictions allow it.
7. Produce the final schedule with thresholds and an annotation of why each item sits where it does.

## 26.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 26.1, Sponsor support spectrum** (original; label `fw:sponsor-support-spectrum`; home ssec:26.5.1). Purpose: choose the level and form of sponsor support for a project and the trigger that releases it. Rungs, from strongest to weakest: full recourse guarantee of debt until completion; capped completion guarantee (cost and delay); cost-overrun undertaking (capped equity commitment); contingent equity commitment with LC; equity contribution agreement only (base equity); operating or offtake support from affiliates; keepwell and comfort letters. Steps to use: (1) identify the risks not transferred by contract or insurance (Chapters 22 to 25, 27); (2) price them at P50 and P90 (Examples 23.3, 26.4); (3) choose the rung that covers the P90 residual at the lowest cost to sponsors; (4) set the release trigger (financial completion definition, ssec:26.5.2); (5) test guarantor credit and add rating triggers. Demonstrated on Example 26.4, Exercise 26.17 and the Case P installment (Case P sits at the contingent-equity rung: no completion guarantee, contingent equity plus a standby facility).

## 26.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: co-development budget, cash-call and dilution terms; ownership of development assets on withdrawal; SHA board, quorum, reserved matters, deadlock; transfer rights with lender minimum holdings; related-party conflict rules; equity contribution agreement amount, timing, conditions, acceleration, LC rating triggers; financial completion definition with long-stop; guarantee cap, joint or several, guarantor credit, release mechanics.
- Red flags: a dilution multiplier with no cure period; reserved matters that let a minority block a lender-required action; a ROFR with no time limit; tag-along missing for a minority; several guarantees from sponsors of very different credit; completion definitions that a sponsor can satisfy before the plant performs; comfort letters presented as support; a buy-sell clause that the lenders' transfer restrictions make unusable.
- Rules of thumb with limits: indicative completion guarantee caps and LC rating thresholds only as dated ranges (D-011), each with its driver.
- Common mistakes: drafting the SHA without reading the finance documents; using "lock-up" for transfer restrictions; assuming a parent's keepwell is a guarantee; forgetting that share pledges transfer voting rights on enforcement.
- Questions experts ask: Who decides a claim against a sponsor's affiliate? What happens to my stake if the majority sells? When exactly are the sponsors released, and who certifies it?

## 26.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are the lead arranger for a 1.1 mtpa green ammonia plant at Duqm, Oman (Illustrative; first-of-a-kind electrolyzer scale; project company Wusta Green Ammonia SAOC, fictional). Sponsors are a Gulf utility (A-rated, 55%) and a European developer (unrated, 45%) (both fictional). They offer a several completion guarantee capped at USD 210 million, released at mechanical completion plus a 30-day run. The IE's P90 overrun is USD 340 million and the offtake contract's long-stop date is 18 months after scheduled completion. Credit committee meets on November 26, 2026, in ten days. Reasoning: compare the cap with P90; the release trigger against what the lenders need proven (sustained output at contracted specification for 90 days, offtake contract in effect); the unrated sponsor's several share (Example 26.4 logic); options: LC backing for the developer's share, a higher cap, joint liability for part of the cap, a cost-overrun facility from a DFI, a later release. Expert answer: accept a cap at or near P90 only with LC backing for the developer's share and a release tied to a 90-day reliability and performance test plus a funded DSRA; otherwise ask for joint liability up to the cap. What would change the answer: a proven technology (lower P90, earlier release); an investment-grade offtaker taking delay risk through a long-stop extension; ECA cover on the electrolyzer supplier.

## 27.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Contracts move risk between the parties to a project; insurance moves some of it outside the project altogether, for a premium, on terms the market sets and resets every year. This chapter teaches the insurance program a project finance lender expects: what each construction and operational cover pays for and excludes; how delay in start-up and business interruption are sized to debt service and fixed costs; where cyber losses fall between property, business interruption and standalone cyber policies; what lenders require so that the policies pay them and cannot be voided by the project company's conduct; how insurance proceeds are applied; what happens when cover becomes unavailable or unaffordable; and how the insurance market cycle reaches the cash flow. The reader sizes a DSU daily indemnity, computes construction and operational claims with time deductibles, tests limit adequacy against a real loss, works a proceeds-application decision and prices a hard-market renewal; then applies an original program adequacy test.

What you will be able to do:

- Specify a construction and operational insurance program for a project and read the policies against the risks they are meant to cover (Capabilities 3, 7).
- Size DSU and BI covers and compute claims net of deductibles (Capabilities 3, 4).
- Draft and negotiate lenders' insurance requirements, including loss payee, non-vitiation, cut-through and proceeds application (Capabilities 6, 10).
- Judge limit adequacy and respond to a hardening market or unavailable cover (Capabilities 11, 15).

- **Framework 27.1, Insurance program adequacy test** (original; label `fw:insurance-adequacy-test`; home sec:27.8). Purpose: decide whether an insurance program protects the lenders and the sponsors against the losses that matter. Steps: (1) list loss scenarios from the risk register (Ch 14) by phase; (2) for each, identify the cover, the trigger and the exclusions, including how each policy treats cyber; (3) compare the limit with the scenario's maximum foreseeable loss, including accumulation across co-located assets; (4) compare DSU or BI daily amounts and indemnity periods with debt service, fixed costs and the longest repair; (5) compute retained loss (deductibles, time deductibles, uninsured items) and test it against liquidity (contingency, reserves, sponsor support); (6) check lenders' protections (loss payee, non-vitiation, cut-through, subrogation waivers); (7) check insurer security and renewal risk; (8) record residual risk and its bearer. Demonstrated on Example 27.1 and in the Case P installment.

## 27.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: covers by phase; limits and basis; DSU and BI daily amount, indemnity period, time deductible; deductible caps; exclusions that matter for the technology; how each policy treats cyber; lenders' clauses; insurer ratings and reinsurance; broker's undertaking; renewal calendar and unavailability clause; who insures in a multi-contract structure.
- Red flags: DSU indemnity period shorter than the longest lead time; property limit set per facility on a co-located site; contractor-controlled program with no lenders' clauses; fronting insurer without cut-through; waiver of subrogation that excludes or includes the wrong counterparties; silent cyber in the property program; unavailability clause with no consent right for lenders.
- Rules of thumb with limits: deductible and indemnity period norms only as dated indicative ranges (D-011) with the driver; market rate movements only as `t-insurance-market` records them.
- Common mistakes: sizing DSU on revenue when debt service is the point (or the reverse when equity is the client); forgetting that DSU responds only to insured physical damage; treating PRI as property insurance; assuming a policy that paid in full was adequate; assuming a cyber policy covers a state-attributed attack.
- Questions experts ask: What is the longest repair, and is the indemnity period longer? What does one event at this site cost if it spreads? Who can void this cover, and do the lenders survive it? Which policy pays if the control system is hacked and nothing is damaged?

## 27.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are the facility agent on an operating 600 MW CCGT portfolio in western Turkey (Illustrative; borrower Ege Kombine Enerji A.Ş., fictional). At the April 1, 2022 renewal, during the rising market of 2018 to 2023, the broker reports that property and BI cover with the required USD 900 million limit is available only with deductibles of USD 10 million (finance documents cap them at USD 5 million) and a premium up 71%; a USD 700 million limit is available at the old deductible for a 38% increase. The sponsors ask the lenders to waive the deductible cap. Renewal is in 12 days. Reasoning: apply Framework 27.1 (maximum foreseeable loss against both limits; retained loss against DSRA and liquidity); the unavailability clause's test; the insurance advisor's confirmation; options such as a waiver with a sponsor-funded deductible reserve, a temporary lower limit with a cure plan, or a captive layer. Expert answer: grant a 12-month waiver of the deductible cap conditional on a sponsor-funded deductible reserve equal to the extra retained amount and a re-marketing plan, rather than accepting a limit below the maximum foreseeable loss. What would change the answer: an MFL study showing USD 700 million is enough (take the lower limit); weak sponsors (insist on the cap or prepayment); a single-site asset (accumulation lower).

## 28.2 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapters 17 to 27 taught each contract on its own. Lenders lend against all of them at once, and a project fails at the joins: where one contract's relief event is another's breach, where a term ends before the debt does, where a counterparty can terminate before anyone can cure. This chapter teaches the two instruments that make the contract set work as a system for the lenders, and the two frameworks that test it. Direct agreements give the lenders their own contract with each key counterparty: notice, cure periods, step-in, step-out and novation to a new owner. The contract map shows the whole set; the contract gap scan (Framework 28.1) finds mismatches across it; the "Who pays if...?" trace (Framework 28.2) follows one adverse event through every contract and policy to whoever finally bears the loss. The reader sizes a cure period from the lenders' decision timeline, runs a gap scan on an illustrative solar-plus-storage project, traces a wind-farm transformer failure and a hospital chiller failure in numbers, prices a mirrored exit right in a light-rail PPP, and watches the Case P legal and technical team run the same tools on the Bélanou contract set in June 2018.

What you will be able to do:

- Draft and negotiate a direct agreement, including cure periods, step-in and novation, from the lenders', the sponsor's and the counterparty's seats (Capabilities 3, 6).
- Draw a project's contract map and run a contract gap scan across it (Capabilities 3, 7).
- Trace any adverse event through the contracts and insurances to the party that finally bears the loss, in numbers (Capabilities 3, 11, 15).
- Present the residual risks the gap scan and trace reveal to a credit committee (Capabilities 14, 15).

## 28.6 Worked examples

**Example 28.1. How long a cure period must be (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.1`. Setting: Sibayak Selatan Geothermal (fictional project company) is negotiating in 2025 the direct agreement for its 210 MW geothermal plant in North Sumatra, Indonesia, whose steam supply agreement with a fictional steam field operator lets the operator terminate 60 days after a notice of payment default. The lenders' realistic decision path: notice reaches the security agent and is circulated (5 days); facility agent convenes lenders (3 days); IE and insurance advisor assess the cause (21 days); lenders' credit approvals for step-in (30 days); step-in entity set up and appointed (14 days). Compute: 73 days, 13 more than the counterparty offers. Show the negotiated outcome: a 90-day cure period for non-payment defaults (30 for payment defaults, with lenders able to pay arrears), extendable by up to 90 more days while the lenders diligently pursue a cure. Explain why counterparties accept it: their alternative is a terminated contract and a stranded plant. Clause 28.1 drafts the result.

**Example 28.2. A gap scan on a solar-plus-storage project (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.2`; supported by Exhibit 28.4. **Example 28.3. Who pays when a wind farm's main transformer fails (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.3`; supported by Exhibit 28.5. Setting: Istmo Viento Sur SA de CV (fictional) operates a 168 MW onshore wind farm on the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, Oaxaca, Mexico, in Year 3 of operation (2026), with a fictional O&M operator; P50 553.8 GWh a year (net capacity factor 37.6%); as-produced PPA at USD 48.65/MWh with a fictional corporate offtaker (the offtaker pays only for energy delivered); the main step-up transformer fails in March 2026 after a switching error by the O&M operator and is out for 128 days. Compute: revenue per day USD 73,815; revenue lost USD 9.45 million (9.4483); BI paid USD 4.72 million (68 × 69,386 = 4.7182); property paid USD 3.11 million; insurers' total USD 7.83 million (7.8282); project's net loss before the operator USD 4.91 million (lost revenue 9.4483 minus BI 4.7182 minus variable costs saved 0.5669 plus the 0.75 deductible = 4.9131); operator pays its cap of USD 2.34 million; equity bears USD 2.57 million (2.5731) through lower distributions; offtaker and lenders bear nothing unless distributions cannot absorb it. Then vary one link at a time and show how the residual bearer changes: with the operator's cap at 10% of fee (USD 0.936 million), equity bears USD 3.98 million (3.9771); with a 90-day transformer lead time instead of 128 days, the project's net loss is unchanged at USD 4.91 million because the shorter outage removes only insured days (BI falls to USD 2.08 million), so the insurers, not equity, gain; with no subrogation waiver, see Exercise 28.8.

**Example 28.4. Who pays when a hospital's chiller plant fails (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.4`. Setting: Maple Creek Health Partners (fictional project company) operates a 386-bed availability-payment hospital PPP in Ontario, Canada, for a provincial health authority (unnamed contracting authority); amounts in CAD, 2027 values; it is Year 4 of operations. In July 2027 the chiller plant fails and two surgical wings, weighted at 11.6% of the unitary charge, are unavailable for 23 days. Inputs (the deduction rule is supplied, as a payment mechanism would state it; its design is Ch 58's, sec:58.3, and availability deductions are defined in ssec:21.6.1): annual unitary charge CAD 61.84 million; deduction for an unavailable critical area at twice its pro rata daily payment; machinery breakdown cover for the chiller repair of CAD 1.94 million with a CAD 0.25 million deductible; BI cover for deductions caused by insured damage after a 14-day time deductible; the hard facilities management (FM) contractor caused the failure by missing a maintenance routine, and its subcontract passes deductions and uninsured repair costs down to it net of insurance recoveries, capped at 25% of its CAD 14.73 million annual fee (CAD 3.68 million); insurers have waived subrogation against the FM contractor. Compute: deduction per day CAD 39,307 (61.84 million ÷ 365 × 0.116 × 2.0); total deductions CAD 0.90 million (0.904); BI pays CAD 0.35 million (9 days, 0.3538); machinery breakdown pays CAD 1.69 million; uninsured loss CAD 0.80 million (14 days of deductions, 0.5503, plus the 0.25 deductible); the FM contractor pays all CAD 0.80 million, inside its cap; equity and lenders bear nothing; the contracting authority paid CAD 0.90 million less for 23 days of lost service. Vary one link: if the cause were a latent design defect discovered after the D&C contractor's defects period, no pass-down applies and equity bears CAD 0.80 million. Key result: in an availability PPP the trace runs through the deduction regime and the FM pass-down, so the residual bearer is usually a subcontractor, and the gap to find is any loss that falls between the D&C defects period and the FM scope.

**Example 28.5. Matched and unmatched exit rights in a light-rail PPP (Illustrative).** Label `ex:28.5`. Setting: Swan Coastal Light Rail Pty Ltd (fictional concessionaire) is building a light-rail line in Perth, Western Australia, under a 2022 availability PPP with the state's transport agency (unnamed contracting authority); amounts in AUD. In 2024, after more than 365 days of qualifying delay from relief events under the concession, the D&C joint venture (fictional) gives notice of exit under its contract. Remaining works are valued at AUD 1,284.6 million; a replacement contractor prices them at a 17.4% premium and needs nine months to remobilize; senior debt drawn is AUD 1,612.8 million at 6.15%; the concessionaire's remaining contingency is AUD 96.2 million. Compute: replacement premium AUD 223.5 million (223.52); interest during the nine-month remobilization AUD 74.4 million (74.39); total extra cost AUD 297.9 million (297.91). Unmatched (the concession gives the concessionaire no exit): after contingency, AUD 201.7 million (201.71) falls on the sponsors or, if they refuse, on the lenders through a restructuring. Matched (the concession mirrors the D&C exit right, as at the Purple Line): the concessionaire may terminate and receive compensation under the concession's termination regime (Ch 17 principles; PPP formulas Ch 58), so the contracting authority bears the replacement cost and the replacement procurement. Key result: a mirrored exit right moves the cost of a contractor's exit from the private side to the state; the state will price or resist it, and lenders should know before close which side of that line they are on.

## 28.7 Walkthrough

**Walkthrough: reviewing a direct agreement line by line (sec:28.3).** Artifact: an illustrative tripartite direct agreement among a state-owned offtaker, a project company and a security agent for a 220 MW solar plant in Zambia (Illustrative; parties fictional), drafted originally for the book, about 12 clauses, presented in excerpts as Clause 28.3 (direct agreement excerpts, Illustrative), each paragraph annotated. Steps:

1. Parties and recitals: confirm the security agent signs for all secured parties and that the counterparty is the contracting entity, not a parent.
2. Consent to security and acknowledgment of assignment: confirm it covers the contract, its proceeds and any replacement contract; under civil law, check the notice and acceptance formalities.
3. Payment direction: payments into the account the lenders control (Ch 52).
4. Notice of default: what the counterparty must send, to whom, and within how many days.
5. Cure periods: separate payment and non-payment defaults; extension while lenders diligently pursue a cure (Example 28.1; Clause 28.1).
6. Standstill: no termination or suspension during the cure and step-in periods.
7. Step-in: who may step in, liability for past arrears (usually limited to amounts the lenders choose to pay), length of step-in, step-out on notice (Clause 28.2).
8. Novation: transferee criteria and the counterparty's consent standard; release of the original project company.
9. Termination compensation and its payment to the lenders (government counterparties, Ch 17).
10. Counterparty's own protections: information, no amendment without its consent, limits on lenders' liability.
11. Governing law and forum, aligned with the main contract (Ch 54).
12. Record each deviation from the lenders' standard position and its price in the term sheet negotiation log.

## 28.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 28.1, Contract gap scan** (original, named in standards Section 7; label `fw:contract-gap-scan`; home sec:28.5). Purpose: find every mismatch across a project's contract set before financial close. Steps: (1) Draw the contract map (sec:28.1). (2) Build the grid: contracts down, dimensions across (Exhibit 28.3). (3) Fill each cell from the documents, quoting clause references. (4) For each dimension, compare every contract with the offtake contract and the finance documents' tenor, and mark each mismatch. (5) Classify each mismatch: relief in time but not money; relief in one contract but breach in another; term shorter than debt or offtake; cap below exposure; termination without cure; disputes that cannot be joined. (6) Score severity and likelihood. (7) Assign a fix and its owner, or name the residual bearer. (8) Feed the log into the CP checklist, the model (cost or reserve) and the credit paper. Use it at term sheet, before signing, and on any amendment.
- **Framework 28.2, "Who pays if...?" trace** (original, named in standards Section 7; label `fw:who-pays-if`; home ssec:28.6.1). Purpose: follow one adverse event through every contract and policy to whoever finally bears the loss. Steps: (1) State the event precisely (cause, timing, duration, physical damage or not). (2) Quantify the gross loss by component (repair, lost revenue or deductions, extra cost, penalties payable). (3) Walk the contracts in order of response: the counterparty that caused it (indemnity, LDs, caps), the insurances (trigger, deductibles, limits, subrogation), the offtake contract (deemed payments, relief, penalties), the input contracts, the sponsor support documents. (4) Net each component through each layer. (5) Name the residual bearer and its capacity (equity distributions, reserves, debt). (6) Vary one link at a time to find the weakest. (7) Record the result in the gap log. Credit Ch 3's loss trace (Framework 3.1) as its retrospective counterpart. Demonstrated on Examples 28.3 and 28.4, on Lake Turkana (ssec:28.6.3) and in the Case P installment.

## 28.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: contract map current; direct agreement or acknowledgment for every material contract; cure periods against the lenders' decision timeline; step-in liability limited; novation criteria workable; government direct agreement routes compensation to lenders; gap scan grid complete with clause references; dispute forums joinable across interface contracts; trace run for the top five risks; gap log feeds CPs, model and credit paper.
- Red flags: a counterparty refusing any direct agreement; cure periods shorter than 30 days for non-payment defaults; step-in that makes lenders liable for all past arrears; relief that is time-only in one contract and time-and-money in its mirror; money relief "net of insurance" with no waiver of subrogation, which invites insurers to sue the offtaker; a license or lease that ends before the debt; dispute forums that cannot join the parties to an interface dispute; a residual bearer with no money.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: cure and step-in period norms only as dated indicative ranges (D-011), with the driver (counterparty type, asset criticality); Case P's and Case T's periods are case inputs, not norms.
- Common mistakes: scanning only commercial contracts and missing permits and leases; tracing a loss through the contracts but not the insurances, or the reverse; assuming back-to-back words produce back-to-back outcomes; treating a waiver of subrogation as harmless.
- Questions experts ask: Which contract moves first when something breaks? Who pays the first 45 days? If we stepped in tomorrow, what would we owe?

## 28.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are lenders' counsel on a 360 MW solar-plus-storage project in Bukhara Region, Uzbekistan (Illustrative; project company Bukhara Quyosh Energy LLC, fictional), two weeks before signing on October 28, 2026. The state-owned company that will operate the substation (a separate contract from the plant's O&M) refuses to sign a direct agreement; it offers a comfort letter and a 30-day cure period for non-payment defaults. The substation is a single point of failure on the contract map. Reasoning: what the lenders lose without a direct agreement (no notice, no cure, no step-in or novation); alternatives (ministry-level acknowledgment under the implementation agreement, a government direct agreement covering the substation contract, an obligation in the PPA that the offtaker procures substation performance, a deemed-energy remedy for substation failure); run the gap scan and a trace for a substation outage. Expert answer: sign only if the implementation agreement or PPA makes substation failure a government or offtaker risk event with deemed energy and the government direct agreement covers it; otherwise make the direct agreement a CP. What would change the answer: an investment-grade offtaker taking substation risk under the PPA (accept); a short tenor; a DFI or PRI cover that responds to non-performance of state entities.

| step-in | – | The lenders' right, through a nominee, to assume the project company's rights and obligations under a contract for a period. | 28.2.4 |
| step-in entity | – | The person the lenders appoint to exercise step-in rights. | 28.2.4 |
| step-out | – | The step-in entity's right to end its step-in on notice, without liability for later obligations. | 28.2.4 |
| novation | – | The replacement of one party to a contract by another, with the counterparty's consent, releasing the original party. | 28.2.5 |
| substitution | – | The transfer of the project company's contracts or shares to a new owner chosen by the lenders on enforcement. | 28.2.5 |
| contract gap scan | – | A structured comparison of every contract in a project across defined dimensions to find mismatches, score them and assign fixes. | 28.5 |
| gap log | – | The record of mismatches found by a gap scan, with severity, fix, owner and status. | 28.5.2 |
| "Who pays if...?" trace | – | A step-by-step following of one adverse event through every contract and insurance to the party that finally bears each part of the loss. | 28.6 |
