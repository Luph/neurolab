## 46.2 Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Equity is the residual claim on a project, and every decision an owner makes (whether to keep developing, what to pay for a stake, when to sell, whether a refinancing is worth it) turns on what that residual is worth at a given moment and to a given investor. Chapter 43 showed how the model computes returns; this chapter teaches what they mean and how to use them. The reader learns to state a return so it can be compared, to choose a hurdle rate that fits the project's stage and risk, to see where in the development cycle value is created and who captures it, and to value an operating project by discounting each kind of cash flow at the rate its risk deserves. The chapter ends with the reader able to read a valuation report and attack its weakest assumption.

What you will be able to do (printed as the `\section*{What you will be able to do}` list):

- Compute and reconcile project IRR, equity IRR, NPV, cash yield, and money multiple for one project, and say which metric answers which question (Capability 12).
- Set a hurdle rate for a project at any stage from development to operations, and defend each premium in it (Capabilities 12 and 15).
- Value a project at each development milestone and price a stake sold at financial close (Capability 12).
- Value an operating project by risk bucket and bridge enterprise value to equity value (Capabilities 12 and 4).

**Example 46.1 Five return metrics on one solar plant (Illustrative)** (`ex:46.1`, in 46.1.1, reused in 46.1.2 to 46.1.4)

- Scenario: 46.2 MWac solar plant in Jordan selling to the national utility under a 20-year USD-denominated PPA with a flat nominal tariff. Simplifications stated in the example: construction compressed to a single date (all capex at t = 0); annual periods; tax losses not arising; no reserves; no terminal value.
- Inputs: capex USD 41.7 million; year-1 revenue USD 6.38 million, falling 0.5% a year (module degradation, flat tariff); opex USD 0.93 million in year 1, rising 2.0% a year; corporate tax 20% (illustrative rate, not a statement of Jordanian law); straight-line tax depreciation over 20 years (USD 2.085 million a year); gearing 72%, so debt USD 30.0 million (30.024) and equity USD 11.7 million (11.676); debt is a 15-year annuity at 6.25%, debt service USD 3.142 million a year.
- Computed: year-by-year EBITDA, unlevered tax, project cash flow, interest, principal, levered tax, equity cash flow. Year-1 line: EBITDA 5.450; unlevered tax 0.673; interest 1.877; principal 1.266; levered tax 0.298; equity cash 2.010.
- Key results: project IRR (post-tax, unlevered) 8.77%; equity IRR 15.19%; project NPV at 8.0% USD 2.34 million; equity NPV at 11.0% USD 3.67 million; cash yield 17.2% (year 1), 16.7%, 16.2%, 15.7%, 15.2% (year 5), 35.5% (year 16, first year after the debt is repaid; equity cash 4.150); total distributions USD 43.93 million; MOIC 3.76x; equity payback 6.29 years; project payback 9.04 years.
- Teaching points: leverage raises IRR and cash yield but the multiple is dominated by the post-debt tail; payback ignores the tail entirely; NPV is the only metric that is a price.
- Equation 46.1 (`eq:46.1`): equity IRR defined as the rate $r_E$ solving $\sum_{t=0}^{T} (\mathrm{CF}^{E}_t)/(1+r_E)^t = 0$ with $\mathrm{CF}^{E}_t$ = distributions plus shareholder-loan interest and principal minus equity contributions; Excel `=IRR(J20:AD20)` on a Returns-sheet row (writer states layout).

**Example 46.2 Building a cost of equity for a contracted operating asset (Illustrative)** (`ex:46.2`, in 46.2.1)

- Scenario: an operating, contracted onshore wind portfolio in an investment-grade OECD market, valued in USD, nominal, post-tax.
- Inputs: risk-free rate 4.35% (illustrative long-dated government yield, not a quote); equity risk premium 5.50%; asset beta 0.35; debt beta 0.15; gearing 75% (D/E = 3.0).
- Computed: levered beta $\beta_E = \beta_A + (\beta_A - \beta_D) \times D/E = 0.35 + 0.20 \times 3 = 0.95$; cost of equity $= 4.35\% + 0.95 \times 5.50\% = 9.57\%$.

- Inputs: equity NPV at financial close at an 11.5% construction-stage hurdle $V_{\mathrm{FC}}$ = USD 28.7 million. Stage table:

|---|---|---|---|---|

## 46.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 46.1: The value staircase** (`fw:value-staircase`), original. Purpose: value a development project at any milestone and decide whether the next tranche of development spend is worth it. Steps: (1) list milestones from today to financial close; (2) estimate the equity NPV at financial close at the construction hurdle; (3) for each stage, estimate probability of reaching the next milestone, time, spend, and a stage hurdle that excludes failure risk (because the probability carries it); (4) roll back with Equation 46.2; (5) compare each value with the spend needed to reach it; (6) identify the step-up you are buying and who could buy it cheaper. Demonstrated in Example 46.3 and Exercise 46.12.
- **Framework 46.2: Risk-bucket valuation** (`fw:risk-bucket-valuation`), original (practice is widespread; the book's formulation is original). Purpose: value an asset whose cash flows carry different risks over time. Steps: (1) classify each year's cash flow into contracted, hedged, merchant (and storage merchant if relevant), using the definitions in Ch 20; (2) assign each bucket a rate consistent with the hurdle ladder; (3) discount each stream separately; (4) report value by bucket and the merchant share; (5) compute the implied single rate as a cross-check; (6) stress the merchant bucket's rate and cash flow separately. Demonstrated in Example 46.5 and the Case R installment.
- Established tools used (credited, not owned): CAPM (Sharpe 1964; owned by Ch 8); NPV and IRR (Ch 5).

## 46.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist:
- Every quoted return carries seven labels: metric, currency, nominal or real, pre- or post-tax, level (project, holdco, fund), gross or net of fees, and date.
- The valuation states EV or equity, includes or excludes shareholder loans, and states the life assumption.
- Each risk bucket has its own rate, and the merchant share of value is reported.
- The development valuation uses probabilities or stage premia for failure risk, not both.

Red flags:
- An equity IRR quoted without the gearing and debt terms behind it.
- A multiple-based valuation with no adjustment for remaining life.
- A single blended rate on an asset whose contract ends partway through its life.
- Distributions assumed during years when the lock-up test would fail on the lenders' case.
- Development value that rises with spend rather than with milestones.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- The PPA award or concession signature is usually the largest single step-up in development value (Example 46.3); it fails where the PPA is weak (sub-investment-grade offtaker without support) and the real step-up comes at financial close.
- Each 1 point on the discount rate moves the value of a flat 20-year contracted cash flow at 7% by about 7% to 8% (minus 7.32% for plus 1 point, plus 8.27% for minus 1 point); the effect grows with duration and shrinks for shorter lives (Example 46.5 moves less than 3% because its life is 12 years).
- Operating contracted equity trades near the operating rung of Exhibit 46.2; the rung moves with long-term rates and with the depth of the buyer pool.

Common mistakes:
- Discounting equity distributions at an unlevered rate, or project cash flows at a levered rate.
- Treating shareholder-loan interest as a cost rather than an equity return.
- Including DSRA cash in equity value at face when it will be released only at the final debt repayment.
- Comparing a nominal bid IRR with a real hurdle.

Questions experts ask:
- What is the value today if the project never refinances and never sells down?
- Which year's cash flow carries the most value, and which bucket is it in?
- At what discount rate does the deal stop working, and who in the market has a cost of capital below that?
- What did the last three comparable transactions imply, after adjusting for life and contract cover?

## 46.12 Judgment drill

The situation (second person, with numbers and deadline): You are the investment director of a developer that owns 100% of a permitted, grid-connected 180 MW wind project (the Example 46.3 project, now at the "permits and grid offer" milestone). A pension-backed fund offers USD 6.1 million today for 49% plus a pro rata share of future development spend, with your firm keeping development management. Your board meets Friday. Your stage valuation (Example 46.3) puts 100% at USD 4.42 million.

Reasoning outline: compute what 49% is worth on your numbers (USD 2.17 million); the offer is 2.8 times that, implying the fund either uses a lower stage hurdle, a higher probability of PPA award, or values portfolio access; test which assumption makes 49% worth USD 6.1 million; weigh what the fund's money buys (it funds 49% of the USD 3.4 million and USD 2.6 million future spend, cuts the developer's exposure to a lost tender) against what it costs (49% of the PPA step-up); consider governance (veto on bid tariff), the fund's own hurdle at financial close, and a tag or drag on any later sale. Expert answer: accept if the fund's price holds after the PPA award only through a deferred-consideration ratchet that keeps part of the upside, or counter at a smaller stake; do not sell 49% at a price that only reflects today's milestone.

What would change the answer: a tender with a low ceiling tariff (reduces the PPA step-up; selling now is better); a second strong project in the developer's pipeline needing the same cash (selling now is better); evidence that the fund will insist on a lower bid tariff to secure an award (selling is worse); a PPA probability well above 0.50 from a tender with few qualified bidders (selling is worse).

## 46.15 Glossary entries owned

| Term | Abbreviation | Definition | Home section |
|---|---|---|---|
| project IRR | – | The discount rate at which the present value of a project's unlevered cash flows (before any debt flows, stated pre- or post-tax) equals zero. | 46.1.1 |
| equity IRR | – | The discount rate at which the present value of all cash flows between the project company and its equity investors (share capital, shareholder loans, their interest and repayments, and dividends) equals zero. | 46.1.1 |
| hurdle rate | – | The minimum return an investor requires before committing capital to a given project at a given stage; the rate at which it discounts to set a price. | 46.1.2 |
| cash yield | – | Cash distributed to equity in a period divided by the equity invested, used by income investors as a measure of current return. | 46.1.3 |
| money multiple | MOIC | Total cash returned to equity divided by total equity invested, ignoring timing. | 46.1.4 |
| stage premium | – | The addition to an operating-asset hurdle that investors require for holding a project at an earlier stage of development or construction. | 46.2.2 |
| bid rate | – | The discount rate a sponsor uses to price a competitive bid, usually below the rate at which it would hold the asset, reflecting expected refinancing and sell-down gains. | 46.2.4 |
| value staircase | – | The book's framework for valuing a development project at each milestone by rolling back from financial close with probabilities, time, stage hurdles and remaining spend. | 46.3.2 |
| development value | – | The probability-weighted present value of a development project's equity at a milestone, net of the spend needed to reach financial close. | 46.3.2 |
| enterprise value | EV | The value of a project's unlevered cash flows to all capital providers, before deducting debt. | 46.4.1 |
| equity value | – | Enterprise value less net debt and other senior claims, or equivalently the present value of distributions to equity at a levered rate. | 46.4.1 |
| equity bridge | – | The reconciliation from enterprise value to equity value through debt, cash, reserves, hedges and shareholder loans. | 46.4.1 |

Purpose. Chapter 46 taught what an asset is worth to one investor. ## 47.5 Opening

Real deal moment from `indiana-toll-road`: in January 2006 a Cintra and Macquarie consortium won a 75-year lease of the 157-mile Indiana Toll Road with a bid of about USD 3.8 billion; it financed the bid with about USD 3.4 billion of debt on a nine-year interest-only bullet loan and an accreting swap whose pre-set rates rose to 11.3% by 2023; in September 2014 the concession company filed a prepackaged Chapter 11, and in 2015 IFM Investors bought the same lease for USD 5.725 billion. ## 47.6 Worked examples

**Example 47.1 Solving the bid tariff for a solar tender (Illustrative)** (`ex:47.1`, in 47.1.2)

- Scenario: 200 MWac solar PV plant bid into a government tender in Uzbekistan under a 25-year USD-denominated PPA with a flat nominal tariff; the tender evaluates the levelized tariff, which equals the flat tariff here.
- Inputs: P50 net capacity factor 27.4%, so P50 output 480.05 GWh in year 1; one-year P90 at 92.1% of P50; degradation 0.4% a year; capex USD 117.4 million (at t = 0; construction compressed, stated); opex USD 2.31 million in year 1, rising 2.0% a year; debt 18 years, all-in fixed rate 6.40%, sculpted to a DSCR of 1.30x on one-year P90 CFADS, capped at 80% gearing; tax holiday for the PPA term (simplification stated; tax enters in Exercise 47.9); no reserves; equity contributed at t = 0; target equity IRR on P50 10.0%.
- Computed: solve the tariff for the target equity IRR (Excel Goal Seek or a data table, cross-reference Ch 13); report debt, gearing, binding constraint, year-1 CFADS and debt service.
- Key results: tariff USD 29.46/MWh at 10.0%; debt USD 80.98 million (gearing 68.98%, DSCR constraint binds, gearing cap slack); equity USD 36.42 million; year-1 CFADS USD 11.83 million (P50) and USD 10.72 million (P90); year-1 debt service USD 8.24 million; year-1 P50 DSCR 1.44x. Tariff at 9.0% target: USD 28.75/MWh; at 11.0%: USD 30.11/MWh. Capex 5% lower: USD 28.29/MWh. Debt rate 5.90%: USD 28.88/MWh; 6.90%: USD 30.04/MWh.
- Equation 47.1 (`eq:47.1`): levelized tariff $= \sum_t \mathrm{Rev}_t \mathrm{DF}_t / \sum_t E_t \mathrm{DF}_t$ at the evaluation discount rate; Excel `=SUMPRODUCT(Rev,DF)/SUMPRODUCT(Energy,DF)` with stated rows.
- Teaching points: 1 point of equity IRR is worth about USD 0.65 to 0.71/MWh; 50 bps of debt rate is worth about USD 0.58/MWh; 5% of capex is worth USD 1.17/MWh. The bidder who wins by USD 0.70/MWh has either a 1-point lower hurdle or a cheaper loan.

**Example 47.2 How much the winner overestimates (Illustrative)** (`ex:47.2`, in 47.2.1)

- Scenario: N bidders independently estimate the traffic revenue of a toll road whose true value is common to all; each estimate is unbiased with a standard error of 15% of true value.
- Computed: expected maximum of N standard normal draws (writer shows the integral and gives the values; Ch 9 owns the normal distribution).
- Key results: expected bias of the highest estimate: 2 bidders 0.564 sigma (8.5%); 3 bidders 0.846 (12.7%); 4 bidders 1.029 (15.4%); 5 bidders 1.163 (17.4%); 8 bidders 1.424 (21.4%).
- Equation 47.2 (`eq:47.2`): $\mathrm{E}[\text{winner's error}] = \sigma \times \mathrm{E}[\max(Z_1,\ldots,Z_N)]$.
- Teaching points: the bias grows with the number of bidders and with forecast uncertainty; it is zero only if all bidders share the same error. Correction: shade the estimate by the expected bias for the expected field, not for the realized field.

**Example 47.3 The expected-value bid (Illustrative)** (`ex:47.3`, in 47.2.3)

- Scenario: the Example 47.1 bidder chooses its tariff knowing its win probability falls as its tariff rises.
- Inputs: breakeven tariff at the bidder's 10.0% hurdle on its own (shaded) case USD 36.85/MWh (a different, larger project than Example 47.1; stated); equity NPV gained per USD 1/MWh above breakeven USD 2.94 million; win probability $P(t) = 1/(1+e^{(t-39.00)/0.90})$ estimated from past tender results; sunk bid cost USD 1.7 million.
- Computed: expected NPV $= P(t) \times (t - 36.85) \times 2.94 - 1.7$ for tariffs USD 37.00 to 41.00/MWh.
- Key results (tariff: P(win), NPV if win, expected NPV, USD million): 37.00: 0.902, 0.44, (1.30); 37.50: 0.841, 1.91, (0.09); 38.00: 0.752, 3.38, 0.84; 38.50: 0.635, 4.85, 1.38; 39.00: 0.500, 6.32, 1.46; 39.50: 0.365, 7.79, 1.14; 40.00: 0.248, 9.26, 0.59; 40.50: 0.159, 10.73, 0.01; 41.00: 0.098, 12.20, (0.51). Optimum USD 38.83/MWh, expected NPV USD 1.48 million.
- Equation 47.3 (`eq:47.3`).
- Teaching points: bidding at breakeven to maximize the chance of winning destroys value in expectation; the win-probability curve is itself an estimate and the most uncertain input.

**Example 47.4 Locked box against completion accounts (Illustrative)** (`ex:47.4`, in 47.3.4)

- Scenario: sale of 100% of an operating 74.4 MW onshore wind farm in County Cork, Ireland, by a closed-end fund to an insurer. All figures in USD million for consistency with the chapter (state that a real Irish deal would be in EUR).
- Inputs: agreed enterprise value USD 186.3 million; locked-box date December 31; at that date debt USD 121.9 million and cash USD 9.2 million (net debt USD 112.7 million); completion 151 days later; ticker 7.0% a year on the locked-box equity price; a dividend of USD 3.4 million paid to the seller after the locked-box date (leakage, not permitted). Completion-accounts alternative: net debt at completion USD 110.9 million; working-capital target USD 2.6 million, actual USD 1.9 million.
- Key results: locked-box equity price USD 73.6 million; ticker USD 2.13 million; leakage deduction USD 3.4 million; price paid USD 72.33 million. Completion accounts price USD 186.3 − 110.9 − 0.7 = USD 74.70 million. Seller's total receipts including the dividend: locked box USD 75.73 million; completion accounts USD 78.10 million. Reconciliation: difference USD 2.37 million = cash generated after the locked-box date (USD 5.2 million: net debt fall of 1.8 plus the 3.4 dividend) minus ticker (2.13) minus working-capital shortfall (0.7).
- Equation 47.4 (`eq:47.4`): locked-box price $= (\mathrm{EV} - \mathrm{ND}_{\mathrm{LB}}) \times (1 + k \times d/365) - \text{leakage}$, with the ticker accrued on the equity price (convention stated; some deals accrue on EV or use a daily cash amount).
- Teaching points: the ticker is a negotiated proxy for the cash the business earns between the locked-box date and completion; buyers resist tickers above expected cash generation, sellers resist completion accounts because the buyer prepares them.

**Example 47.5 What a W&I policy pays (Illustrative)** (`ex:47.5`, in 47.3.5)

- Inputs: Example 47.4 deal; buy-side W&I limit 10% of EV (USD 18.63 million); retention 0.5% of EV (USD 0.93 million); premium 1.05% of limit (USD 0.196 million, one-off); a breach of the tax warranty discovered after completion costs the buyer USD 2.47 million.
- Key results: policy pays USD 1.54 million; buyer bears the retention USD 0.93 million unless the SPA makes the seller liable for the retention; a claim for a matter disclosed in the data room pays nothing.
- Teaching point: W&I lets a selling fund distribute proceeds without a long escrow; the buyer trades seller recourse for insurer recourse and inherits exclusions. Norms are indicative (D-011) pending fact sheet `t-ma-wi-insurance`.

**Example 47.6 Diversifying a portfolio's cash yield (Illustrative)** (`ex:47.6`, in 47.4.1)

- Inputs: three holdings: contracted solar (weight 45%, mean cash yield 7.2%, standard deviation 3.1%), merchant wind (30%, 10.6%, 12.4%), toll road (25%, 8.9%, 8.7%); correlations solar-wind 0.35, solar-road 0.05, wind-road 0.15.
- Key results: portfolio mean 8.65%; portfolio standard deviation 5.18% against a weighted average of 7.29%; alternative weights 25/50/25: mean 9.33%, standard deviation 7.17%.
- Teaching point: diversification lowers volatility only while correlations hold; in a scarcity event or a sovereign crisis correlations rise toward 1.

## 47.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 47.1: The bid discipline check** (`fw:bid-discipline-check`), original. Purpose: stop a bid team from winning by assumption. Steps: (1) restate the bid on the lenders' banking case, not the sponsor case; (2) compute the return with no refinancing gain and no terminal value beyond contract; (3) compare each key assumption with the independent advisor's and with the most conservative competitor you can infer; (4) apply the winner's-curse shading for the expected field (Example 47.2); (5) state the walk-away tariff and who approved it; (6) list what must be true for the bid to clear the hurdle, and assign each to an owner. Demonstrated on Example 47.1 in 47.2.4, used in the Case P and Case T installments.
- **Framework 47.2: Choosing the price mechanism** (`fw:pricing-mechanism-choice`), original. Purpose: choose locked box or completion accounts. Steps (decision questions): reliable audited or reviewed accounts at a recent date; stable, predictable cash generation; seller's need for price certainty and quick distribution; buyer's ability to police leakage; length of the gap to completion; working-capital volatility. Output: locked box, completion accounts, or a hybrid (locked box with a net-debt true-up).
- Established concepts credited: winner's curse (Capen, Clapp and Campbell 1971, oil lease auctions); common-value auctions.

## 47.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (bid approval):
- Bid tariff restated on the banking case with no refinancing gain.
- Walk-away price approved in writing before the final round.
- EPC price validity and committed-finance letter cover the bid validity period.
- Winner's-curse shading documented for the expected number of bidders.
- Every assumption above the independent advisor's case is listed with its owner.

Checklist (acquisition):
- Price mechanism chosen with Framework 47.2; leakage definition and permitted-leakage list agreed.
- W&I scope matched to the diligence actually performed; known issues covered by specific indemnity or price.
- Consents mapped: lenders, offtaker, government, co-shareholders' pre-emption.

Red flags:
- The bid works only if the debt is refinanced within five years.
- Your forecast is the highest you have seen for the asset.
- The ticker exceeds the cash the business generates.
- A data room that excludes lender correspondence or waiver letters.
- A fund near the end of its investment period with uncalled commitments.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- In a contracted renewables tender, 1 point of equity IRR moves the tariff by about 2% to 2.5% (Example 47.1); less where capex is a smaller share of tariff (gas plants with fuel pass-through).
- Buy-side W&I limits sit around 10% to 30% of EV (indicative, D-011, pending fact sheet).
- A preferred return of 8% is the common LP hurdle in closed-end infrastructure funds; core open-ended funds often have no carry or a lower one (indicative).

Common mistakes:
- Shading the bid for the curse after already using the lowest-cost assumptions everywhere else.
- Treating the bid bond as the cost of walking away from a won tender (the real cost is reputation and future qualification).
- Using completion accounts in a deal where the buyer cannot prepare reliable accounts at completion.
- Comparing fund gross IRRs with listed-vehicle total shareholder returns.

Questions experts ask:
- Who else is bidding, and what is their cost of capital?
- What assumption would have to be wrong for us to regret winning?
- What has the seller not put in the data room?
- How is the manager paid, and when does it need to sell?

## 47.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You lead the bid for a 300 MW solar tender (Illustrative, Middle East). Your committee-approved walk-away tariff is USD 17.40/MWh at a 9.0% equity IRR on the banking case with no refinancing. On the morning of the deadline the arranger offers a 22-year soft mini-perm that would let you bid USD 16.90/MWh at the same IRR if a refinancing at 50 bps lower margin happens in year 6. Your CEO wants to win; two rivals are known to use soft mini-perms. Bids close at 15:00.

Reasoning outline: quantify what the refinancing assumption is worth (USD 0.50/MWh, which on Example 47.1's sensitivity of about USD 0.70/MWh per point is roughly 0.7 points of equity IRR that must come from a refinancing nobody has committed to) and what happens if the refinancing does not occur (margin step-ups and cash sweeps of the soft mini-perm, taught in Ch 36 and Ch 37, push the IRR below the walk-away basis); compare with the winner's-curse bias for three bidders; check whether the committee approval covers a change in financing basis; consider the reputational cost of winning and failing to close against losing. Expert answer: do not bid below the approved walk-away without committee re-approval; if time allows, seek a revised approval explicitly for the mini-perm structure with a downside case; otherwise bid at the approved tariff.

What would change the answer: a refinancing market with deep, repeated evidence for the asset class (moves toward accepting); an offtaker that allows refinancing gains without sharing (moves toward accepting); a tender that penalizes failure to close with exclusion (moves against); a fund near the end of its investment period (pressure to accept, which the drill names as the bias to resist).

## 48.2 Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Every number in a project's base case comes from somebody's report: capex and schedule from the independent engineer, output from the yield consultant, reserves from the competent person, prices from the market advisor, traffic from the traffic consultant. Lenders size debt on these reports and investors bid on them, yet few people who rely on them know how to read them. This chapter opens Part IX by setting out how a diligence program is scoped and who relies on whom, then teaches the reader to read and challenge the technical, resource, market and traffic reports: what the advisor does, how to scope the work, how to read the report, and where reports have been wrong. The reader leaves able to direct these advisors and to say, with numbers, where a report's conclusion is weaker than it looks.

What you will be able to do:

- Scope a diligence program for a project financing, including reliance and timing, and challenge any advisor's report with a fixed protocol (Capability 7).
- Test an independent engineer's capex, contingency and schedule conclusions with simple probabilistic arithmetic (Capabilities 7, 2).
- Rework an energy yield, reserves, price-curve or traffic forecast for a changed assumption and show the effect on revenue and debt capacity (Capabilities 7, 5).
- Explain why traffic and demand forecasts in competitive bids are biased upward and how lenders protect themselves (Capabilities 7, 9, 15).

## 48.6 Worked examples

**Example 48.1 Is the contingency enough? (Illustrative)** (`ex:48.1`, in 48.2.3)

- Scenario: 96 MW run-of-river hydropower plant in Nepal with a 5.8 km headrace tunnel; sponsor budget USD 185.8 million (sum of most-likely estimates) plus contingency USD 11.7 million (total USD 197.5 million). The IE builds three-point estimates (USD million):

| Package | Low | Most likely | High | PERT mean | PERT sd |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Headworks and civil works | 58.2 | 63.9 | 79.4 | 65.53 | 3.53 |
| Headrace tunnel (5.8 km) | 41.6 | 47.3 | 71.8 | 50.43 | 5.03 |
| Powerhouse and E&M equipment | 44.7 | 46.1 | 50.3 | 46.57 | 0.93 |
| Transmission line (38 km, 132 kV) | 11.4 | 12.6 | 16.9 | 13.12 | 0.92 |
| Owner's costs and development | 14.8 | 15.9 | 18.2 | 16.10 | 0.57 |

- Equation 48.1 (`eq:48.1`): PERT mean $= (a + 4m + b)/6$, standard deviation $= (b - a)/6$; total standard deviation from the sum of variances (independent) or with a correlation term.
- Key results: expected cost USD 191.75 million; independent case: standard deviation USD 6.31 million, P80 USD 197.06 million, P90 USD 199.84 million; sponsor budget USD 197.5 million sits at about P82 (probability 0.819 of not exceeding); contingency needed for P80 USD 11.26 million. With a correlation of 0.6 between headworks and tunnel (same geology): standard deviation USD 7.82 million, P80 USD 198.33 million, probability of staying within budget 0.769.
- Teaching points: the most-likely sum understates expected cost by USD 5.95 million because the distributions are skewed; independence understates risk where packages share a cause; the IE's contingency opinion should state its confidence level and correlation assumption.

**Example 48.2 What a delay distribution costs (Illustrative)** (`ex:48.2`, in 48.2.4)

- Scenario: 300,000 m³/day reverse-osmosis desalination plant in Oman under construction; the IE assigns probabilities to delay bands beyond the guaranteed completion date.
- Inputs: delay bands and probabilities (band midpoint in days): 0 to 30 days, 0.42 (15); 31 to 90, 0.30 (60.5); 91 to 180, 0.16 (135.5); 181 to 270, 0.08 (225.5); more than 270, 0.04 (330 assumed). Cost to the project company of each day of delay USD 212,400 (interest, fixed costs, lost margin net of savings). EPC delay LDs USD 185,000 a day, capped at 120 days.
- Key results: probability of delay above 180 days 0.12 (about one in eight); expected delay 77.4 days; expected delay cost USD 16.43 million; expected LD recovery USD 10.74 million; expected uncovered cost USD 5.69 million; by band, the uncovered cost is USD 0.41 million (15 days), 1.66 (60.5), 6.58 (135.5), 25.70 (225.5), 47.89 (330).
- Teaching points: LDs cover the likely delays; the tail is uncovered and is what the lenders' contingency, DSU insurance (Ch 27) and sponsor support (Ch 26) must answer; the IE's probability estimate drives the size of each.

**Example 48.3 Challenging a wind yield report (Illustrative)** (`ex:48.3`, in 48.3.1)

- Scenario: 160 MW wind farm in Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil. Sponsor's yield consultant: gross output 612.4 GWh a year.
- Inputs: sponsor losses (multiplicative): wake 8.3%, availability 3.0%, electrical 2.1%, environmental 1.2%, curtailment 2.5%, performance 1.0%. Lenders' consultant revises wake to 10.6% and curtailment to 4.0%. Uncertainties (independent, % of net): wind measurement 4.5, long-term correction 3.0, vertical extrapolation 3.5, wake model 3.0, other 2.0, interannual variability 6.0 (one-year; divided by the square root of 10 for ten-year).
- Equation 48.2 (`eq:48.2`): net $= \text{gross} \times \prod_i (1 - L_i)$.
- Key results: sponsor net P50 508.58 GWh (total loss 16.95%); lenders' net P50 488.19 GWh (20.28%), 4.01% lower; combined uncertainty 9.51% one-year, 7.62% ten-year; P90 one-year: sponsor 446.57 GWh, lender 428.67; P90 ten-year: 458.89 and 440.50.
- Teaching points: two loss lines account for a 4% revenue cut that flows almost one for one into P90 debt capacity; ask for the wake model's validation against operating neighbors and for curtailment grounded in grid studies.

**Example 48.4 Reserve life against loan tenor (Illustrative)** (`ex:48.4`, in 48.3.3)

- Scenario: open-pit copper mine in southern Peru financed with a nine-year amortizing loan.
- Inputs: proved and probable ore reserves 186.4 Mt at 0.52% copper; mill throughput 14.0 Mtpa; metallurgical recovery 88.5%. Competent person's sensitivity: at a higher cut-off grade the reserve falls to 161.9 Mt.
- Equation 48.3 (`eq:48.3`): reserve tail $= (\text{reserve life} - \text{loan life})/\text{reserve life}$.
- Key results: reserve life 13.31 years; contained copper 0.969 Mt; recovered copper 857.8 kt, 64.43 kt a year; reserve tail at a 9-year final maturity 32.4% (at 10 years 24.9%, at 11 years 17.4%); revised reserve 161.9 Mt gives life 11.56 years and tail 22.2%.
- Teaching points: lenders require a tail (indicative minimum in the 25% to 35% range, D-011, to be confirmed in Ch 78 and its fact sheet) so that reserve downgrades and price falls do not leave the loan unpaid when the ore runs out; the tail is only as good as the cut-off price behind the reserve.

**Example 48.5 Two price curves for one merchant tail (Illustrative)** (`ex:48.5`, in 48.4.3)

- Scenario: 150 MW solar plant in Spain with a PPA to year 10 and merchant sales in years 11 to 25; P50 output 287.6 GWh a year (degradation ignored, stated).
- Inputs (EUR, nominal): Advisor A baseload price EUR 61.40/MWh in year 11 rising 1.5% a year, capture rate 0.78 falling 1.0 point a year to a floor of 0.62. Advisor B baseload EUR 54.70/MWh rising 1.0%, capture 0.71 falling 1.5 points a year to a floor of 0.55. Discount rate 9.0% (merchant bucket).
- Key results: year-11 merchant revenue EUR 13.77 million (A) and EUR 11.17 million (B); PV at today of years 11 to 25 merchant revenue EUR 47.23 million (A) and EUR 35.67 million (B), a difference of EUR 11.56 million (B is 24.5% lower).
- Teaching points: the capture-rate path does more work than the baseload level; ask each advisor for its build-out assumption for solar in the zone, which drives cannibalization.

**Example 48.6 Value of time and diversion (Illustrative)** (`ex:48.6`, in 48.5.2)

- Scenario: 31 km toll bypass around a growing town in Texas; for a typical car trip the bypass saves 14 minutes at a toll of USD 3.60.
- Inputs: binary logit diversion: share $= 1/(1 + e^{-\theta(\mathrm{VOT} \times \Delta t/60 - \text{toll})})$ with $\theta = 0.85$ per USD; sponsor's value of time USD 21.40 per hour; a revealed-preference survey finds 22% less, USD 17.54 (rounded to USD 17.50 in the example).
- Equation 48.4 (`eq:48.4`).
- Key results: value of time saved USD 4.99 (sponsor) and USD 4.08 (survey); generalized-cost advantage USD 1.39 and USD 0.48; diversion share 76.6% and 60.1%, a 21.5% fall in toll-road traffic from one parameter.
- Teaching points: the value of time is the single most sensitive and least observable input in a toll forecast; demand for a forecast with the higher value means demand for a higher bid; Case T installment links to the Case Bible's statement that Pellow used a value of time 22% above what a later survey found.

## 48.7 Walkthroughs

**Walkthrough: reading an independent engineer's report** (Section 48.6). Artifact: a generic IE report structure for a thermal or renewable project financing, page by page, with an Illustrative marked-up extract. Steps:

1. Scope and reliance page: who commissioned, who may rely, liability cap, information relied on and its dates.
2. Executive summary: separate findings from recommendations; list every "subject to" phrase.
3. Technology section: reference plants, fleet hours, any scale-up; equipment supplier's track record.
4. Site, permits and interfaces: grid, fuel, water, access; who carries interface risk.
5. EPC contract review: scope gaps, performance guarantees against the PPA (link to Ch 22), LD adequacy.
6. Schedule: critical path, float, delay probability; compare with Example 48.2's method.
7. Budget and contingency: method and confidence level (Example 48.1).
8. Operating assumptions: availability, degradation, opex, lifecycle against the model.
9. Model input reconciliation table: every technical input in the base case with the IE's view.
10. Reservations and outstanding items: what the IE could not verify.
11. Write the five questions to put to the IE before credit approval (model answers).

## 48.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 48.1: The diligence scope matrix** (`fw:dd-scope-matrix`), original. Purpose: plan every workstream of a financing's diligence. Rows: technical, resource, market, traffic or demand, legal and regulatory, insurance, model audit, tax and accounting, E&S, counterparty credit, KYC and integrity. Columns: who commissions; who relies and how; scope questions; key outputs that feed the model and documents; timing (bid, mandate, credit approval, signing, financial close, bring-down); cost and cap. Demonstrated in Exhibit 48.1; Ch 49 and Ch 50 cross-reference it.
- **Framework 48.2: The report challenge protocol** (`fw:report-challenge`), original. Purpose: challenge any advisor's report the same way. Steps: (1) scope and reliance: what was the advisor asked and who may rely; (2) independence: who paid and what else the advisor does for that client; (3) inputs: data sources, dates and gaps; (4) method: standard or bespoke, calibrated against what; (5) the three assumptions that move the answer most and the advisor's sensitivity on each; (6) comparison with an independent view or a reference class; (7) reservations: what the advisor says it did not check; (8) the downside the report supports, stated as a number. Applied throughout Ch 48 and in Ch 49 and Ch 50.
- Established methods credited: PERT three-point estimating (US Navy, 1958); four-step transport model (urban transport planning practice).

## 48.11 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (before credit approval):
- Every technical, resource, market and traffic input in the banking case traces to a named report, page and date.
- Each report carries a reliance letter to the lenders with a stated cap.
- The IE has reconciled the model's technical inputs (Walkthrough step 9).
- The yield, curve or traffic report gives the lenders' sizing case explicitly, not only P50.
- Bring-down letters are scheduled for financial close.

Red flags:
- A report whose scope letter excludes the issue you care about.
- A traffic forecast above every other forecast you can find.
- A capex estimate with contingency stated as a flat percentage and no confidence level.
- A yield report with curtailment below the grid operator's own study.
- A price curve older than the last major policy change in the market.
- An IE who has also acted as owner's engineer for the sponsor.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Most-likely capex sums understate expected cost when risks are skewed (Example 48.1); the gap grows with ground risk.
- Interannual variability dominates one-year P90 for wind; measurement and model uncertainty dominate ten-year P90.
- In user-pay toll roads, first-year traffic below forecast is the norm in published evidence (pending fact sheet `t-traffic-forecast-accuracy`); a lender's haircut on the sponsor case is market practice.

Common mistakes:
- Treating a reliance letter as a guarantee of accuracy.
- Accepting the sponsor's P50 and haircutting only P90.
- Using the market advisor's base case for merchant-tail sizing.
- Ignoring correlated risks in contingency analysis.

Questions experts ask:
- What would you have to see to change your conclusion?
- Which of your assumptions is furthest from the operating record of comparable assets?
- What did your firm forecast for the last three comparable projects, and what happened?
- What did you not have time to check?

## 48.12 Judgment drill

Situation: You are the credit officer at the lead arranger on the Example 48.3 wind farm. Credit committee is Thursday. The sponsor's consultant's P90 one-year is 446.6 GWh; your consultant's is 428.7 GWh. Sizing on yours cuts debt by USD 6.45 million on the modeling bank's simple sizing (Illustrative: tariff USD 48.30/MWh; debt sculpted to 1.25x on one-year P90 CFADS over 16 years at 7.20%, annuity factor 9.3227; the 17.90 GWh difference is worth USD 0.865 million of revenue a year). The sponsor says a rival bank will lend on their report.

Reasoning outline: identify which differences drive the gap (wake and curtailment); test each against evidence (neighbors' operating data, grid operator's curtailment study); apply Framework 48.2; consider splitting the difference only where evidence supports it; consider structural answers (size on the sponsor case with a cash sweep until two years of operating data confirm the yield, or a P90-based DSRA top-up). Expert answer: size on the lenders' consultant's curtailment, accept the sponsor's wake only if operating data from neighbors validate it, and offer a re-sizing mechanism after 24 months of data.

What would change the answer: curtailment backed by a firm grid reinforcement date (moves toward the sponsor); neighbors' data showing wake losses above 10% (moves toward the lenders' case); a market where lenders are competing hard for a small pipeline (pressure, not evidence; the drill names it).
