## 0. How this unit is organized

Part VII builds one workbook, the Case P reference model, from a blank file. Chapters 39 to 43 each add sheets and rows in a fixed order; Chapter 44 audits a seeded copy of the finished model; Chapter 45 adds sector modules on Cases T and R and on illustrative assets. Because the seven chapters share one workbook, this file opens with the **Part VII model build map** (Section 0.2): the sheet list, the row map of every sheet with the chapter that builds each block, the formula for the first period column in mathematical notation and in Excel, and the macros. Each chapter brief then cites the rows it builds. Writers must not re-specify rows another chapter builds; they cross-reference them.

### 0.1 Coordination with the modeler and the companion workbook

The modeler agent is building `model/case_p.py`, `model/Case_P_Model.xlsx` and the figure ledgers at the same time as this brief. When this brief was written, `book/model/` held only the three input files; no workbook, Python mirror or ledger existed. Therefore:

1. The row map below is the book's canonical layout. It follows the style sheet (Section 5.4) exactly: sheet order Cover, Inputs, Time, Construction, Operations, Tax, Funding, Debt, Reserves, Waterfall, Financials, Ratios, Returns, Checks, Outputs; column D label, E unit, F constant or scalar result, G row total or check, H and I blank, J the first model period; flags 1 or 0 with the `Flag_` prefix; no range names except `Scenario`; no OFFSET or INDIRECT; costs stored positive and subtracted explicitly.
2. The coordinator should send Section 0.2 to the modeler with the request that `build_excel_p.py` lay out the workbook on this map. If the generated workbook differs, the workbook wins for cell addresses: each writer re-reads the row numbers from `Case_P_Model.xlsx` before quoting a cell, and keeps the sheet names, block order and formulas of this brief. Every row-number difference is reported in the writer's status note.
3. Every Case P number printed in Chapters 39 to 44 is a figure-ledger value cited by ID. This brief never states a Case P model output. Where a chapter needs a figure that has no ID, it is listed in "Case Bible flaws and requests" at the end of this file with a proposed new ID (P-F37 onward, R-F11).
4. Where the Case Bible or the JSON leaves a modeling rule open (for example, how operating years map onto semiannual periods), this brief proposes the rule, marks it "proposed rule", and lists it for the modeler. If the modeler's report adopts a different rule, the writer teaches the modeler's rule.

Equation (39.1), the period end rule:
$$e_t = \begin{cases}\text{end of the month of } s_t & s_t < \text{COD}\\ \min\left(\text{end of June or December after } s_t,\ \text{PPA expiry}\right) & s_t \ge \text{COD}\end{cases}$$
Equation (39.2), operating year (proposed rule, flaw F-01): $\mathrm{OY}_t = \lceil \text{months since COD at } e_t / 12 \rceil$. In the FC base this makes OY1 the eight months May to December 2021 and OY$k$ the calendar year $2020+k$ for $k \ge 2$, so tax years, availability cycle years and maintenance years all align with fiscal years.

### 39.K Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: blueprint written and agreed; every input has a unit and a source; one formula per row (test: select row, Ctrl+\ finds no differences); timeline contiguous; flags sum to expected counts; master check visible on every sheet; version and change log on the Cover.
- Red flags: dates typed as values; TRUE/FALSE flags; OFFSET, INDIRECT or merged cells on calculation sheets; columns whose period length is not on a row; a model where changing COD moves revenue but not debt service; inputs on calculation sheets; the same input in two places.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "operating period equals debt service period" (breaks for monthly-paying bonds or quarterly-paying loans, where the operating period follows the debt); "monthly construction" (quarterly is acceptable when drawdowns are quarterly and IDC is immaterial); "one formula per row" (exceptions: the first column of a corkscrew, handled with N(I...) rather than a different formula).
- Common mistakes: using `>` where `>=` is needed; a stub period treated as a full half-year in a fixed-charge row; year fractions computed inside formulas; scenario logic written into calculation formulas instead of the live column.
- Questions experts ask: Which decision is this model for? What happens to every row if COD moves three months? Where is the day count? Show me the check that proves the timeline. Who changed this input and when?

### 40.H Frameworks

- **Framework 40.1 The circularity ladder** (`fw:circularity-ladder`, original). Purpose: choose the least fragile way to resolve a loop. Steps: (1) map the loop and confirm it is real (a value depends on itself through other cells); (2) if the loop sits inside one period and is linear, solve it algebraically in the period (gross-up); (3) if it spans periods but is linear in one or two scalars, solve for the scalars in closed form or paste them; (4) paste the fewest possible values, on the Inputs sheet, in input color, with a calculated twin and a residual check; (5) run a macro that copies, recalculates and stops on tolerance or a pass limit; (6) never leave iterative calculation switched on in a model others use; if an inherited model relies on it, add a circuit-breaker switch and a convergence check before relying on its outputs. When to use: every time a model shows a circular reference warning, or a number "moves" between recalculations. Demonstrated in Example 40.3 and Section 40.5.5.

### 40.K Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: budget lines sum to the contract budget; every timing rule an input; sources equal uses in every period; total gearing at or below the cap; ECA tranche within its cap; commitment fees stop when a tranche is fully drawn; IDC computed on opening balances; pasted values in input color with residual check; pass count logged.
- Red flags: IDC computed on closing balances (creates an in-period loop); a single blended rate for all tranches when margins differ; contingency modeled as unspent while the lenders size on full spend; ECA premium omitted from uses or from the capitalized cost; equity percentage applied to costs before financing instead of total uses; iterative calculation switched on with no convergence check.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "IDC is roughly half the construction period's interest on the final debt" (holds for a smooth S-curve and level rates; fails for a front-loaded profile with a large advance payment, such as a 10% advance plus development costs at close); "pro rata costs the sponsor less IRR than equity first" (true when the equity hurdle exceeds the debt cost, which it nearly always does).
- Common mistakes: treating the LNTP as a new cash use at close; charging commitment fees on the standby facility after it is cancelled at COD; forgetting that a swap's fixed leg and floating leg use different day counts; converging only on debt and not on total uses.
- Questions experts ask: What is the residual after the last converge pass? Which constraint binds? How much of the funding requirement is financing cost? What happens to IDC if notice to proceed slips three months? Where is the LNTP in sources and uses?

### 40.L Judgment drill

The situation: You are Kilnworth's financing manager in May 2018 (narrative frame without named-character dialogue). ### 41.K Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: every tariff line mapped (Framework 41.1); capacity uses contracted capacity, energy uses degraded capacity; pass-through test row zero apart from headroom; index reading dates printed on a row; local shares reconverted at the invoice rate; every cost has its own index and currency; tax depreciation base reconciles to capitalized cost; holiday bands by operating year; loss expiry tested; thin-cap ratio computed from balances, not typed; working capital unwinds at the end of the PPA.
- Red flags: an availability factor without a cap; fuel cost and fuel revenue computed from the same heat rate (hides headroom risk); one inflation index applied to all costs; KCR costs converted at a fixed rate for 25 years; tax holiday modeled as a lower rate on a positive result with no deferred depreciation logic; interest limitation ignored because "the loans are grandfathered" without a switch to test a refinancing.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "fuel pass-through makes fuel price irrelevant" (only while the offtaker pays; Chapter 59); "a holiday is worth the tax rate times the holiday profits" (wrong when depreciation is deferred or losses expire); "45 receivable days" (a contract term, not a forecast, in an emerging market).
- Common mistakes: applying the 2.3% part-load penalty to the contracted rate but not to the plant rate (or the reverse); escalating gas price by CPI instead of the GSA's fixed 2.0%; counting EOH per plant instead of per turbine; computing VAT refunds with a fixed nine-column offset that breaks at COD.
- Questions experts ask: Show me the pass-through test. What is the heat-rate headroom in OY10? Which costs are in KCR and at what rate are they converted? Does the holiday defer or destroy depreciation? What does the tax line do if the loans are refinanced in 2025?

### 41.L Judgment drill

The situation: You are Kilnworth's modeler in April 2018. Kessara's tax counsel writes that the deferred-depreciation rule for holiday years is "settled in the Code but contested in practice": the tax authority has twice challenged it for other investors, without a court ruling. The sponsors want it in the base case; the lenders' tax adviser wants it out of the banking case. The credit paper goes to committee in three weeks.
Reasoning it through: quantify both cases in the model (ledger P-F14 gives the base; the writer frames the comparison by method, not by invented numbers); identify who bears the risk (equity if debt is sized without it; both if with it); note that from OY9 the effect falls in years with the 30% rate and after the commercial tranche's sweep starts; consider the minimum turnover tax as a floor that narrows the difference; consider a tax ruling as a condition precedent. Expert answer: keep the deferred depreciation in the sponsor base case, exclude it from the lenders' banking and downside cases used for sizing, and seek an advance ruling; disclose the difference in the model's assumptions book.
What would change the answer: a written ruling (include it in all cases); a court decision against another investor (exclude from all cases); a sizing that is gearing-bound rather than DSCR-bound (the exclusion does not change debt, so the argument is about distributions only).

### 42.H Frameworks

- **Framework 42.1 The four-row waterfall tier** (`fw:waterfall-tier`, original). Purpose: build any priority of payments that cannot break under shortfall. Steps for each tier: (1) cash available (previous tier's remainder); (2) amount due, including arrears carried from the prior period; (3) amount paid as the MIN of available and due; (4) remainder and unpaid amount, the unpaid carried forward or flagged. Add a final check that cash in equals cash out plus closing balances. When to use: every waterfall, reserve and sweep; demonstrated on the Case P Waterfall.
- **Framework 42.2 The dividend trap test** (`fw:dividend-trap-test`, original). Purpose: decide at financial close whether a structure will trap cash. Steps: (1) project net income and retained earnings with book depreciation and all interest; (2) project cash available to shareholders after reserves and sweeps; (3) compute trapped cash each period as cash available less lawful payments (interest and principal on shareholder loans, dividends up to distributable reserves); (4) if trapped cash is material, test solutions in order of cost: change the equity split within the thin-cap limit, change book depreciation within accounting rules, plan a capital reduction, and accept the residual; (5) report the trapped cash and its release year in the model's outputs. Demonstrated in Example 42.8 and on Case P (P-F15, with and without shareholder loans).

### 42.K Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: CFADS definition matches the term sheet; sculpting uses the same cost factor as the interest rows; scheduled balance zero after maturity; every tranche corkscrew non-negative; DSRA target looks ahead one period and is funded at COD; MMRA window check; distribution flag is a product of conditions; shareholder payments in the documented order; balance and cash checks zero in every period; locked mode reproduces sizing mode on the base case.
- Red flags: sculpted principal negative in any period; DSRA target computed on the current period's debt service; a waterfall that pays dividends before testing reserves; a sweep that feeds back into sculpting; a balance sheet held together by a plug; sensitivities reported from sizing mode; distributions with negative retained earnings.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "sculpting makes the DSCR flat" (only in sizing mode and only on the sizing case); "the DSRA is six months of debt service" (it is the next period's scheduled debt service, which differs from the last period's when the profile is steep); "shareholder loans solve the dividend trap" (until thin cap, withholding or a lender's subordination terms bite).
- Common mistakes: discounting at a rate that excludes the swap or the gross-up; charging the stub period with principal; forgetting to stop the MMRA after the last overhaul; counting DSRA releases as CFADS; letting lock-up cash earn distributions in the same period it is locked.
- Questions experts ask: What is the binding constraint and by how much? Show me the scheduled balance at maturity. What does the model do if CFADS is negative? When does trapped cash first appear and when is it released? Does locked mode reproduce the base case to the cent?

### 43.H Frameworks

- **Framework 43.1 The locked-debt test protocol** (`fw:locked-debt-test-protocol`, original). Purpose: make every test answer the question the reader thinks it answers. ### 44.F Worked examples

- **Example 44.1 The missing availability cap (Illustrative).** A capacity payment written as charge times capacity times A/90% with no MIN. Over an eight-year availability cycle of 94.1, 93.0, 94.1, 91.2, 94.1, 93.0, 94.1, 89.4%, the capped factor averages 0.99917 and the uncapped 1.03194: capacity revenue overstated by 3.281%. On USD 58.3 million of half-year capacity revenue, the overstatement is USD 2.656 million in a 94.1% year and USD 1.913 million a half-year on average. Symptom in outputs: capacity revenue rising and falling with availability above target, which a contract-aware reader knows is impossible.
- **Example 44.2 A hard-coded dispatch factor (Illustrative).** The fuel-charge revenue row multiplies by 0.765 typed into the formula instead of the live dispatch cell. In the base case nothing is wrong. In a downside at 58% dispatch, the true fuel charge for the half-year is USD 40.125 million and the gas cost USD 39.630 million (from Example 41.3's base of 52.923 and 52.270 scaled by 58/76.5), but the model shows a fuel charge of 52.923 against a cost of 39.630: CFADS overstated by USD 12.798 million in every downside half-year. Symptom: a downside whose fuel margin is larger than the base case's.
- **Example 44.3 Analytical review of a DSCR profile (Illustrative).** A sculpted model whose DSCR is 1.35x in every period except a dip to 1.133x in one period: the profile tells the auditor that something in that year is outside the sculpting, here a major overhaul paid in opex while the sizing block used CFADS before maintenance. ### 44.H Frameworks

- **Framework 44.1 The seven-pass model review** (`fw:seven-pass-review`, original, organizing established practice): (1) scope and documents; (2) structure; (3) unique formulas; (4) inputs to sources; (5) analytical review of outputs; (6) reperformance by shadow model; (7) flex testing; then report. Each pass names the error families it is designed to catch (structure catches flow and banned functions; formulas catch logic, ranges and units; inputs catch omissions and transcription; analytics catch timing and shape; reperformance catches sizing logic; flex testing catches hard-codes and scenario leaks). When to use: any audit or review; the two-day review of 44.4 runs passes 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7 at reduced depth.
- **Framework 44.2 Finding severity grades** (`fw:finding-severity`, original). Grade A: changes a decision output beyond the agreed materiality in any case the lenders rely on (for illustration: senior debt by 0.5% or more, a minimum DSCR by 0.01x or more, equity IRR by 0.10 percentage points or more); Grade B: changes outputs below materiality, or beyond it only in cases not used for decisions; Grade C: no current effect but a defect that will produce errors when inputs change (fragility); Grade D: presentation or standards. The thresholds are illustrative and set in the scoping letter. When to use: every findings log.

### 44.K Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist (model-review checklist for back matter, compiled from here): scope and materiality agreed; document set complete; sheet map and flow reviewed; banned functions and external links searched; unique formulas listed and reviewed; every input traced to a source; master check and each check independently built; analytical review of revenue, opex, tax, CFADS and DSCR shapes; shadow sizing within tolerance; every switch and sensitivity flexed; findings graded and evidenced; re-audit of fixes.
- Red flags: checks built from the same totals they check; constants on calculation sheets; a scenario selector that copies blocks; outputs that do not move when an input that should move them is changed; a DSCR profile with an unexplained dip or spike; tax that is never positive in a profitable year; circular references with iterative calculation on.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "half of material findings are in inputs, not formulas" (an auditor's heuristic, not a statistic; useful for planning time, not for reporting); "if the shadow model agrees within 0.5% the sizing is right" (only if the shadow is independent of the model's inputs book errors).

- Questions experts ask: What did you not test? Which findings change the downside? Which checks are independent? What is the shadow model's difference and why? Who signs the opinion and on what version?

### 45.K Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: every revenue factor on its own row with its own scenario column; uncertainty components recombined and reconciled to the consultant's P-values; degradation compounding from first operation; curtailment and availability not double counted with the yield report; battery augmentation triggered by a floor and funded; traffic ramp-up explicit and separate from growth; class mix and trip length rows; mining payables with minimum deductions; LNG fixed and variable components separate; PPP deductions with caps and pass-down.
- Red flags: a P90 computed from a one-year σ and used as a ten-year case; portfolio P90 taken as the sum of P50s times one factor; capture ratio held flat for 20 years in a market adding the same technology; traffic that reaches maturity in year 1; a mine plan whose grade never declines; LNG revenue modeled at full lifting with no fixed-fee split; availability payments modeled with zero deductions.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "ten-year P90 sits between the one-year P90 and P50" (true when interannual variability is a material share of uncertainty; almost no gap when model uncertainty dominates); "ramp-up takes two to four years" (a range used in practice and worth stating only with a dated source from the requested fact sheet; Case T's took longer); "diversification is worth 1% to 3% of portfolio P90" (depends entirely on correlation; compute it).
- Common mistakes: applying degradation to the P-value factor rather than the energy; deducting curtailment twice; using hub prices for an asset settled at a node; forgetting moisture in concentrate freight; indexing a unitary charge fully when the contract indexes a share; passing PPP deductions to the operator without the operator's cap.
- Questions experts ask: Which losses are inside the P50? What correlation did you assume across the portfolio? What happens to revenue if capture falls at the low-case rate? What is the traffic in year 3 as a share of mature traffic, and on what evidence? What grade is in the last year of the debt? What does the buyer pay if it lifts nothing? What deductions did comparable PPPs actually suffer?

### 45.L Judgment drill

The situation: You are the lenders' modeler on an illustrative 240 MW onshore wind refinancing in 2026. The sponsor's model sizes on a one-year P90 built from a consultant's report whose interannual variability (one-year) is 6.0% and other uncertainty 4.5%; the sponsor wants the lenders to size on the ten-year P90 because "the debt lasts 15 years". The term sheet says "P90". The credit committee meets next week.
Reasoning it through: compute both P90s (one-year σ 7.50%, ten-year σ 4.88%; P90 factors 0.9039 and 0.9374 of P50); explain what each protects (a single bad year against the DSCR covenant versus the debt's life cash); note that DSCR covenants are tested every period, so a one-year bad year hits the lock-up and default tests regardless of the debt's tenor; consider a hybrid (sculpt on the ten-year P90 with a one-year P99 DSCR floor test, the structure Case R's facilities use at 1.00x and 1.05x); check the DSRA's ability to cover the one-year shortfall. Expert answer: size on the one-year P90 for the covenant-relevant DSCR, or accept the ten-year P90 for sculpting only with a one-year P99 floor and a larger DSRA, and make the term sheet say which P90.
What would change the answer: a long operating history (variability observed, not modeled); a merchant tail (size more conservatively); a portfolio with low correlation (the one-year portfolio P90 rises toward the ten-year figure).
