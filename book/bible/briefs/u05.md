## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A private project company cannot finance an asset that the state can take, tax away, regulate out of business or simply stop paying for. The concession, the implementation agreement and the instruments of government support are how a host government turns those powers into promises that lenders can price. This chapter teaches what those documents grant, which government risks they allocate and how, what the state pays on early termination and why each party fights over the formula, and how to tell a guarantee that pays from a letter that only comforts. It equips the reader to read a government agreement clause by clause and to say, for any adverse government act, whether the project company is protected, by what instrument, for how much, and from whose budget.

What you will be able to do (for the chapter's `\section*`):
- Read a concession or implementation agreement and map each government risk to the clause and party that bears it (Capability 3).
- Calculate termination compensation under debt-based, equity-based and market-value regimes, and test a sovereign guarantee cap against it over the project's life (Capabilities 3 and 8).
- Rank government support instruments by legal strength and say what each is worth to lenders (Capability 8).
- Negotiate the termination schedule and support package from the sponsor's, the lenders' and the government's chair (Capabilities 6 and 9).

## 6. Worked examples

The plant of all four examples, the walkthrough and Exercises 17.6 and 17.13 (Illustrative): Ankobra Power Ltd, a fictional project company owning a 320 MW open-cycle gas plant near Takoradi, Ghana, financed in March 2018, at COD in October 2020, selling under a 25-year PPA to a state-owned utility, with an implementation agreement and a sovereign guarantee from the finance ministry. Every term, act and figure about Ankobra is hypothetical; the writer makes no statement about Ghana's actual PPAs, levies or guarantees.

**Example 17.1 A new levy and the change-in-law threshold** (`ex:17.1`, Illustrative). Ankobra's annual non-fuel revenue is USD 96.8 million. Suppose a finance act of January 2024 introduces a 1.2% levy on the gross revenue of all electricity generators. The IA passes through change in law above a threshold of USD 0.5 million a year, and the clause covers only changes specific to the power sector (variant b of Clause 17.3) or all changes (variant a). Compute: levy USD 1.162 million a year; above the threshold, so recoverable in full under a "whole amount once threshold exceeded" drafting, or USD 0.662 million under a "deductible" drafting; tariff adjustment to restore the position under whole-amount drafting = USD 0.302/kW-month (1.162 million divided by 320,000 kW times 12). Teaching point: the threshold drafting (franchise versus deductible) and the scope (specific versus general) decide the answer; under government-friendly variant c (discriminatory changes only) the levy, which hits all generators, is not compensable at all.

**Example 17.2 Restoring economic equilibrium by term extension or by tariff** (`ex:17.2`, Illustrative). Ankobra again, but suppose for this example that its IA is a civil-law administrative contract with an economic equilibrium clause (the variant the writer uses to show the civil-law remedy; Ghana is a common-law country, so the narration says the clause is drafted in, not implied by law). Suppose a 2027 regulation lowers the project company's net cash flow by USD 2.9 million a year for the remaining 18 years. Discount rate agreed in the IA 9.0%. Compute PV of the loss: USD 25.39 million. Remedy 1: term extension, with net cash flow in the extension years (after debt is repaid) of USD 38.6 million a year: cumulative PV of extension cash flows after 3 years is USD 20.71 million and after 4 years USD 26.51 million, so a four-year extension slightly over-compensates and the parties settle on extension plus a small payment, or a pro-rated final year. Remedy 2: tariff uplift for the remaining 18 years of USD 0.755/kW-month (2.9 million divided by 3.84 million kW-months a year). Teaching point: lenders prefer the tariff uplift because a term extension pays after their debt is gone; governments prefer extension because it costs nothing in their budget horizon; sponsors look at the discount rate.

**Example 17.3 Termination compensation for a 320 MW gas-fired IPP under three termination grounds** (`ex:17.3`, Illustrative). Scenario: Ankobra's IA is terminated in September 2023, 5.5 years after its March 2018 financial close (about three years after COD). Inputs: senior debt outstanding USD 187.4 million; swap breakage payable by the project company USD 6.3 million; equity contributed USD 78.6 million at financial close; contractual equity rate 15.0%. Two scenarios. Scenario A (performing): distributions received to date USD 12.7 million; projected remaining distributions 17 annual amounts starting at USD 24.6 million and growing 2.0% a year. Scenario B (underperforming): distributions received USD 4.1 million; projected remaining distributions 17 annual amounts starting at USD 9.6 million growing 2.0%. Compute under: (i) government default: senior debt plus swap breakage plus the equity amount, the equity amount being the greater of the NPV of remaining distributions at 15.0% and equity contributed compounded at 15.0% for 5.5 years less distributions received; (ii) natural force majeure: senior debt plus equity contributed less distributions received; (iii) project company default: 85% of senior debt. Expected results (USD million): equity contributed compounded = 169.5. Scenario A: NPV of distributions 164.6; compounded less distributions 156.8; government-default amount 187.4 + 6.3 + 164.6 = 358.3; natural FM 253.3; project company default 159.3. ## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 17.1 Termination compensation matrix** (`fw:termination-compensation-matrix`, original). Purpose: classify any termination regime and spot where the lenders or the state are unprotected. Steps: (1) list grounds down the rows (government default, political FM, natural FM, project company default, voluntary or public-interest, expiry); (2) across the columns record what each ground pays to senior debt, hedges, subordinated debt, equity; (3) mark each cell as full, partial (with the percentage) or nil; (4) read the diagonal of incentives: no ground should pay equity more than performance would have earned, and no ground short of project company default should leave senior debt unpaid; (5) record who pays (offtaker, government, guarantor, insurer) and from which budget. Used in 17.4.2, the walkthrough, Case P and Exercise 17.12.
- **Framework 17.2 Support instrument strength test** (`fw:support-strength-test`, original). Purpose: decide what an instrument of government support is worth to lenders. Five questions, each answered yes or no with evidence: (1) Does it create a payment obligation, or only an obligation to use efforts or to consult? (2) Is payment due on demand, or only after remedies against the primary obligor are exhausted? (3) Is the issuer authorized and is the amount within a legislated ceiling and budget? (4) Can it be enforced: governing law, forum, waiver of immunity (forward to Chapter 54)? (5) Is there a backstop if the issuer does not pay (DFI guarantee, political risk insurance; forward to Chapters 34 and 60)? Score: five yeses is a guarantee lenders credit in sizing; three or fewer is comfort. Applied in 17.5.4.
- Established tools credited in prose only: the "no better and no worse" compensation standard (common-law PPP practice); economic equilibrium (French administrative law tradition). No framework box for either.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: grant and term run from COD with a long-stop; competing-facility protection where revenue depends on demand; IA and PPA termination grounds mirror; convertibility and transfer undertaking covers debt service, O&M, and distributions; change-in-law scope and threshold drafting; political versus natural FM split; termination amount covers senior debt at every date; protected-debt definition; payment currency, timing and interest; guarantee scope covers both payment obligations and termination amounts; guarantee cap tested against the exposure profile; immunity waiver and forum; budget authorization evidenced by a legal opinion and the Finance Act ceiling.
- Red flags: guarantee payable only after exhaustion of remedies against the utility; termination amount payable "subject to budgetary appropriation"; change-in-law clause limited to discriminatory changes in an emerging market; a cure-period structure that lets the government terminate before lenders can step in; a guarantee cap set as a round number without a profile; a letter of support described in the term sheet as a guarantee.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: in emerging-market IPPs closed 2015–2025 (indicative, D-011), lenders expect project company default compensation of at least most of senior debt (often 80% to 100%); limits: OECD user-pay concessions use market-value retender regimes without a debt floor (Case T), and a debt floor is worth only as much as the payer. Equity compensation on government default at the bid IRR is common; limit: a compounded-equity floor rewards poor performance (Example 17.3).
- Common mistakes: treating "government support" as a guarantee; forgetting that termination compensation is often payable in local currency; reading the IA without the PPA; assuming the guarantee covers swap breakage; ignoring that a term extension remedy pays after the debt is gone.
- Questions experts ask: Who pays the termination amount, from which budget line, in which currency, and how fast? What happens to the protected debt if we refinance? Which termination grounds pay equity, and is any ground better for equity than performing? Has the guarantee ever been called in this country, and was it paid?

## 12. Judgment drill

- Situation: You are the mandated lead arranger (Halvorsen Bank, fictional) on Mbeya Sun Storage Ltd's 260 MW solar-plus-storage IPP in Tanzania, September 2026 (Illustrative; every government position below is hypothetical). The finance ministry, now under an agreed ceiling on new guarantees, withdraws the draft sovereign guarantee and offers instead: a letter of support from the minister; a put option under which the government buys the plant on termination for 90% of senior debt, payable in four semiannual installments; and acceptance of a USD 57.5 million DFI partial risk guarantee covering the government's put payment. Senior debt is USD 284.5 million. Your credit committee meets in nine days.
- Reasoning outline: apply Framework 17.2 to each instrument; compute the uncovered exposure (90% of 284.5 = USD 256.05 million, of which 57.5 is covered by the PRG; USD 198.55 million relies on the government); value the installment delay (two years of payment risk and interest); weigh the ceiling as a sign of fiscal discipline (which improves the payer's ability to honor the put) against the softer form; consider asking for 100% debt coverage on government default and political FM, accelerated installments if the PRG is drawn, a local-currency risk solution, political risk insurance on the put (forward to Chapter 60). The expert answer: accept the put-plus-PRG structure if the put covers 100% of senior debt on government default and political FM, the installments carry interest at the loan rate, and the PRG is sized to the first two installments; treat the letter of support as having no sizing value.
- What would change the answer: the country's record of honoring guarantees (a recent default moves the answer to "decline"); whether the offtaker is investment grade (reduces reliance on the put); a DFI A/B loan in the structure (preferred creditor status changes the payment priority; forward to Chapter 29); currency of the put payment (local currency without a convertibility undertaking moves toward decline).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. For a contracted power plant the PPA is the revenue line in the model and the reason lenders lend. This chapter builds the PPA tariff from first principles: which costs the capacity payment covers and which the energy payment covers, how availability, dispatch, heat rate, fuel and indexation turn each cost into a matched tariff line, and where the gaps open (unindexed fuel, grid delays, curtailment, a part-load heat rate no one priced). It then teaches deemed energy, curtailment compensation and take-or-pay as the clauses that move volume risk to the offtaker, sketches how the project company actually gets paid, and contrasts the tolling agreement, in which the offtaker supplies the fuel. The reader leaves able to compute any month's invoice and to mark up the clauses that decide whether it gets paid.

## 6. Worked examples

The plant of Examples 18.1 to 18.3, 18.5 and 18.6, the walkthrough and Exercises 18.7, 18.9, 18.12 and 18.14 (Illustrative): Phu Tan Power Company (fictional), a 412.5 MW CCGT in Long An province, Vietnam, selling under a hypothetical 25-year USD-denominated PPA to the state-owned utility; the month is March 2025 (31 days). The writer makes no statement about Vietnam's actual PPA terms or tariffs.

**Example 18.1 Capacity payment at three availability levels** (`ex:18.1`, Illustrative). Phu Tan, March 2025: capacity payment rate USD 11.85/kW-month; indexation factor for the month 1.0734; target availability 90.0%; no bonus above target; one month. Formula eq:18.1: $\mathrm{CP}_t = C \times 1{,}000 \times \mathrm{cpr} \times \mathrm{IF}_t \times \min(1, A_t/A^{*}) \times m_t$ with $m_t = 1$. Results: availability 87.3% gives USD 5.0895 million; 90.0% gives USD 5.2469 million; 94.2% gives USD 5.2469 million (no bonus). Teaching points: the 2.7-point shortfall costs USD 0.157 million that month; above target there is no reward, so the O&M incentive design (Chapter 24) has to create one if the offtaker wants it. Excel: on the Revenue sheet, with contracted capacity in F5, the capacity payment rate in F6, the target availability in F7, the month's indexation factor in row 12 and availability in row 14, the capacity payment in J16 is `=$F$5*1000*$F$6*J12*MIN(1,J14/$F$7)` (no `%` in the formula, so `\xl{}` is allowed).

**Example 18.2 Fuel charge, heat-rate headroom, and the project company's fuel margin** (`ex:18.2`, Illustrative). Phu Tan, March 2025, dispatched at full load all month: net energy 214,600 MWh; contracted heat rate 6,410 kJ/kWh (LHV); actual heat rate 6,352 kJ/kWh; gas price USD 6.42/MMBtu on GCV; HHV/LHV ratio 1.108; 1 MMBtu = 1,055,056 kJ. eq:18.2: $\mathrm{EP}^{\mathrm{fuel}}_t = E^{\mathrm{del}}_t \times \mathrm{HR}^{\mathrm{c}} \times 1.108 \times p^{\mathrm{fuel}}_t / 1{,}055.06$. Results: gas paid for by the offtaker 1,444,615 MMBtu, fuel charge USD 9.2744 million; gas actually burned 1,431,543 MMBtu, cost USD 9.1905 million; fuel margin USD 0.0839 million. If the plant runs 1% worse than the contracted heat rate (6,474.1 kJ/kWh), the project company loses USD 0.0927 million that month. Teaching point: headroom is a small, recurring margin that turns into a loss with modest degradation; the degradation allowance in the contracted heat rate is what keeps it positive. eq:18.2 is the canonical form with the factor (style sheet Addendum A.5); the chapter explains when the factor applies (gas priced on GCV, heat rate on LHV).

**Example 18.3 Part-load dispatch and who pays for it** (`ex:18.3`, Illustrative). Phu Tan in a low-demand month, July 2025, dispatched mostly at 60% load: net energy 96,400 MWh. Part-load curve: +4.2% at 75% load, +11.8% at 50% load; linear interpolation at 60% gives +8.76%, so the contracted heat rate at 60% is 6,971.5 kJ/kWh. Fuel charge with the part-load curve USD 4.5311 million; at the full-load contracted heat rate USD 4.1661 million; difference USD 0.365 million. Teaching point: without a part-load curve in the PPA, the project company pays for the offtaker's dispatch choice; with it, the offtaker pays and therefore has an incentive to dispatch efficiently.

**Example 18.4 Deemed energy for a wind farm whose grid connection is late** (`ex:18.4`, Illustrative). Kisaki Wind Ltd (fictional), a 150 MW wind farm in Singida region, Tanzania, under a hypothetical PPA with the state utility; the transmission line the utility must build, due June 1, 2024, is 273 days late; P50 net capacity factor 38.2%; tariff USD 61.40/MWh; senior debt USD 172.6 million at 6.85%. Deemed energy = 150 × 24 × 273 × 0.382 = 375,430 MWh; deemed energy payment USD 23.051 million; interest for the period USD 8.843 million. Variants shown: (a) deemed at P50 from the scheduled connection date (sponsor-friendly); (b) deemed at P50 with measured turbine availability and a cap of 12 months (lender-friendly shape); (c) deemed at P90 from 60 days after the scheduled date, paid by tariff extension at the end of the term (offtaker-friendly), with P90 fixed at 91.0% of P50 and a 60-day deferral, giving 213 days × 150 × 24 × 0.382 × 0.910 = 266,555.0 MWh, payment USD 16.366 million (verified). Teaching point: the measurement basis, the start date and the payment route matter as much as the principle.

**Example 18.5 Sizing an offtaker letter of credit** (`ex:18.5`, Illustrative). Phu Tan, LC to be delivered before COD: one month's capacity payment at target availability USD 5.2469 million; one month's energy payment USD 10.008 million (fuel charge 9.2744 plus VOM at USD 3.42/MWh on 214,600 MWh = 0.7339). LC sizes: two months of capacity plus one month of energy USD 20.50 million; three months of capacity plus one month of energy USD 25.75 million; three months of full billing USD 45.77 million. Teaching point: the formula decides how long the project company can pay its own costs and debt when the offtaker stops paying; the energy payment is mostly fuel that the project company stops buying if it stops running, which is why lenders accept a smaller energy component.

**Example 18.6 PPA with fuel pass-through or tolling agreement** (`ex:18.6`, Illustrative). Phu Tan, calendar year 2025, compared with a hypothetical tolling agreement under which the state utility's gas affiliate supplies the fuel: net energy 2,850,000 MWh; actual heat rate 6,352 kJ/kWh; gas USD 6.42/MMBtu (GCV). Annual fuel cost USD 122.05 million. A 30% gas price rise adds USD 36.62 million: under the PPA, passed to the offtaker through the fuel charge but financed by the project company for the payment lag; under tolling, borne by the toller from the start. Working capital: if the offtaker pays 45 days after the month in which the project company pays for gas, the project company finances about 1.5 months of fuel, USD 15.26 million at base gas prices, and USD 19.83 million after the 30% rise (verify: 122.05 × 1.5 / 12 = 15.26; × 1.3 = 19.83). Teaching point: tolling removes fuel price and working capital from the project company's balance sheet, at the cost of depending on the toller's fuel supply and credit.

**Example 18.7 What an unindexed fuel charge cost Mundra** (`ex:18.7`, Real case: Coastal Gujarat Power, Mundra, 2006–2017). From the mundra fact sheet: levelized fuel charge INR 0.86/kWh in the bid, of which INR 0.49 non-escalable and INR 0.37 escalable; coal assumed at an average USD 43/t in the bid; benchmark USD 74/t in June 2012. Simplifying assumption, stated: fuel cost per kWh moves in proportion to the coal price and the escalable part tracks it fully. Shortfall on the non-escalable part = 0.49 × (74/43 − 1) = INR 0.353/kWh. Compare with ADB's reported per-unit operating profit of about INR 0.20/kWh in FY2013 to FY2019 against INR 0.73/kWh in the base case, and ADB's estimate that about INR 0.60/kWh more would have made the plant viable. Teaching point: an indexation choice made to win a bid is a risk allocation; the bankable fix is a pass-through with heat-rate and procurement tests, as CERC observed in 2013 (fact sheet "What went wrong").

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 18.1 Tariff-to-cost match test** (`fw:tariff-to-cost-match`, original). Purpose: find every cost the tariff does not recover. Steps: (1) list each project cost line (debt service, equity return, fixed O&M foreign and local, insurance, fuel, fuel transport, VOM, taxes, major maintenance); (2) for each, record the tariff component that pays it; (3) check currency match (cost currency against tariff denomination and payment currency); (4) check indexation match (cost driver against the index applied, including lags); (5) check volume match (is the cost fixed when the payment is volumetric, or the reverse?); (6) record the residual mismatch and who bears it. Used in 18.1.1, 18.8 (Mundra fails at steps 3 and 4) and Case P (18.11).
- **Framework 18.2 Volume risk allocation checklist** (`fw:volume-risk-checklist`, original). Purpose: decide who bears lost output for each cause. Rows: resource shortfall, plant unavailability, grid unavailability before COD, grid unavailability after COD, economic curtailment, system-security curtailment, fuel supply failure (private supplier), fuel transport failure (state transporter), offtaker breach, force majeure (natural, political). Columns: who bears it under a sponsor-friendly PPA, a typical bankable PPA, an offtaker-friendly PPA; the clause that does it (availability, deemed availability, deemed energy, curtailment compensation, FM). Used in 18.4 and the judgment drill.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: tariff-to-cost match done for every cost line; contracted capacity re-rating mechanics; availability target, outage allowances and treatment of offtaker risk events; part-load curve; degradation allowance; fuel pass-through with transport and take-or-pay; change-in-law threshold; deemed energy start date, measurement, cap; curtailment allowance; indexation lags; payment currency and conversion rate source; LC size, replenishment, cure; disputed-amount rule; termination grounds mirror the IA.
- Red flags: a non-escalable component covering a commodity cost; availability measured on demonstrated output without excluding grid events; deemed energy payable only by term extension; LC replenishment curable by a payment plan; a single conversion-rate source controlled by the offtaker; a PPA that excludes fuel price changes from force majeure without a pass-through.
- Rules of thumb and their limits (indicative, D-011; no fact sheet verifies thermal IPP terms, see Open requests): payment security of two to three months of capacity billing is common in sub-Saharan and South Asian IPPs closed 2015 to 2025; limit: the size matters less than the replenishment and backstop. Heat-rate headroom of about 1% over the EPC guarantee is a typical sponsor ask; limit: it vanishes with degradation unless an allowance is indexed to operating hours.
- Common mistakes: modeling the capacity payment on nameplate rather than contracted, tested capacity; forgetting the HHV/LHV factor; indexing the capital charge to CPI when the debt is fixed in USD; treating deemed energy as certain cash.
- Questions experts ask: Which costs does this tariff not pay? Who decides dispatch, and who pays for the inefficiency? If the grid is late, what exactly is deemed, from when, and who funds it? What does the offtaker pay when it cannot pay?

## 12. Judgment drill

- Situation: You are the head of origination of Meghna Engines Power Ltd (fictional sponsor), developing a 236 MW gas engine plant near Narayanganj, Bangladesh, November 2026 (Illustrative; the utility's position is hypothetical). The state-owned offtaker offers to drop its demand for a heat-rate cap if you accept that the fuel charge passes through actual gas costs only up to 112% of a published regional gas index; above that, the project company pays. Your gas supply agreement is priced at a fixed slope to oil. The plant burns 13.6 million MMBtu a year. Board approval is due Friday.
- Reasoning outline: map the proposal with Framework 18.1 (indexation mismatch between an oil-linked GSA and a gas-index cap); quantify exposure on three illustrative years (USD/MMBtu): regional index 9.80, 11.40, 12.65, so the cap is 10.976, 12.768, 14.168; oil-linked GSA price 10.70, 13.10, 15.20; unrecovered fuel cost 0, 0.332 and 1.032 per MMBtu, or USD 0, 4.515 and 14.035 million a year; weigh the value of dropping the heat-rate cap (efficiency upside) against the uncapped commodity exposure; consider counter-proposals: cap tied to the GSA formula, a re-opener, a collar; consult lenders, who will size on the downside. The expert answer: decline as offered; accept a cap tied to the GSA's own formula with a heat-rate test, because a cap on an index you do not buy against is the Mundra position in a new form.
- What would change the answer: a GSA repriced to the same index (removes the basis); an investment-grade utility willing to add a re-opener; a sponsor with a natural hedge at project level (not group level; Mundra lesson).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Where no creditworthy utility will sign a 25-year PPA, states still want low-carbon plants, interconnectors, nuclear stations and big network assets built with private money. They support them through schemes rather than offtake contracts: a feed-in tariff set by statute or regulation, a two-sided contract for difference with a government-owned counterparty, a cap-and-floor regime, or a regulated asset base that pays a return on capital from the first day of construction. This chapter teaches how each scheme pays, which of price, volume, shape, construction, inflation and policy risk it leaves with investors, and what that does to debt capacity and the cost of capital. Its spine is the contrast the real cases make vivid: Spain's statutory tariffs changed by later statutes; Hinkley Point C's CfD left an overrun of roughly double the real cost with its sponsor; Sizewell C and Tideway were paid during construction and shared overruns with consumers and the state.

What you will be able to do:
- Compute payments under a feed-in tariff, a two-sided CfD (including negative-price rules), a cap-and-floor regime and a RAB allowed-revenue formula (Capability 4).
- Map which risks each support scheme leaves with equity and lenders, and explain the effect on debt capacity and the cost of capital (Capabilities 1 and 5).
- Test how durable a support promise is, by its legal form, counterparty and funding source (Capabilities 8 and 15).
- Advise a government on which scheme fits a given asset (Capability 9).

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 19.1 Support scheme risk map** (`fw:support-scheme-risk-map`, original). Purpose: compare support schemes on the risks they remove. Steps: (1) rows: price level, price shape, basis, volume, construction cost and delay, inflation, interest rate during construction, policy change, counterparty; (2) columns: the schemes under review; (3) mark each cell investor, consumer or state, with the clause or rule that does it; (4) add a row for when payment starts (on output, on availability, on spend); (5) read across: the scheme that moves construction risk and pays during construction lowers the cost of capital most, at the cost of putting consumers or taxpayers on the hook. - **Framework 19.2 Statute-or-contract durability test** (`fw:support-durability-test`, original). Purpose: estimate how likely a support promise is to survive a fiscal squeeze. (3) What funds payments: the budget, a levy on suppliers, network charges? (4) What does the document say about future changes (grandfathering, change-in-law compensation)? (5) Which forum can the investor use, and is there treaty protection (forward to Chapter 54)? (6) What is the fiscal pressure on the scheme (deficit, cost growth, political salience)? ## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: legal form of the support; counterparty and funding; strike price base and indexation; reference price definition; negative-price rule; milestone, target commencement window and long-stop; change-in-law protection; term and start date; RAB: allowed WACC, indexation, thresholds, disallowance rules, reopeners, support package instruments and triggers; cap-and-floor assessment period.
- Red flags: support in a regulation with no grandfathering; a CfD whose long-stop falls inside the sponsor's realistic schedule; a negative-price rule that removes payments for more hours than the model assumes; a RAB whose thresholds were set before the design was mature; a counterparty funded from the annual budget.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: CfD-backed renewables carry lower cover factors than merchant (indicative; t-market-norms gives US contracted versus merchant ranges for 2024–2026, while European CfD norms are unverified and must not be stated as fact); limit: residual shape and basis risk differ by technology and market. RAB financing at investment-grade gearing near regulatory caps (Tideway 70% covenant; Sizewell C 65% of RAB); limit: these are single-asset regimes with state support packages.
- Common mistakes: comparing strike prices across rounds without the price base; treating a FiT as a contract; ignoring that a CfD pays nothing until generation; forgetting that a RAB return is real and indexed, not a nominal equity IRR.
- Questions experts ask: What is the counterparty's money, and can the government change the levy? What happens to the CfD if the plant is a year late? Who decides what spend enters the RAB? What has this government changed retroactively before?

## 12. Judgment drill

- Situation: You advise Portugal's finance ministry in October 2026 on a hypothetical 1.4 GW pumped-storage project in the Douro basin with a target price of EUR 2,740 million (Illustrative; the bidders and the fiscal rule are hypothetical). Two bidders: Serra Hydro Partners asks for a 25-year cap-and-floor with the floor at 80% of its debt service, which implies a real cost of capital of 6.70%; Tâmega Energia Nova offers to build under a RAB with a real allowed WACC of 5.10%, 1.6 percentage points lower, but asks for a contingent support package covering overruns above 35% of the target price (that is, spend above EUR 3,699 million) and uninsurable losses. The minister wants a recommendation for cabinet in a week, and the ministry's fiscal rules count contingent liabilities above EUR 1.0 billion against a ceiling.
- Reasoning outline: map both offers with Framework 19.1; value the WACC difference on the project's capital: the level real annual capital charge over a 40-year life is EUR 161.9 million at 5.10% against EUR 198.4 million at 6.70%, a saving of EUR 36.5 million a year (2,740 × the 40-year annuity factor at each rate); quantify the contingent liability under the RAB package (the overrun band above 35%) against the fiscal ceiling; weigh consumer exposure during construction; consider hybrid designs (cap-and-floor with a construction-phase return, or RAB with a lower overrun threshold and a cap on state exposure). Expert answer: the RAB, if the ministry caps the contingent package below the fiscal ceiling and requires independent assessment of spend, because the WACC saving compounds over 40 years; otherwise the cap-and-floor.
- What would change the answer: design maturity (an immature design favors cap-and-floor, which leaves construction risk with investors); the government's record on regulatory resets; a market price outlook that makes the floor likely to bind (raising the cap-and-floor's real cost).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. In energy-only markets such as ERCOT, and for corporate buyers in Europe, Australia and Latin America, there is no utility PPA and often no support scheme. Projects build revenue certainty themselves, contract by contract: a corporate PPA, a fixed-volume or fixed-shape swap with a bank, a proxy revenue swap with an insurer-style provider, a floor under a battery's merchant revenue, a toll. Each instrument fixes some risks and leaves others, and the risks it leaves (shape, basis, volume in scarcity) are the ones that bankrupt projects. This chapter teaches the reader to decompose any merchant revenue line into price level, shape, basis and volume, to compute what each hedge pays in normal hours and in a Uri-type event, and to draft and negotiate the clauses that decide who pays when output fails exactly when prices spike.

What you will be able to do:
- Settle a virtual PPA, a fixed-volume swap, a fixed-shape hedge, a proxy revenue swap and a revenue floor for any hour or period (Capability 4).
- Decompose a project's revenue into contracted, hedged and merchant buckets and identify the residual price, shape, basis and volume exposure of each (Capabilities 2 and 5).
- Stress a hedge book for a correlated scarcity event and say what collateral and drafting would have limited the loss (Capabilities 11 and 15).
- Negotiate volume, force majeure and credit support terms of a hedge from the sponsor's, the lenders' and the hedge provider's chair (Capability 6).

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 20.1 Four-risk decomposition of merchant revenue** (`fw:four-risk-decomposition`, original). Purpose: name what a hedge fixes and what it leaves. Steps: (1) write revenue as volume × capture price at node; (2) split the price into hub price level × shape factor (capture ratio) − basis (hub minus node); (3) for each candidate instrument, mark which of price level, shape, basis and volume it transfers; (4) compute the residual for normal conditions and for a scarcity hour; (5) choose the combination that leaves the residual where the project can bear it. Used in 20.1.1, the walkthrough, the judgment drill and Case R.
- **Framework 20.2 Scarcity stress for a hedge book** (`fw:scarcity-stress`, original). Purpose: test a hedge book against a correlated tail event. Steps: (1) define the event: duration, hub and node price, output by asset (use the joint behavior: low output when prices are high); (2) settle every hedge under the event; (3) add physical sales; (4) compare the net loss with liquidity (cash, DSRA, LC capacity, holdco support); (5) test drafting levers: force majeure, unit contingency, price caps in settlement, volume caps; (6) report the loss as a share of annual hedge revenue and of liquidity. Used in 20.8 and Case R (R-F03).
- Established concepts credited in prose: capture price and cannibalization (from Chapter 11); the as-generated, baseload and shaped product terms used by power markets.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: settlement point (hub or node) against delivery point; quantity type (firm, unit-contingent, as-produced, proxy); shape definition; force majeure and its effect on settlement; price and volume caps; negative price treatment; certificates and their delivery; credit support amount, form and threshold; first-lien sharing with lenders; termination events and close-out; buyer's guarantee; tenor against debt tenor.
- Red flags: a firm-volume swap on an intermittent asset with no price cap; settlement at a hub far from the node in a congested system; a hedge provider's right to call cash collateral; a corporate buyer with no rating and no parent guarantee; a battery revenue case built on a year of high ancillary prices.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: hedge no more than the P99 one-year volume as firm quantity in scarcity-prone markets (indicative; limit: P99 is about annual energy, not about the hours that matter, so stress the hours, Framework 20.2). US lenders in 2024–2026 sized contracted revenue near 1.25x to 1.40x and merchant near 1.75x to 2.0x (t-market-norms, US, indicative; forward to Chapter 36).
- Common mistakes: counting a vPPA as fixed revenue without basis; treating a fixed-shape hedge as an as-produced PPA; ignoring the collateral a hedge can demand in a price spike; double-counting a battery's capacity and ancillary revenue for the same megawatts.
- Questions experts ask: What's the shape? Where does it settle? What does the book pay in the worst 72 hours of the last decade? Who posts what when prices move against us, and do we have the LC capacity?

## 12. Judgment drill

- Situation: You are the credit officer of Ridgeline Capital Bank (fictional) on a USD 307.4 million term loan to Caprock Renewables' 400 MW wind and 200 MWac solar portfolio in ERCOT, December 2026 (Illustrative). The sponsor proposes replacing a 10-year as-produced vPPA on the wind (strike USD 28.10/MWh) with a 10-year 7x24 fixed-volume swap of 120 MW at USD 41.30/MWh from a bank, arguing the higher price adds USD 9.8 million a year of revenue and raises distributions. The swap has no price cap; the bank wants first-lien status. Credit committee meets on Thursday.
- Reasoning outline: apply Framework 20.1 (the swap fixes a higher price but adds shape and volume risk the vPPA did not carry); run Framework 20.2 with a Uri-type event (writer computes, for example, 72 hours at USD 5,000/MWh with output at 15%: settlement on 120 MW is (41.30 − 5,000) × 120 × 72 = −USD 42.843 million before physical sales; verified); compare with the added annual revenue; consider counter-proposals: unit-contingent volume, a settlement price cap, a smaller block sized to P99 hourly output in winter peaks, an as-produced structure at a higher price, a liquidity facility. Expert answer: approve only with a settlement price cap or unit-contingent drafting and a liquidity reserve sized to the stress; otherwise keep the vPPA.
- What would change the answer: a portfolio whose winter output is reliable (for example, coastal wind with low icing exposure); a holdco willing to guarantee settlements; a market rule change that lowers the price cap (the PUCT's cut of ERCOT's high offer cap from USD 9,000 to USD 5,000/MWh effective January 1, 2022 shows such changes happen; t-electricity-market-design); a hedge provider whose own credit is correlated with the scarcity event (wrong-way risk, ssec:14.20.2).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Outside power, the revenue contracts that carry project debt look different on the surface (an LNG SPA priced at 115% of Henry Hub plus a fee, a pipeline's reservation charge, a copper concentrate contract full of payables and penalties, a gold stream, a tunnel's availability payment, a toll schedule, an airport's per-passenger charge, a hyperscaler's lease) but they answer one question: how much of the revenue is owed whether or not the asset is used, and by whom? This chapter teaches each contract family's structure and key clauses, computes what each pays, and trains the reader to apply one test, the fixed-fee test, to any revenue contract in any sector. Chapter 12 explained how these assets work; this chapter explains how they get paid; the sector chapters (75 to 82) apply both.

What you will be able to do:
- Compute revenue under an LNG SPA with a fixed fee, a ship-or-pay transportation agreement, a concentrate offtake contract, a stream, an availability payment with deductions, a toll schedule, a regulated airport charge and a data-center lease (Capability 4).
- Classify any revenue contract by usage risk, price risk, counterparty and term, and identify the share of revenue that is fixed regardless of throughput (Capabilities 3 and 13).
- Negotiate the clauses that decide whether a fee survives a downturn: cancellation, ship-or-pay, toll escalation, deductions and rent commencement (Capability 6).

**21.11 Walkthrough: applying the fixed-fee test to a port terminal term sheet.** Artifact: an illustrative term sheet (Exhibit 21.4) dated March 2026 for a hypothetical 25-year concession of a new container terminal at Tema, Ghana, bid by Volta Gateway Terminals Ltd (fictional; no statement about the actual Tema concessions): capacity 900,000 TEU a year; fixed annual concession fee to the port authority USD 7.85 million; variable fee USD 14.20 per TEU; minimum annual guarantee of 520,000 TEU, a shortfall being paid at the variable fee (Clause 21.8 is the drafted form); a 10-year throughput agreement with a fictional shipping alliance guaranteeing 410,000 TEU a year, with a shortfall payment of USD 38.50 per TEU; terminal handling charges capped at USD 168 per TEU, indexed to US CPI. 1. List every revenue line and every fixed payment out: base-case handling revenue 655,000 × 168 = 110.04; payments to the authority 7.85 + 655,000 × 14.20 = 17.15.
2. Apply Framework 21.1 to each revenue line: fixed or usage-linked, from whom, for how long, what cancels it. Only the alliance's shortfall payment is owed regardless of use: 410,000 × 38.50 = 15.785 in a year with no volume, for 10 years.
3. 4. Check the shipping alliance's credit and the shortfall payment's enforceability (alliances reshuffle).
5. Check tariff caps and their indexation against costs.
6. Classify the contract in Framework 21.2 and state the share of revenue a lender would treat as contracted: 15.785 of 110.04, or 14.3%, for 10 of 25 years.
7. List three term-sheet changes the lenders would request and the authority's likely answer.

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 21.1 Fixed-fee test** (`fw:fixed-fee-test`, original). Purpose: find the part of a revenue contract that pays regardless of use. Questions: (1) Which payments are owed whether or not the asset is used? (2) Who owes them, and what is their credit, with support? (3) For how long, and when do they start? (4) What cancels or reduces them (cancellation rights, force majeure, availability deductions, termination rights)? (5) What is the variable part and which market risk does it carry? (6) What share of debt service do the fixed payments cover in a year with no use? Used throughout and in 21.11.
- **Framework 21.2 Revenue contract classification grid** (`fw:revenue-contract-grid`, original). Purpose: compare revenue contracts across sectors on four axes: usage risk (none, partial, full), price risk (none, pass-through, full), counterparty (sovereign, investment grade, sub-investment grade, many users), term relative to debt tenor. Steps: place the contract, then name the residual risk the project and lenders carry. Used in 21.1.2, 21.11 and Exercise 21.12.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: fixed versus variable revenue share; cancellation and suspension rights; take-or-pay or ship-or-pay percentage; counterparty credit and support; term against debt tenor; indexation of fees; force majeure effect on fixed fees; for mining: payables, minimum deductions, TC/RC basis, penalties, quotational period, assay and umpire; for streams: delivery obligation, completion test, intercreditor; for availability: deduction weighting, caps, ratchets; for tolls: maximum tolls, escalation, discounting freedom, leakage, enforcement; for data centers: rent commencement trigger, power delivery responsibility, SLA credits, renewal and RVG.
- Red flags: a fee cancellable without payment; ship-or-pay from an unrated shipper with no support; TC/RCs reopened annually with no floor in a contract the model treats as fixed; a stream senior to the lenders on deliveries; an availability regime with uncapped deductions; toll escalation set in a statute the next government can amend; rent commencement tied to the landlord delivering power the utility controls.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: lenders treat fixed fees from investment-grade buyers as contracted revenue and size near contracted levels (indicative; t-market-norms shows US LNG term debt at roughly 52% to two-thirds of capex in 2023, which reflects construction scale and refinancing structure as much as the fee); limit: fee quality depends on the cancellation terms and the buyer.
- Common mistakes: treating an offtake contract as price protection; ignoring moisture and minimum deductions; reading an availability payment's maximum as the expected payment; treating a renewable data-center lease as a 20-year contract.
- Questions experts ask: In a year when nobody uses the asset, what is still paid, and by whom? What cancels the fee? Who decides the toll? What does the stream take before the lenders?

## 12. Judgment drill

- Situation: You are the lead arranger (Halvorsen Bank, fictional), January 2027, on a USD 2,386 million financing of Bayou Teche LNG (fictional), a two-train 5.0 mtpa export project in Cameron Parish, Louisiana (Illustrative). - Reasoning outline: Framework 21.1 on each SPA; the affiliate SPA's fee is only as good as a BB parent; compute the contracted share (3.6 / 5.0 = 72.0%) against the share counted if the affiliate is included; consider sizing the debt only on the third-party fees, a cash sweep from affiliate revenues, or a requirement to replace the affiliate SPA with a third-party SPA by a date; compare with Sabine Pass's practice of reaching a contracted threshold before FID (fact sheet teaching angle). # Chapter 22. The EPC contract

## 1. Header

- Chapter number: 22
- Title: The EPC contract
- File: `chapters/22-the-epc-contract.tex`
- Target length: 13,500 words (core technical chapter)
- Dominant expert lens: construction and project finance lawyer who has negotiated EPC contracts for lenders and sponsors, with the independent engineer and the EPC project director as counter-chairs

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every revenue contract in Chapters 18 to 21 pays only once the asset exists, at the promised capacity, by the promised date. The EPC contract is how the project company buys that certainty from a contractor: one party, one price, one date, one set of performance guarantees, backed by liquidated damages, caps, bonds, retention and a parent guarantee. This chapter teaches what each promise transfers and what it leaves behind; how delay and performance LDs are calibrated to the debt, the fixed costs and the offtake contract; how extension-of-time and concurrent-delay rules decide who pays for a late plant; how the security package is sized against a contractor failure; and why a fixed price is worth only as much as the contractor's balance sheet (Vogtle, Carillion). The reader leaves able to calibrate and mark up an EPC risk package from every chair.

What you will be able to do:
- Calibrate delay and performance liquidated damages and test the caps against the delay and shortfall the independent engineer thinks plausible (Capabilities 4 and 7).
- Read an EPC contract and identify every risk left with the owner, including interface, site and employer-risk events (Capability 3).
- Size the security package against a contractor failure and decide when a parent guarantee or joint and several liability is needed (Capabilities 5 and 10).
- Negotiate delay LDs, liability caps and extension-of-time clauses from the sponsor's, the lenders' and the contractor's chair (Capability 6).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 22.1 Calibrating the delay LD rate** (`ex:22.1`, Illustrative). 342 MW CCGT in a Southeast Asian IPP program; EPC price USD 318.6 million; senior debt at scheduled COD USD 276.4 million at an all-in 7.35%; first principal installment of 5.2% of debt due six months after scheduled COD; fixed O&M, insurance and owner's costs during delay USD 18,900/day; PPA delay LDs payable to the offtaker USD 45,000/day; equity distributions expected at USD 24.8 million a year. Daily layers: interest USD 55,659; plus fixed costs and PPA LDs gives the lenders' floor USD 119,559; plus principal falling due USD 78,755 gives USD 198,314; plus lost distributions USD 67,945 gives USD 266,259. The negotiated rate is USD 132,500/day (0.0416% of the price per day): above the floor, below full debt service. Teaching points: what the rate covers is a negotiated choice; principal can be rescheduled if the loan repayment dates run from actual COD rather than calendar dates, which is why lenders and the EPC negotiation interact; equity's lost return is the sponsor's ask and the first thing traded. Exhibit 22.2 (the calibration stack).

**Example 22.2 How many days does the cap cover?** (`ex:22.2`, Illustrative). Same plant: delay LD cap 15% of the price, USD 47.79 million, covers 360.7 days at USD 132,500/day. The independent engineer's plausible delay is 210 days: LDs collected USD 27.825 million against the lenders' floor cost of USD 25.107 million and USD 41.646 million including principal. Teaching point: the cap covers the plausible delay at the floor; the principal gap needs repayment dates linked to COD, DSU insurance (Chapter 27) or sponsor support (Chapter 26).

**Example 22.3 Pricing performance LDs as the NPV of lost margin** (`ex:22.3`, Illustrative). Same plant: capacity charge USD 10.40/kW-month for 20 remaining years, discount rate 9.5%: PV per kW of contracted capacity lost USD 1,099.79, set at USD 1,100/kW. Heat rate: annual energy 2,180,000 MWh, gas USD 7.10/MMBtu on GCV, HHV/LHV 1.108; each 1 kJ/kWh above the PPA's contracted heat rate costs USD 16,255 a year (the PPA pays only the contracted heat rate), PV USD 143,243, set at USD 143,000 per kJ/kWh. At the test the plant is 7.2 MW short and 31 kJ/kWh over: output LDs USD 7.92 million; heat-rate LDs USD 4.433 million. Teaching point: performance LDs are a capital payment that buys down the debt or compensates equity for a permanent loss; they must be applied in the waterfall (Case P applied them to prepay senior debt, a Chapter 61 fact referenced only as a pointer here).

**Example 22.4 Extension of time under three concurrency rules** (`ex:22.4`, Illustrative). Same project; three critical-path delay events: A, owner's late site access, 23 days; B, contractor's late turbine delivery, 37 days; C, the grid operator's late backfeed (owner risk under the EPC), 28 days, overlapping B by 15 days. Total critical delay 73 days (23 + 37 + 28 − 15). Rule 1, time for every employer delay including concurrent periods, no money for the concurrent period: extension 51 days; LD days 22; LDs USD 2.915 million. Rule 2, no extension where contractor delay is concurrent: extension 36 days; LD days 37; LDs USD 4.9025 million. Rule 3, the overlap shared equally: extension 43.5 days; LD days 29.5; LDs USD 3.909 million. Prolongation costs for non-concurrent employer delay (36 days at USD 61,200/day): USD 2.2032 million. Teaching point: the concurrency rule moves several million dollars on one project; the lenders' interest is that the owner's relief under the PPA matches the time it grants under the EPC.

**Example 22.5 What the security package covers when the contractor fails** (`ex:22.5`, Illustrative). Same contract: performance bond 10% (USD 31.86 million); advance payment 10% recovered pro rata from progress payments; retention 5%. The contractor becomes insolvent at 62% complete. Work done USD 197.53 million; retention held USD 9.88 million; unrecovered advance USD 12.11 million; paid to date USD 199.76 million. Remaining contract value USD 121.07 million; a replacement contractor prices completion at 1.45 times that, USD 175.55 million, an extra USD 54.48 million; 240 days of further delay at the lenders' floor of USD 119,559/day cost USD 28.69 million. Exposure USD 95.28 million (extra cost, delay, unrecovered advance); security (performance bond, advance payment guarantee at its reduced amount, retention held) USD 53.84 million; gap USD 41.44 million for the parent company guarantee, sponsor support or a standby facility. Teaching point: bonds sized at 10% cover a contractor failure only when combined with a solvent parent; this is Carillion's lesson for thin-equity project companies.

## 7. Walkthrough

**22.11 Walkthrough: calibrating and marking up an EPC liability package.** Artifact: an illustrative EPC term sheet extract (Exhibit 22.4) with delay LD rate and cap, performance guarantees and LD rates, caps, security package, EOT and concurrency clause, defects period. Steps:
1. Build the delay LD calibration stack (Framework 22.1) from the financing term sheet and the PPA.
2. Compare the cap with the independent engineer's P90 delay and the DSU indemnity period.
3. Compute performance LD rates as NPV of lost margin and compare with the contractor's offer.
4. Check minimum performance levels against the PPA's contracted capacity reset rules.
5. Read the EOT clause for concurrency and notice time bars; test it on a three-event scenario (Example 22.4 method).
6. Map the security package with Framework 22.2 and test it against a failure at 50% and 75% complete.
7. Check carve-outs from the overall cap and the consequential-loss exclusion (LDs must sit outside it).
8. Check that accrued LDs survive termination and that post-termination losses are recoverable (Triple Point, section 22.10).
9. Draft the lenders' issues list: five points, each with reason and fallback.

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 22.1 Delay LD calibration stack** (`fw:delay-ld-calibration`, original). Purpose: set and defend a delay LD rate and cap. Steps: (1) layer daily costs of delay from the bottom: interest on debt outstanding at scheduled COD; fixed operating and owner's costs; LDs owed under the offtake contract; principal falling due; equity's lost return; (2) mark where the contractor's offer sits; (3) set the cap by multiplying the agreed rate by the delay the independent engineer considers plausible, then compare with market caps (indicative ranges with drivers); (4) fill any gap above the floor with DSU insurance, repayment dates linked to COD, or sponsor support; (5) check the result against the penalty rule (Chapter 10): each layer is a loss the owner would suffer. Used in 22.3, the walkthrough and Case P (P-F33).
- **Framework 22.2 EPC security stack** (`fw:epc-security-stack`, original). Purpose: test whether the security package survives a contractor failure. Steps: (1) list each instrument with amount, form (on demand or conditional), issuer and expiry: performance bond, advance payment guarantee, retention or retention bond, warranty bond, parent guarantee, joint and several co-obligors; (2) compute exposure at failure at two or three completion points (Example 22.5 method); (3) add the parent's realistic capacity (its credit, its other fixed-price exposures); (4) state the gap and who fills it. Used in 22.6.2, 22.6.3, the walkthrough and the judgment drill.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: scope and fitness for purpose; employer risk events list matches PPA relief events; guaranteed completion date and long-stop; delay LD rate calibrated and cap tested; performance guarantees with correction curves and minimum levels; performance and aggregate LD caps; overall cap and carve-outs; consequential-loss exclusion carves out LDs; EOT and concurrency; notice time bars; variation thresholds requiring lender consent; security package amounts, forms, issuers' ratings, expiry beyond defects period; parent guarantee scope; joint and several liability; defects and serial defects; LD survival on termination; insurance responsibilities; assignment to lenders and direct agreement.
- Red flags: LDs stated as the "sole and exclusive remedy" with no termination right for prolonged delay; a cap below the IE's plausible delay at the lenders' floor; concurrency drafted so that any contractor delay removes all EOT, inviting disputes, or the reverse; bonds from banks below the lenders' rating threshold; a parent guarantee capped below the overall liability cap; a contractor with a large book of loss-making fixed-price contracts; a consortium with several, not joint and several, liability.
- Rules of thumb and their limits (indicative, D-011, thermal and renewables EPC in emerging-market project finance 2015–2025): delay LD caps of 15% to 25% of price, performance LD caps of 10% to 15%, aggregate LD caps of 25% to 35%, overall caps near 100% of price, performance bonds near 10%; limits: caps move with the contractor market (fewer bidders, lower caps), technology maturity and the contractor's balance sheet; Case P's 18%, 12%, 30% and 100% are inputs, not market data.
- Common mistakes: calibrating delay LDs to interest only; forgetting that performance LDs are capital, not income; accepting an EOT clause whose employer risk events are wider than the PPA's relief events; assuming bonds cover a contractor failure; letting the defects period expire before the first major inspection.
- Questions experts ask: What does a day of delay cost us, layer by layer, and which layers does the rate cover? How many days does the cap buy against the IE's plausible delay? If the contractor fails at 60%, who finishes and who pays? What does the contractor's parent have on its other fixed-price contracts? Does every event that gives the contractor time give us relief under the PPA?

## 12. Judgment drill

- Situation: You are the sponsor's project director on a 480 MW solar-plus-storage project in North Africa, March 2027, EPC price USD 412.0 million. The preferred EPC contractor, a mid-sized regional firm with net worth of about USD 900 million, offers a delay LD cap of 12% and an overall cap of 60% of price, with a 10% performance bond and no parent guarantee. A larger international contractor offers 20% and 100% with a parent guarantee, at a price USD 37.4 million higher. The lenders' term sheet requires a delay LD cap that covers the IE's plausible delay of 240 days at the lenders' floor of USD 143,800/day. Board meets in ten days.
- Reasoning outline: test both offers with Frameworks 22.1 and 22.2; compute the lenders' requirement (240 × 143,800 = USD 34.512 million; 12% of 412.0 = USD 49.44 million covers it in cash terms but the rate matters: the writer sets the regional contractor's daily rate, for example USD 131,000, so the cap covers 377 days at that rate while each day falls USD 12,800 short of the floor; verify); weigh the smaller contractor's credit (a 60% overall cap and no parent leave the owner exposed in a failure; Example 22.5 shows bonds alone fall short); options: a bank-issued performance bond at 20% instead of a parent guarantee, sponsor completion support for the gap, a contingency increase, joint venture with a stronger partner. Expert answer: choose the regional contractor only with a 20% on-demand bond and a raised delay LD rate at or above the floor, priced against the USD 37.4 million saving; otherwise take the international offer.
- What would change the answer: the regional contractor's record on similar plants; its backlog of fixed-price work; an ECA requirement tied to the international contractor's home country (forward to Chapter 29); a tighter lender market that makes the higher price unfinanceable.
