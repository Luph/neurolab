# Chapter 17. Concessions, implementation agreements and government support

## 1. Header

- Chapter number: 17
- Title: Concessions, implementation agreements and government support
- File: `chapters/17-concessions-and-government-support.tex`
- Target length: 11,500 words (core technical chapter)
- Dominant expert lens: project finance lawyer who has negotiated concessions and implementation agreements, paired with the government PPP-unit official (the book's four-lens rule applies; the lender and sponsor chairs are present in every section)

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A private project company cannot finance an asset that the state can take, tax away, regulate out of business or simply stop paying for. The concession, the implementation agreement and the instruments of government support are how a host government turns those powers into promises that lenders can price. This chapter teaches what those documents grant, which government risks they allocate and how, what the state pays on early termination and why each party fights over the formula, and how to tell a guarantee that pays from a letter that only comforts. It equips the reader to read a government agreement clause by clause and to say, for any adverse government act, whether the project company is protected, by what instrument, for how much, and from whose budget.

What you will be able to do (for the chapter's `\section*`):
- Read a concession or implementation agreement and map each government risk to the clause and party that bears it (Capability 3).
- Calculate termination compensation under debt-based, equity-based and market-value regimes, and test a sovereign guarantee cap against it over the project's life (Capabilities 3 and 8).
- Rank government support instruments by legal strength and say what each is worth to lenders (Capability 8).
- Negotiate the termination schedule and support package from the sponsor's, the lenders' and the government's chair (Capabilities 6 and 9).

## 6. Worked examples

All Illustrative unless labeled. Numbers verified with Python on October 3, 2026.

**Example 17.1 Termination compensation for a 320 MW gas-fired IPP under three termination grounds** (`ex:17.1`, Illustrative). Scenario: a 320 MW open-cycle gas plant in a West African single-buyer market (not Kessara), terminated 5.5 years after financial close (about two years after COD). Inputs: senior debt outstanding USD 187.4 million; swap breakage payable by the project company USD 6.3 million; equity contributed USD 78.6 million at financial close; contractual equity rate 15.0%. Two scenarios. Scenario A (performing): distributions received to date USD 12.7 million; projected remaining distributions 17 annual amounts starting at USD 24.6 million and growing 2.0% a year. Scenario B (underperforming): distributions received USD 4.1 million; projected remaining distributions 17 annual amounts starting at USD 9.6 million growing 2.0%. Compute under: (i) government default: senior debt plus swap breakage plus the equity amount, the equity amount being the greater of the NPV of remaining distributions at 15.0% and equity contributed compounded at 15.0% for 5.5 years less distributions received; (ii) natural force majeure: senior debt plus equity contributed less distributions received; (iii) project company default: 85% of senior debt. Expected results (USD million): equity contributed compounded = 169.5. Scenario A: NPV of distributions 164.6; compounded less distributions 156.8; government-default amount 187.4 + 6.3 + 164.6 = 358.3; natural FM 253.3; project company default 159.3. Scenario B: NPV 64.2; compounded less distributions 165.4 (the floor bites); government-default amount 359.1; natural FM 261.9; project company default 159.3. Teaching points: under the "greater of" formula the state pays more on termination of the weaker project (359.1 against 358.3), so the compounded-equity floor transfers performance risk to the state; the project company default amount is set by the debt floor alone and leaves the lenders 14.0% short, which is why lenders push the floor toward 100% and governments resist. State the simplifications: annual periods, no tax on the termination sum, no insurance proceeds, distributions not compounded in the floor (as in Case P's formula).

**Example 17.2 Sizing a sovereign guarantee cap** (`ex:17.2`, Illustrative). Same 320 MW plant, different structure for clarity: senior debt at COD USD 412.6 million amortizing as a 17-year annuity at 7.1% (annual debt service USD 42.55 million); projected distributions USD 23.8 million a year while debt is outstanding and USD 61.3 million a year for the eight years after; equity amount on government default = NPV of remaining distributions at 15.0%; capacity charge USD 12.65/kW-month, so one month's capacity payment is USD 4.05 million and three months USD 12.14 million. The guarantee covers three months of payment obligations plus the termination amount; swap breakage ignored. Compute the termination amount plus three months' payments at years 0, 3, 6, 10, 15 and 20 from COD. Expected results (USD million): year 0: debt 412.6, equity amount 169.5, termination amount 582.1, guaranteed exposure 594.2; year 3: 369.9, 175.1, 545.0, 557.2; year 6: 317.5, 183.7, 501.2, 513.3; year 10: 228.5, 202.4, 431.0, 443.1; year 15: 76.8, 246.7, 323.5, 335.7; year 20: 0.0, 205.5, 205.5, 217.6. Question posed: the ministry offers a flat cap of USD 520.0 million. The cap falls short for roughly the first six years, exactly when debt is highest. Show two fixes: a declining cap schedule that tracks the exposure profile, or a cap tied to the termination formula plus a fixed payment-obligation buffer. Exhibit 17.5 (the exposure profile against the flat cap) belongs here, as a pgfplots line chart with the table above.

**Example 17.3 A new levy and the change-in-law threshold** (`ex:17.3`, Illustrative). Same 320 MW plant; annual non-fuel revenue USD 96.8 million. A finance act introduces a 1.2% levy on the gross revenue of all electricity generators. The IA passes through change in law above a threshold of USD 0.5 million a year, and the clause covers only changes specific to the power sector (variant b of Clause 17.3) or all changes (variant a). Compute: levy USD 1.162 million a year; above the threshold, so recoverable in full under a "whole amount once threshold exceeded" drafting, or USD 0.662 million under a "deductible" drafting; tariff adjustment to restore the position under whole-amount drafting = USD 0.302/kW-month (1.162 million divided by 320,000 kW times 12). Teaching point: the threshold drafting (franchise versus deductible) and the scope (specific versus general) decide the answer; under government-friendly variant c (discriminatory changes only) the levy, which hits all generators, is not compensable at all.

**Example 17.4 Restoring economic equilibrium by term extension or by tariff** (`ex:17.4`, Illustrative). Same plant, civil-law IA. A regulation lowers the project company's net cash flow by USD 2.9 million a year for the remaining 18 years. Discount rate agreed in the IA 9.0%. Compute PV of the loss: USD 25.39 million. Remedy 1: term extension, with net cash flow in the extension years (after debt is repaid) of USD 38.6 million a year: cumulative PV of extension cash flows after 3 years is USD 20.71 million and after 4 years USD 26.51 million, so a four-year extension slightly over-compensates and the parties settle on extension plus a small payment, or a pro-rated final year. Remedy 2: tariff uplift for the remaining 18 years of USD 0.755/kW-month (2.9 million divided by 3.84 million kW-months a year). Teaching point: lenders prefer the tariff uplift because a term extension pays after their debt is gone; governments prefer extension because it costs nothing in their budget horizon; sponsors look at the discount rate.

**17.8 Walkthrough: marking up an implementation agreement's termination schedule.** The artifact is an illustrative two-page termination schedule (Exhibit 17.6, a captioned table rendering of the schedule's grid: ground, notice, cure, compensation, payment terms) for the 320 MW plant of Examples 17.1 and 17.2. 2. For each ground, write the compensation formula in one line and classify it with Framework 17.1.
3. Test the debt protection: compute the project company default amount against senior debt at three dates (financial close plus 2, 6 and 12 years); flag any year where it falls below the lenders' floor.
4. Read the definition of protected senior debt: refinancing increases, hedging breakage, accrued interest, default interest; mark each in or out and say who wants what.
5. Check payment mechanics: due date, interest for late payment, currency and convertibility, installments, set-off against amounts the project company owes.
6. Check the link to the guarantee: is the termination amount within the guarantee's scope and cap at every date (use Example 17.2's profile)?
7. Check the interaction with lenders' step-in: does the government's termination right wait for the lenders' cure period (forward to Chapter 28)?
8. Write the markup: three comments the lenders' counsel would send, with the reason for each, and the likely government response.

## 8. Frameworks

- **Framework 17.1 Termination compensation matrix** (`fw:termination-compensation-matrix`, original). Purpose: classify any termination regime and spot where the lenders or the state are unprotected. Steps: (1) list grounds down the rows (government default, political FM, natural FM, project company default, voluntary or public-interest, expiry); (2) across the columns record what each ground pays to senior debt, hedges, subordinated debt, equity; (3) mark each cell as full, partial (with the percentage) or nil; (4) read the diagonal of incentives: no ground should pay equity more than performance would have earned, and no ground short of project company default should leave senior debt unpaid; (5) record who pays (offtaker, government, guarantor, insurer) and from which budget. Used in 17.4.2, the walkthrough, Case P and Exercise 17.12.
- **Framework 17.2 Support instrument strength test** (`fw:support-strength-test`, original). Purpose: decide what an instrument of government support is worth to lenders. Five questions, each answered yes or no with evidence: (1) Does it create a payment obligation, or only an obligation to use efforts or to consult? (2) Is payment due on demand, or only after remedies against the primary obligor are exhausted? (3) Is the issuer authorized and is the amount within a legislated ceiling and budget? (4) Can it be enforced: governing law, forum, waiver of immunity (forward to Chapter 54)? (5) Is there a backstop if the issuer does not pay (DFI guarantee, political risk insurance; forward to Chapters 34 and 60)? Score: five yeses is a guarantee lenders credit in sizing; three or fewer is comfort. Applied in 17.5.4.
- Established tools credited in prose only: the "no better and no worse" compensation standard (common-law PPP practice); economic equilibrium (French administrative law tradition). No framework box for either.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook

- Checklist: grant and term run from COD with a long-stop; IA and PPA termination grounds mirror; convertibility and transfer undertaking covers debt service, O&M, and distributions; change-in-law scope and threshold drafting; political versus natural FM split; termination amount covers senior debt at every date; protected-debt definition; payment currency, timing and interest; guarantee scope covers both payment obligations and termination amounts; guarantee cap tested against the exposure profile; immunity waiver and forum; budget authorization evidenced by a legal opinion and the Finance Act ceiling.
- Red flags: guarantee payable only after exhaustion of remedies against the utility; termination amount payable "subject to budgetary appropriation"; change-in-law clause limited to discriminatory changes in an emerging market; a cure-period structure that lets the government terminate before lenders can step in; a guarantee cap set as a round number without a profile; a letter of support described in the term sheet as a guarantee.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: in emerging-market IPPs closed 2015–2025 (indicative, D-011), lenders expect project company default compensation of at least most of senior debt (often 80% to 100%); limits: OECD user-pay concessions use market-value retender regimes without a debt floor (Case T), and a debt floor is worth only as much as the payer. Equity compensation on government default at the bid IRR is common; limit: a compounded-equity floor rewards poor performance (Example 17.1).
- Common mistakes: treating "government support" as a guarantee; forgetting that termination compensation is often payable in local currency; reading the IA without the PPA; assuming the guarantee covers swap breakage; ignoring that a term extension remedy pays after the debt is gone.
- Questions experts ask: Who pays the termination amount, from which budget line, in which currency, and how fast? What happens to the protected debt if we refinance? Which termination grounds pay equity, and is any ground better for equity than performing? Has the guarantee ever been called in this country, and was it paid?

## 12. Judgment drill

- Situation: You are the mandated lead arranger on a 260 MW solar-plus-storage IPP in a B-rated East African country, September 2026. The finance ministry, now under an agreed ceiling on new guarantees, withdraws the draft sovereign guarantee and offers instead: a letter of support from the minister; a put option under which the government buys the plant on termination for 90% of senior debt, payable in four semiannual installments; and acceptance of a USD 60.0 million DFI partial risk guarantee covering the government's put payment. Senior debt is USD 284.5 million. Your credit committee meets in nine days.
- Reasoning outline: apply Framework 17.2 to each instrument; compute the uncovered exposure (90% of 284.5 = 256.05 million, of which 60.0 covered by the PRG; 196.05 million relies on the government); value the installment delay (two years of payment risk and interest); weigh the ceiling as a sign of fiscal discipline (which improves the payer's ability to honor the put) against the softer form; consider asking for 100% debt coverage on government default and political FM, accelerated installments if the PRG is drawn, a local-currency risk solution, political risk insurance on the put (forward to Chapter 60). The expert answer: accept the put-plus-PRG structure if the put covers 100% of senior debt on government default and political FM, the installments carry interest at the loan rate, and the PRG is sized to the first two installments; treat the letter of support as having no sizing value.
- What would change the answer: the country's record of honoring guarantees (a recent default moves the answer to "decline"); whether the offtaker is investment grade (reduces reliance on the put); a DFI A/B loan in the structure (preferred creditor status changes the payment priority; forward to Chapter 29); currency of the put payment (local currency without a convertibility undertaking moves toward decline).

| concession agreement | – | A contract by which a public authority grants a private company the right to build or take over, operate and earn revenue from an asset or service that the authority would otherwise provide. | ssec:17.1.1 |

| economic equilibrium | – | The principle that a concessionaire is entitled to have its financial position restored when the authority's acts or specified events upset the balance agreed at signing. | ssec:17.1.3 |
| convertibility and transfer undertaking | – | A government promise that the project company may convert local currency into foreign currency and transfer it abroad to pay debt service, costs and distributions. | ssec:17.2.2 |
| change in law | – | A change after signing in the laws, regulations or their official interpretation that affects the project company's costs or revenues, classified as discriminatory, specific or general. | ssec:17.3.1 |
| discriminatory change in law | – | A change in law that applies to the project, its sector peers of the same kind, or its investors and not to businesses generally. | ssec:17.3.1 |
| compensation event | – | An event, usually caused by the authority, that entitles the project company to relief from its obligations and to compensation that leaves it no better and no worse off. | ssec:17.3.2 |
| material adverse government action | MAGA | Action by a government entity, other than a lawful general change in law, that materially harms the project company's ability to perform or its economics. | ssec:17.3.2 |
| relief event | – | An event that excuses the project company from the consequences of delay or non-performance, without entitling it to compensation. | ssec:17.3.2 |
| political force majeure | – | Force majeure caused by war, civil disturbance, expropriation or other acts in the government's sphere, for which the government side usually bears the cost. | ssec:17.3.3 |
| natural force majeure | – | Force majeure from natural events or other causes outside both parties' control and outside the government's sphere, for which losses are usually shared. | ssec:17.3.3 |
| termination compensation | – | The amount a contract requires one party to pay the other when the contract ends early, set by the ground of termination. | ssec:17.4.2 |
| debt floor | – | The minimum share of senior debt that termination compensation must pay, even when the project company is the party in default. | ssec:17.4.2 |
| put and call option agreement | PCOA | An agreement under which, on early termination, the project company may require the government to buy the plant or its shares for a price at least equal to outstanding debt, and the government may require the sale. | ssec:17.4.5 |
| sovereign guarantee | – | An undertaking by the state, usually through its finance ministry, to pay specified obligations of a state entity if that entity does not. | ssec:17.5.2 |
