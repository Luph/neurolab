Conventions fixed by this unit for the whole book (Chapter 35 is their home; every later chapter and the Case P model must use them):

1. CFADS (Equation 35.2) is a cash measure (the Case P ledger applies this definition to P-F10 and P-F41; Annex P §8.3): revenue received in cash, less operating costs paid, less cash taxes paid, less maintenance capital expenditure not funded from a reserve or from equity, less net transfers into the major maintenance reserve account (transfers in minus withdrawals used for maintenance), with working capital captured by the cash basis. It excludes debt drawings, equity contributions, DSRA movements, interest earned on the DSRA, insurance proceeds for physical damage (which pay for reinstatement), and performance liquidated damages applied to prepay debt. It includes business interruption and delay-in-start-up insurance proceeds and delay liquidated damages that replace lost revenue. Interest earned on operating accounts is included. The reinstatement test that decides where property-damage proceeds go is Chapter 27's (ssec:27.6.4, ruling R-058); Chapter 35 only states that those proceeds are outside CFADS. Each exclusion is negotiable and the chapter teaches the arguments; this list is the book's default.
2. LLCR (Equation 35.3) and PLCR (Equation 35.5) are computed at the start of a period: present value of CFADS from that period to the final maturity (LLCR) or to the end of the revenue contract or concession (PLCR), discounted at the weighted average all-in interest rate of the senior debt (hedged rate for the hedged share, projected floating rate for the rest, plus margins; fees and ECA premium excluded), plus the DSRA cash balance at that date, divided by senior debt outstanding at that date. The chapter also shows the variant without the DSRA balance and states that a document must say which it uses. The Case P ledger states this definition on P-F08 (LLCR at close 1.42x with the DSRA and 1.36x without it, so the 1.40x test does not bind; Annex P §4.15).
3. Gearing (Equation 35.9), in its project finance measurement, is senior debt divided by the sum of senior debt and equity (share capital plus shareholder loans), measured on the total funding requirement at financial close, which includes financing costs and initial DSRA funding. This matches Case P's cap (75% of total funding requirement; the FC base outcome is P-F07). The general sense (debt over debt plus equity) is defined earlier in ssec:8.2.1; Chapter 35 cites it and adds the measurement rule.
4. Minimum DSCR is the lowest period DSCR from first repayment to final maturity on the stated case. Average DSCR is reported in two forms, the simple arithmetic average of period DSCRs and the debt-service-weighted average (sum of CFADS divided by sum of debt service); the book prefers the weighted form and says so.

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose: give the reader the measuring instruments of project debt. The chapter defines cash flow available for debt service (CFADS) precisely, item by item, and shows why lenders measure cash rather than profit; then builds the four ratios lenders live by (DSCR in its minimum, average, historic and projected forms; LLCR; PLCR; gearing) from first principles, with the algebra that links them; then defines the cases on which the ratios are run (base, banking, downside, break-even) and who owns each. It stops short of using the ratios to set the debt amount, which is Chapter 36's job, and of setting lock-up and default levels, which is Chapter 37's.

What you will be able to do (four items for the `\section*`):

- Build CFADS for any operating period from contracts and cost data, and defend each inclusion or exclusion in a definitions negotiation (Capabilities 4, 5).
- Compute and interpret minimum and average DSCR, historic and projected DSCR, LLCR, PLCR, and gearing, and explain what each one can and cannot tell a credit committee (Capability 5).
- Define base, banking, downside, and break-even cases, run them on a simple model, and read the headroom they reveal (Capabilities 4, 14).
- Audit a lender's ratio summary for the classic definitional errors (Capabilities 4, 15).

### 35.3 The loan life cover ratio (`sec:35.3`)

- 35.3.1 Definition, discount rate, and the DSRA (`ssec:35.3.1`). Equation 35.3. Discount rate is the weighted average all-in senior interest rate (book convention); why not a cost of equity or WACC; DSRA balance in the numerator (book convention) and the variant without it. Example 35.5 (continuing the Peru line): LLCR 1.34x at close without DSRA, 1.40x with the DSRA; sensitivity of LLCR to the discount rate (1.44x at 5.10%, 1.25x at 7.60%); LLCR 1.38x at the end of year 5.
- 35.3.2 LLCR as an average DSCR in present-value terms (`ssec:35.3.2`). Equation 35.4: LLCR (without DSRA) equals the DSCRs weighted by the present value of each period's debt service, when discounted at the debt's own rate. Verified numerically on Example 35.5 (both 1.340x). Consequences: LLCR is always at least the minimum DSCR on that basis; a profile sculpted to a constant DSCR target has an LLCR equal to that target; so an LLCR covenant set above the sculpting target (with no DSRA in the numerator) is a tighter sizing constraint in disguise. This is the point the Case P installment returns to.
- 35.3.3 What LLCR adds that DSCR cannot (`ssec:35.3.3`). LLCR sees the whole remaining loan in one number, so one bad period does not trip it, but a permanent fall does; its use in distribution tests and in sweeps keyed to LLCR (forward reference `sec:37.3`, `sec:37.4`); how LLCR moves through life as debt amortizes.

## 6. Worked examples

All Illustrative unless labeled. Computed in Python; the writer recomputes. Currency figures in millions unless stated.

Example 35.1, Building CFADS for one operating year (Illustrative), `ex:35.1`.
- Scenario: 96.4 MWac single-axis solar plant near Ma'an, southern Jordan, owned by Qatrana Solar PSC (fictional), selling under a 20-year USD PPA signed in 2024 with Jordan's state-owned single buyer (not named), paid monthly on 45-day terms; COD 2025. The year built is OY3 (calendar 2027), as the agent bank's analyst prepares the December 2026 budget review.
- Inputs: P50 net generation 222.1 GWh (capacity factor 26.30%: 96.4 × 8,760 × 0.2630 / 1,000); tariff USD 49.85/MWh flat nominal; O&M contract USD 1.27 million; insurance 0.36; land lease 0.19; grid and transmission charges 0.24; project company administration 0.41; cash tax paid 0.52; increase in receivables 0.23; inverter replacement 0.58 funded by an MMRA withdrawal of 0.58; MMRA contribution 0.29; interest earned on DSRA 0.11; insurance proceeds of 0.35 received and spent on hail-damage repairs.
- Compute: revenue 222.1 × 49.85 / 1,000 = USD 11.07 million; operating costs 2.47; EBITDA 8.60; CFADS = 11.07 − 2.47 − 0.52 − 0.23 − 0.58 + 0.58 − 0.29 = USD 7.56 million. DSRA interest and the property-damage proceeds and repair spend are excluded (book convention). Show Exhibit 35.1 build table and the Excel rows (Operations, Tax, Reserves sheets linked into Waterfall).
- Key results: CFADS USD 7.56 million; CFADS/EBITDA 0.88. A sponsor-friendly definition (adding DSRA interest, ignoring the MMRA contribution) gives USD 7.96 million, 5.3% more.

Example 35.2, The same year on accruals and on cash (Illustrative), `ex:35.2`.
- Scenario: 105 MW geothermal plant in Kenya's Rift Valley owned by Eburru Geopower Ltd (fictional), selling under a USD-denominated PPA to the state utility (not named); in the 2026 test year the utility moves from paying in 30 days to paying in 105 days, and the lenders' agent (a South African bank, not named) sees it in the December 31, 2026 compliance certificate.
- Inputs: invoiced revenue USD 61.84 million; operating costs paid 17.26; tax nil (holiday); debt service 33.15.
- Compute: accrual-basis CFADS 44.58, DSCR 1.345x (1.34x printed with two decimals: write "1.34x" and keep the unrounded value for the solution's check); increase in receivables 61.84 × 75/365 = 12.71; cash CFADS 31.87, DSCR 0.96x.
- Lesson: the same contract and output, a 0.38x swing from one payment-term change; the year after, if the utility stays at 105 days, the DSCR returns to 1.34x but the 12.71 is still owed. Link to Case P's 2022 to 2025 arrears (forward pointer to sec:59.10, one sentence, no figures). Chapter 7 (ssec:7.9.2) already showed the accounting side of a late-paying offtaker; cite it.

Example 35.3, Minimum and average DSCR on a 12-year loan (Illustrative), `ex:35.3`.

## 8. Frameworks

Framework 35.1, the four-ratio read (`fw:four-ratio-read`), original. Purpose: read any ratio package in five minutes and know where the risk sits. Steps:
1. Minimum DSCR on the banking case: is the worst year above the sizing target and where does it fall (early, middle, late)?
2. Weighted average DSCR against minimum: a wide gap means a lumpy profile and one or two dangerous years.
3. LLCR against minimum DSCR and against the target: if LLCR roughly equals the target, the debt is sculpted; if LLCR exceeds it materially, something else (gearing, tenor) bound.
4. PLCR minus LLCR: the tail cushion available in distress.
5. Break-even headroom on the two drivers that matter most (for Example 35.8: generation and tariff), compared with the downside case the IE thinks plausible.
Use: on every credit paper and every lender model output before reading the narrative.

Established tools credited in prose: none named beyond standard ratio definitions; do not attribute the ratios to any author.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (CFADS and ratio definitions review):
- The CFADS definition lists every inflow and outflow, says cash basis, and states the treatment of each Exhibit 35.2 item.
- Debt service includes all senior interest, scheduled principal, fees payable periodically, and net swap payments, and excludes voluntary prepayments and sweeps.
- Each ratio states its period, test dates, case (historic actuals, agent's banking case), and discount rate.
- LLCR says whether the DSRA balance is in the numerator.
- Gearing states its denominator.
- The model's ratio rows tie to the definitions word for word (cross-reference Chapter 44).

Red flags:
- CFADS rising faster than revenue in the projections (reserve releases or working capital reversals counted as income).
- Average DSCR quoted without the minimum.
- Downside cases that show exactly the target ratio (debt resculpted per case).
- Break-even on the main driver within the IE's own plausible range.
- PLCR barely above LLCR in a project whose contract ends with the loan.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- 1 minus 1/DSCR is the fall in CFADS a period can absorb before debt service goes unpaid (26% at 1.35x); it ignores the DSRA and the correlation between periods.
- With sculpting at a constant target and discounting at the debt rate, LLCR equals the target; breaks when the rate is not the debt rate or the DSRA is included.
- Operating leverage multiplies volume risk: CFADS volatility is roughly revenue volatility times revenue/CFADS (the multiplier of eq:14.2, ssec:14.7.1, cited not re-derived; Chapters 36 and 37 use it); fails when costs are variable.

Common mistakes:
- Using the tax charge instead of cash tax.
- Counting insurance proceeds for reinstatement as CFADS.
- Testing six-month ratios in a seasonal business.
- Computing LLCR at the end of a period instead of the start (off by one period of debt service).

Questions experts ask:
- What is the minimum DSCR on the case the IE signed?
- Which single driver takes the DSCR to 1.00x first, and how far away is it?
- Does the CFADS definition follow cash at the project's own settlement point?
- What is in the LLCR numerator?

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: You are the credit analyst at the lead arranger (a Singapore-based bank, not named) for Thar Hybrid Power Pvt Ltd (fictional), a 180 MW solar-plus-storage project near Jaisalmer, Rajasthan, India, with a 25-year PPA with a state distribution utility (not named), financed in USD for this illustration. Credit committee meets Thursday, October 15, 2026. The sponsor's counsel has sent a marked-up CFADS definition that (a) adds interest earned on the DSRA (USD 0.42 million a year), (b) adds expected battery augmentation funded by "committed equity" back to CFADS (USD 1.8 million in years 9 and 10), (c) treats a state-government grant receivable in year 2 (USD 3.1 million) as CFADS. On the lenders' banking case, minimum DSCR is 1.27x against a 1.30x sizing target; the markup lifts it to 1.33x. Illustrative numbers; writer computes the effect of each item separately on a stated debt service (choose debt service USD 18.6 million in the minimum year so 1.27x = USD 23.6 million CFADS; DSRA interest raises it to 1.29x; the others apply in other years).

Reasoning it through (outline): separate cash that recurs and is available to pay debt (DSRA interest: arguably yes, but it disappears exactly when the DSRA is drawn; landing is often to include it with the DSRA counted once only, not both in CFADS and LLCR numerator) from cash that is not available (equity-funded augmentation: if equity is genuinely committed and LC-backed, deducting the capex and adding the equity is circular; treat the capex as funded and outside CFADS rather than adding equity to CFADS) and from one-offs (the grant: include in the year received only if the receivable is from a creditworthy payer and not counted in sizing). Expert answer: accept (a) with the DSRA not double counted, reject (b) as drafted and replace with exclusion of equity-funded capex, accept (c) for covenant testing but exclude from the sizing case; tell committee the sizing minimum is 1.27x and propose resizing debt down (quantify with Chapter 36 forward reference: on a 1.30x target, debt falls about 2.3% in proportion, 1 − 1.27/1.30, only if that year binds).

What would change the answer: a rated sponsor guarantee of augmentation funding (moves toward accepting b in the form "capex funded by committed equity is excluded"); a state payer with arrears history (moves c out entirely); a liquidity-facility instead of a cash DSRA (moves a out, because there is no interest).

- eq:35.2: $\mathrm{CFADS}_t = \mathrm{Rev}_t - \mathrm{Opex}_t - \mathrm{Tax}_t - \mathrm{Capex}^{\mathrm{m}}_t - \Delta\mathrm{WC}_t - (\mathrm{MMRA}^{\mathrm{in}}_t - \mathrm{MMRA}^{\mathrm{out}}_t)$, cash basis (if the model is accrual-based, $\Delta\mathrm{WC}_t$ converts it).
- eq:35.3: $\mathrm{LLCR}_t = \left(\sum_{s=t}^{N} \mathrm{CFADS}_s\,(1+r)^{-(s-t+1)} + \mathrm{DSRA}_t\right) / D_t$, with $r$ the weighted average all-in senior rate per period and the sum over the periods $t$ to $N$ (state the timing convention: cash flows at period ends, debt measured at the start of period $t$).

- eq:35.5: PLCR as eq:35.3 with the sum to $T$.
- eq:35.6: $\overline{\mathrm{DSCR}} = \sum \mathrm{CFADS}_t / \sum \mathrm{DS}_t$ over repayment periods.
- eq:35.9: $G = D/(D+E)$ on the total funding requirement (project finance measurement; the general ratio is ssec:8.2.1).

## 8. Frameworks

Framework 36.1, the sizing constraint stack (`fw:sizing-constraint-stack`), original. Purpose: size any project's debt in a fixed order so nothing is missed.
1. Fix the revenue buckets and the CFADS for each case (base, banking, downside, P-values).
2. Set the tenor from contract end less the required tail, and the repayment start from COD plus grace.
3. Compute the sculpted debt on each DSCR test with debt service shaped on the sizing case.
4. Compute the gearing amount on the funding requirement (financing costs converged in closed form or by iterative calculation with a circuit breaker, Chapter 40, sec:40.5).
5. Take the lesser; record the binding constraint and the slack in each other one.
6. Test profile rules (WAL, largest installment, first repayment, LLCR covenant at close) and repair the profile, re-sizing if needed.
7. Run downside and break-even cases with debt service fixed and report headroom (Framework 35.1).
Use: every sizing, from a first indicative term sheet to the final credit paper.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (sizing run sign-off): sizing case named; DSCR tests and P-values stated; debt service fixed across tested cases; gearing denominator; tenor, first repayment, tail; average-life and installment limits; rate used for PV (hedged plus margin, step-ups); IDC and fees converged (Chapter 40, sec:40.5); binding constraint and slack recorded.

Red flags: debt sized on the sponsor's base case with no downside test; negative principal anywhere; tail under two years on a contract with renewal risk; balloon above what the remaining contract can refinance at a conservative ratio; a "notional" profile longer than the contract; WAL creeping toward the limit after a late change in CFADS.

Rules of thumb and their limits: debt is inversely proportional to the sculpting target (each 0.10x near 1.30x moves debt about 7% to 8%; holds only when DSCR binds); each extra year of tenor adds less debt than the last (discounting and the shrinking tail); revenue/CFADS times generation uncertainty approximates CFADS uncertainty (the eq:14.2 multiplier; fails with large variable costs).

Common mistakes: sculpting on post-sweep cash; using one rate for PV and another for interest; resculpting each sensitivity; forgetting that grace-period interest must be covered; mixing one-year and ten-year P-values; quoting a mini-perm's tenor as its notional profile.

Questions experts ask: which constraint binds and by how much is the next one slack; what happens to the profile if COD slips six months; who refinances the balloon and on what ratios; what tail is left if the PPA is not renewed; what did the last three comparable deals size at, and in what market conditions.

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: You are the lead arranger on Shofirkon Sun Power LLC (fictional), a 300 MWac solar plant in Bukhara region, Uzbekistan, with a 25-year USD PPA from the state single buyer (not named), backed by a sovereign guarantee, with an ECA and two DFIs (not named) in the lender group. On October 5, 2026, three weeks before credit committee, the sponsor's financial advisor asks for (a) a 1.25x P50 sizing DSCR instead of your 1.35x, (b) an 18-year tenor post-COD instead of 15, leaving a 7-year tail, and (c) a 12-month grace period. Illustrative computation (writer recomputes): CFADS USD 61.4 million in year 1 with 60% of it indexed at 2.0% a year (CFADS_t = 61.4 x (0.4 + 0.6 x 1.02^(t-1))), 7.25% all-in, sculpted. Starting point 1.35x over 15 years: USD 438.1 million. Separately: 1.25x adds 8.0% (USD 473.1 million); 18 years adds 11.6% (USD 488.9 million); a 12-month interest-only grace followed by 15 years adds 1.3% (USD 443.6 million, interest cover 1.91x in the grace year). Combined: USD 534.7 million, +22.1%.

Reasoning it through (outline): rank the requests by the risk they add. Tenor to 18 years keeps a 7-year tail on a 25-year contract and is within what ECAs allow for renewables under the CCSU (cite `t-oecd-arrangement`; check ECA, not just bank, appetite); it is the cheapest risk to grant. The 1.25x target removes cushion in every year and matters most in a country where payment delays, not resource, are the main downside; the DSRA covers six months, not a structural shortfall. The grace period shifts principal into later years and raises WAL, possibly breaching ECA limits (callback to Example 36.12). Expert answer: grant (b) subject to the ECA WAL test, offer 1.30x with a P99 one-year floor of 1.00x instead of (a), and refuse (c) unless COD timing risk justifies it (interest-only for six months at most). Explain to the sponsor in money: the counteroffer (1.30x, 18 years, no grace) gives USD 507.7 million, +15.9%, about 72% of the uplift requested, with a P99 floor the sponsor's own resource report should pass.

What would change the answer: a DFI partial risk guarantee covering offtaker payment (moves toward 1.25x); a weaker sovereign rating or arrears history (away); local-currency tariff (shorter tenor); strong market competition with three other arrangers bidding (more of the request granted, with tighter lock-ups instead, `sec:37.4`).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

Purpose: Chapter 36 sized the debt on forecasts that will be wrong. This chapter teaches the structural features that keep a correctly sized loan alive in the years the forecast misses and that stop cash leaving the project company when it is needed: reserve accounts (sizing, funding, letters of credit, drawing and refilling), cash sweeps, distribution lock-ups, financial and non-financial covenants as design choices (drafting is Chapter 51's), and the hedging requirements that fix the interest cost the sizing assumed. ## 5. Opening

In 2006 the Indiana Toll Road's concessionaire financed its USD 3.8 billion upfront payment with a nine-year interest-only loan and a 20-year accreting swap whose pre-set rates climbed from 3% toward 11.3%; eight years later the swap alone was a liability of about USD 2.15 billion and the company filed a prepackaged Chapter 11, while the road itself kept running (from `indiana-toll-road`). Open on the inversion: the hedge, meant to make debt service predictable, became the largest single claim. Then turn to the question the chapter answers: which protective features belong around a sized loan, how big should each be, and what does each cost?

## 8. Frameworks

Framework 37.1, the trigger ladder (`fw:trigger-ladder`), original. Purpose: see every covenant trigger as a rung on one ladder of CFADS falls, so levels are coherent. Steps:
1. Start from the sizing ratio on the sizing case.
2. For each trigger (lock-up, sweep step-up, default, DSRA drawn, DSRA exhausted, payment default), compute the CFADS fall that reaches it: $1 - \text{trigger}/\text{sizing ratio}$, and for reserve rungs the duration of shortfall the reserve covers.
3. Compare each fall with the IE's downside and with the probability from the resource or price model (Example 37.5 method).
4. Check spacing: each rung must leave time and cash for the next party to act (sponsor cure, lender standstill).
5. Adjust levels or sizing until the ladder is coherent.
Use: designing a term sheet and reviewing a covenant package.

Framework 37.2, the hedge fit test (`fw:hedge-fit-test`), original. Purpose: confirm a hedge fits the debt. Steps:
1. Notional: does it follow projected balances, including construction accretion, and what ratio results after plausible prepayments (LDs, sweeps, insurance)?
2. Tenor: does it end with the debt, not before (unhedged tail) or after (naked swap)?
3. Index and dates: same reference rate, fixing and payment dates as the loan (Case P's 2023 LIBOR-to-SOFR basis is Chapter 6's lesson).
4. Direction: does the hedge reduce the project's exposure in every scenario or add a new one (Indiana Toll Road's rising pre-set rates)?
5. Breakage: who pays, how it ranks, and the size at a 100 bps and 200 bps move.
Use: at structuring, at every prepayment, and at refinancing.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (reserve and covenant package): DSRA basis and funding; LC issuer rating and replacement trigger; MMRA linked to the IE's maintenance plan; reserve release rules; sweep type and application order; distribution conditions and the case used for projected tests; lock-up and default levels checked with the trigger ladder; cure limits; hedge ratio, band, tenor, counterparties, execution process and charge; breakage ranking.

Red flags: lock-up level within one standard deviation of the sizing case; DSRA LC from a sponsor-affiliated or downgrade-prone bank without replacement trigger; MMRA sized from the sponsor's budget not the IE's; sweeps applied pro rata that leave final maturity unchanged; hedge notional fixed at close with no adjustment for prepayments; swaps longer than the debt; a single hedge bank pricing without competition.

Rules of thumb and their limits: six months of debt service covers one semiannual miss, not a structural fall; a lock-up a full 0.15x to 0.20x below the sizing ratio is a rough starting point for contracted assets, tested against the probability calculation (fails for merchant assets, where the variance is higher); hedge 75% to 90% of projected floating debt, leaving room for prepayments (Case P band; US LNG minimum 60%; neither is a universal norm).

Common mistakes: counting DSRA releases as CFADS; refilling the DSRA ahead of senior interest; testing the projected DSCR on the sponsor's case; forgetting that a cure by CFADS injection must not be counted again in the next test; hedging the sculpted profile of a loan that will be swept.

Questions experts ask: how many months of zero revenue can the structure survive (reserves plus LCs); in a P90 year, does the project lock up; who pays swap breakage on a mandatory prepayment; can the DSRA LC be called on downgrade; what is the expected trapped cash in the downside.

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: You are the agent bank's credit officer on Tema East Power Ltd (fictional), a contracted 400 MW CCGT near Tema, Ghana, selling to the state utility (not named), two years after its 2024 COD. On November 30, 2026 the sponsor asks, with a two-week deadline before its fund's year-end, to (a) replace the USD 31.4 million cash DSRA with an LC from a BBB+ bank that is also an affiliate of one sponsor, releasing the cash as a distribution; (b) lower the lock-up from 1.20x to 1.10x because "the plant has run at 96% availability"; and (c) cut the hedge ratio from 85% to 60% to benefit from expected rate cuts. Reasoning it through (outline): separate liquidity (a) from risk appetite (b, c). (a) An LC is economically equivalent only if the issuer will pay when the project is stressed; an affiliate bank creates wrong-way risk (ssec:14.20.2; R-036) and BBB+ falls short of the A- standard most structures require; offer acceptance with an A- unaffiliated issuer, a downgrade replacement trigger, and no release until the LC is in place. (b) Availability is not the risk; offtaker payment delay is: use the probability method (Example 37.5) and the late payments to show that 1.10x leaves almost no rung between lock-up and default; refuse, or offer 1.15x tied to the offtaker paying within terms for four consecutive quarters. (c) Lenders sized on a hedged rate; at 60% the trigger ladder shortens (compute with Example 37.8's method); refuse below the documented band, or allow a step-down only as debt amortizes. Expert answer: yes to (a) on conditions, no to (b) and (c) now, with a path to revisit.

What would change the answer: an offtaker payment-security upgrade (a DFI partial risk guarantee, Chapter 34) moves (b) toward yes; a forward curve showing cuts priced in reduces the sponsor's benefit from (c) and strengthens the refusal; an issuer rated A or better removes most objection to (a).

## 8. Frameworks

Framework 38.1, the all-in cost build (`fw:all-in-cost-build`), original. Purpose: compute and present the cost of any debt offer the same way. Steps:
1. Lay out the borrower's cash flows by period: drawings net of upfront fees and financed premia; interest at base plus margin by period (hedged rate for the hedged share); commitment, agency, and other periodic fees; PRI and gross-ups; principal.
2. Compute the IRR (Equation 38.2) and state the basis.
3. Decompose: base, weighted margin, fees per year of WAL, premium per year, other; reconcile to the IRR (the residual is the approximation error).
4. Attach the non-price terms that change the comparison (tenor, WAL, prepayment, flex, covenants).
Use: every pricing comparison, credit paper, and refinancing decision.

Established method credited in prose: the building-block or risk-adjusted return on capital (RAROC) approach banks use to set minimum margins (credit generically; no named author or date).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist (pricing comparison): same amount, drawdown, base rate, basis; every fee in the fee letters captured (structuring, underwriting, participation, commitment, agency, security agent, account bank, ECA agency); premium financed or paid; PRI, gross-up, and hedge charges included; step-ups and ratchets modeled on balances; prepayment and make-whole costs priced; flex caps understood.

Red flags: an all-in quoted as "swap plus margin"; fee letters not shared with the model auditor; hedge pricing by a single bank that is also the arranger; a step-up schedule that assumes refinancing before it bites; an ECA tranche compared on margin alone; uncapped flex.

Rules of thumb and their limits: upfront fee per year is roughly the fee divided by the WAL (fails for short WALs and long construction periods); the commitment fee is about 30% to 40% of margin in the deals in this chapter's sources (Port Arthur 30%, Case P 40%; not a universal rule); each 25 bps of margin on a 7-year WAL loan is worth about 1.4 to 1.7 points of price.

Common mistakes: annualizing fees over tenor instead of WAL; forgetting the premium is interest-bearing when financed; comparing a bond's coupon with a loan's margin; ignoring withholding tax on one lender group only; leaving commitment fees out of the IDC circularity.

Questions experts ask: what is the all-in on an IRR basis, and on what reporting basis; what is the cost of prepaying in year 5 under each offer; what does flex allow and who decides; how much of the price is the hedge; where does the price sit against the last three comparable closes.

## 12. Judgment drill

The situation: You are the bookrunner for a USD 820 million, 18-year loan (WAL 9.5 years) signed in July 2026 for Suez Gulf Power S.A.E. (fictional), a 960 MW CCGT near Ain Sokhna, Egypt, with a PPA with the state transmission company (not named), underwritten by three arrangers (not named) at a 2.40% margin. Three weeks into syndication the market has moved: comparable deals now clear at 2.70%. The flex clause allows up to 25 bps of margin flex and up to 0.25% of fee flex (fees moved from arrangers to participants). The sponsor's CFO calls: invoking flex will break their tariff model and "the relationship." You need USD 670 million sold to reach the arrangers' target holds. Numbers (Illustrative, computed): full margin flex costs the sponsor 25 bps on the outstanding balance, USD 19.47 million undiscounted (USD 12.59 million at 7.5%, equal annual principal); fee flex costs the arrangers USD 1.68 million on USD 670 million sold; the remaining 5 bps gap, if closed by discount, costs the arrangers about USD 2.08 million (spread duration 6.2 years); closing the full 30 bps by discount without flex would cost the arrangers about USD 12.46 million.

Reasoning it through (outline): flex exists for exactly this; not using it transfers USD 12 million of cost to the arrangers and sets a precedent. But the sponsor relationship and future mandates are real, and the tariff is fixed. Options: use fee flex first (cheapest for the sponsor), partial margin flex (for example 15 bps), and absorb the residual; or restructure (shorter tenor commercial tranche with a soft mini-perm, or an ECA or DFI tranche to replace part of the commercial demand, which takes time). Expert answer: invoke fee flex in full, invoke margin flex in part (15 bps costs the sponsor USD 11.68 million undiscounted, USD 7.56 million at 7.5%; 20 bps costs USD 15.58 million and USD 10.08 million; recomputed in round 1), absorb the last few bps as arranger discount, and offer a margin ratchet down after completion if the project performs; explain in money to the CFO.

What would change the answer: flex capped below the market move (arrangers must absorb more or restructure); other arrangers insisting on full flex under the underwriting agreement (the bookrunner cannot decide alone); a sponsor with a pipeline of mandates (relationship value up); a market still moving (sell fast, flex fully).
