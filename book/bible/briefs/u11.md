## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** By Chapter 51 the reader has sized the debt (Chapter 36), designed its covenants, reserves and cures (Chapter 37) and priced it (Chapter 38). This chapter turns those decisions into enforceable words. It maps the full document set from mandate letter to financial close, then teaches the clauses that do the work: conditions precedent, representations, information, positive and negative covenants, financial covenants as drafted, permitted debt and distributions, events of default and remedies, the equity cure as drafted, the hedge documents' termination events, change of control, transfers, and amendments, waivers and voting. It shows which parts come from LMA, LSTA or APLMA chassis wording and which are bespoke project terms where the negotiation happens, and it trains the reader to read a common terms agreement as a system of linked definitions rather than a stack of clauses.

**What you will be able to do** (style sheet 4.1 list, two to four items, binding):
- Map a project financing's document set and say which document holds each term and who drafts it (Capability 6).
- Read and mark up the conditions precedent, covenants, events of default, equity cure and hedge termination events in a common terms agreement and its hedging documents, and compute what each drafting choice costs the sponsor (Capabilities 6 and 10).
- Draft a permitted-debt test, a distribution condition, an event of default and a waiver letter with grace periods, materiality and conditions set where the market sets them (Capability 6).
- Spot the linked definitions that change a ratio covenant's meaning before signing (Capability 15).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned (taught in full here)

- The finance document set and its flow: mandate letter (exclusivity, clear market, conditions to commitment, expiry), the term sheet as a document in the set (basic definition at `ssec:4.10.3`; negotiation owned by Chapter 56), commitment letter, fee letters, common terms agreement (CTA), facility agreements per tranche, intercreditor agreement (content owned by Chapter 53), accounts agreement (content owned by Chapter 52), security documents (Chapter 52), direct agreements (Chapter 28), hedging documents under the ISDA framework.
- Hedging documents in a project financing: master agreement, schedule and confirmations; the hedging policy in the CTA; the project-finance schedule elections (additional termination events tied to loan prepayment, cancellation and acceleration; no credit support annex because the hedge counterparties share the senior security; transfer and novation limits; the hedge counterparty's narrowed events of default and termination rights; partial termination to restore the hedge ratio after a prepayment). Valuation of a close-out is Chapter 6's (`ssec:6.8.3`); ranking and voting of hedge claims Chapter 53's (`sec:53.5`); breakage in refinancing and distress Chapters 63 (`ssec:63.2.2`) and 64 (`ssec:64.7.4`).
- Standard market documentation: LMA, LSTA and APLMA forms, what each publishes, the absence of a recommended-form project finance CTA, the Infrastructure Asia 2020 Singapore-law core suite, English-law against New York-law conventions (majority thresholds, agency, transfers), and how to work with a chassis.
- Conditions precedent as finance-document content: signing, initial utilisation and each-utilisation sets; the initial CP list for a project financing; drawdown conditions in construction (IE certificate, cost-to-complete condition as drafted, no Default, repeating representations); waiver of CPs and conditions subsequent (CS, finance-documents sense).
- Representations and warranties in finance documents: one-time and repeating; materiality and knowledge qualifiers; project-specific representations (project documents, permits, no other business, information memorandum and base case, sanctions and anti-corruption).
- Covenants as drafted: information covenants and the compliance certificate; positive and negative covenants (negative pledge, no disposals, no other business, project document amendments, budgets); financial covenants as drafted (definitions, calculation dates, who calculates, dispute resolution of calculations); permitted debt, permitted security and permitted disposals; incurrence tests for additional debt; restricted payments and distribution conditions as drafted; "Default" against "Event of Default".

- The equity cure as drafted: CFADS cure against prepayment cure, application order, caps and frequency, timing windows, deemed cure. The design and the amounts are Chapter 37's (`ssec:37.5.3`, eq:37.4); this chapter shows how the drafting moves the cost.

- Amendments, waivers and voting inside a facility: majority, super-majority and unanimous matters; entrenched matters; defaulting lender disenfranchisement; deemed consent ("snooze-you-lose"); replacement of a non-consenting lender ("yank-the-bank"); the waiver and amendment letter as a document, with its conditions, fee and reservation of rights. ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 51.1 Boilerplate-or-bargain sort** (`fw:boilerplate-or-bargain`), original. Purpose: decide where negotiation time goes in a long document. Steps: (1) identify the chassis (LMA English-law, LSTA New York-law, APLMA Singapore or Hong Kong law, the Infrastructure Asia suite, or a firm precedent); (2) sort each clause into chassis wording, adapted chassis, or bespoke project term; (3) check chassis clauses only for deviations from the published form; (4) check adapted clauses for project fit (for example, tax gross-up for DFI exemptions); (5) put the team's time into bespoke clauses ranked by money at stake. When to use: first read of any draft finance document. Credit: the "chassis, not the car" framing follows the t-loan-docs fact sheet's teaching angle; the sort itself is original.

**Framework 51.2 Event of default severity grid** (`fw:eod-severity-grid`), original. Purpose: compare drafts and markups of the EoD clause. For each EoD record: trigger; materiality qualifier; grace or cure period; threshold amount; whether it is a Default during the grace period; remedy available (drawstop only, cash trap, acceleration); who can waive (Majority, all lenders, ECA). Score each EoD from 1 (hair trigger) to 4 (heavily softened); compare sponsor and lender markups; negotiate the gaps that matter (usually cross-default thresholds, MAE, project document termination, permit loss). When to use: every EoD negotiation and every new-deal credit review.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: first-read checklist of a CTA (ratio definitions, EoDs, distributions, CPs, permitted debt, change of control, voting, cure, hedging policy and ISDA schedule elections, waiver mechanics).
- Red flags: "as determined by the Agent" without a dispute mechanism; ratio definitions that count equity cures as CFADS indefinitely; a cross-default threshold below a single monthly O&M invoice; an MAE event of default without objective tests; CPs that depend on a government act with no longstop; "no Event of Default" in the distribution conditions (from the lender's chair); unanimous consent for routine permitted-debt baskets; an ISDA schedule that lets a hedge bank terminate the whole swap on any prepayment; a waiver letter with no reservation of rights.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: grace periods (payment three to five business days; other covenants 30 days, with project document defaults tracking the counterparty's cure period); cross-default thresholds sized to one to two weeks of revenue, not to round numbers; equity cures limited to two or three over the life and never consecutive. Each rule states the market and period where it holds (mark as indicative per D-011, for example "English-law project financings, 2018 to 2025, indicative") and when it breaks.
- Common mistakes: negotiating the ratio level and ignoring the definitions; forgetting that LDs and insurance proceeds flow through the definitions; letting the model and the CTA define CFADS differently; leaving the hedging policy in a side letter; forgetting to restore the hedge ratio after a mandatory prepayment.
- Questions experts ask: Which definition would I change to move this covenant by 0.05x? Which CP is in a third party's hands? What happens on the day after an EoD before anyone accelerates? Which matters need the ECA? What happens to the swaps if we prepay a third of the debt?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are sponsor's counsel on an illustrative 360 MW CCGT near Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, for which financing is to be signed on Friday, June 12, 2026 (do not name or imply a real project; sponsors and lenders fictional). Nine days before signing, the lenders add a material adverse effect event of default ("any event that in the opinion of the Majority Lenders has or is reasonably likely to have a Material Adverse Effect"). The sponsor's investment committee meets in four days. The rest of the EoD list is agreed.
**Reasoning it through.** What the MAE EoD does in a non-recourse deal (subjective trigger, drawstop leverage during construction); what lenders fear (an event the specific EoDs miss, such as a sovereign rating collapse); what sponsors fear (drawstop used to reprice); the options: delete (rarely achieved with ECA or DFI lenders), objectivize ("reasonably" plus specific limbs: ability to pay, validity of security, project documents), restrict to construction, require all-lender or super-majority decision, make it a drawstop only, not an acceleration trigger. Expert answer: accept an objective MAE limited to the project company's ability to perform payment obligations and the validity of the finance documents, operating only as a drawstop before completion, with Majority Lenders acting reasonably; trade it for a longer grace on project document defaults.
**What would change the answer.** An investment-grade offtaker and sovereign guarantee (push harder for deletion); a lender group dominated by ECAs that cannot sign without it (accept drawstop-only); a construction period longer than three years (lenders' need is real); a precedent from the same lenders on the same program (cite it).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** Chapter 51 gave the lenders rights; this chapter gives them control. It teaches why project lenders take security over everything (to control the project and keep other creditors out, not to sell a power plant for scrap), what the package contains, how common-law and civil-law systems deliver it (security trusts and floating charges against pledges, the accessory principle, parallel debt and the statutory security agents that OHADA and France have legislated), how security is perfected and ranked and when it can be unwound in a hardening period, and how enforcement keeps the project running. It then teaches the accounts structure and the cash waterfall in full as legal machinery: which accounts exist, who may sign on them, how every dollar moves from the offtaker to the distribution account, where the sweep and distributions sit and who wins from each position, and how special flows (insurance, compensation, liquidated damages, cures, disposals) are routed.

**What you will be able to do** (binding):
- Design a security package for a project in a common-law or civil-law jurisdiction and say what each element is for and how it is perfected (Capabilities 6 and 8).
- Compare going-concern enforcement with liquidation and explain why the share pledge and step-in come first (Capabilities 6 and 11).
- Draft and audit an accounts structure and a priority of payments, place the sweep and distributions from the sponsor's or the lenders' chair, and trace any receipt through it to its destination (Capabilities 4 and 6).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned

- Purpose of security in project finance: control, protection against third-party creditors, leverage in restructuring, step-in enablement; contrasted with liquidation recovery.
- The security package: share pledge over the project company, fixed assets and land rights, receivables, bank accounts, assignment by way of security of project contracts and insurances (the term itself is Chapter 10's, `ssec:10.8.1`), intellectual property and licenses where assignable, subordination and assignment of shareholder loans, sponsor-level security; what cannot be secured (some permits, public land).
- Common-law security: fixed and floating charges, the security trust and the security trustee, appointment of a receiver or administrator as a lender tool; the English project-finance exception that preserves administrative receivership (Insolvency Act 1986 s.72E: debt incurred or expected of at least GBP 50 million and lender step-in rights; fact sheet t-security-civil-law).
- Civil-law security: pledges (nantissements; the generic term is Chapter 10's) over shares, receivables, accounts and the business as a going concern; mortgages over registered land rights; the accessory principle; the parallel debt undertaking and the security agent; statutory security agents holding security in their own name in a segregated estate (OHADA Uniform Act on Securities of December 15, 2010, in force May 15, 2011, arts 5 to 11; France, Civil Code arts 2488-6 to 2488-12 from October 1, 2017); the French fiducie as a trust-like transfer.
- Perfection and priority: registration, notice and acknowledgment, control agreements, possession; hardening periods with the two verified examples (England: a floating charge to an unconnected party within 12 months of the onset of insolvency is invalid except for new value, IA 1986 s.245; France: the date of cessation of payments may be set up to 18 months before the opening judgment, and security for pre-existing debt in that period is void, Commercial Code arts L631-8 and L632-1); preferential claims that outrank security (principles); cost and timing of perfection as a closing issue.
- Enforcement as a going concern (principles): share pledge enforcement by sale or appropriation, step-in through direct agreements (mechanics owned by Chapter 28), receivership or administration where available, asset sale; constraints from concession consents and insolvency moratoria (process owned by Chapter 64).
- The accounts structure: proceeds or revenue account, operating account, debt service account, DSRA, MMRA, insurance proceeds account, compensation account, distribution account, lock-up account, disbursement and equity accounts in construction; onshore and offshore accounts as architecture (glossary home of offshore account); the accounts agreement, account bank, signing rights, block notices, permitted investments.
- The cash waterfall (priority of payments) in full: order and rationale of each step; where the cash sweep and distributions sit and the sponsor and lender drafts of that position; timing between monthly and semiannual flows; special flows: insurance proceeds routed by thresholds (the reinstatement test itself is `ssec:27.6.4`), compensation proceeds, performance and delay LD receipts, equity cure amounts, disposal proceeds; drafting traps.
- Frameworks: Security package control test (Framework 52.1); Trace-a-dollar test for a priority of payments (Framework 52.2).

## 6. Worked examples

All Illustrative; every named party is fictional. Numbers verified in Python on October 3, 2026.

**Example 52.1 Going-concern against liquidation recovery (Illustrative).** Scenario: PT Merak Energi Gas (fictional), a 310 MW coal-to-gas converted plant in Banten, Indonesia, in payment default in March 2025 with senior debt of USD 407.3 million. Going-concern sale of the shares under a share pledge: a buyer values CFADS of USD 52.3 million a year for the 14 remaining PPA years at 9.75%: enterprise value USD 390.6 million (390.58), recovery 95.9% of debt before costs. Liquidation of the equipment after PPA termination: realizable USD 96.4 million less costs USD 11.8 million = USD 84.6 million, recovery 20.8%. Lesson: security is valuable because it lets lenders sell or run the going concern, which depends on the PPA surviving the enforcement (direct agreement, Chapter 28). Simplifications stated: flat CFADS, no taxes on sale, buyer's discount rate given.

**Example 52.2 Parallel debt and the distribution of enforcement proceeds (Illustrative).** Scenario: Dobrogea Vânt S.R.L. (fictional), a 280 MW wind portfolio in Constanța County, Romania, financed in 2020 under English-law finance documents whose onshore security is held by a security agent through a parallel debt undertaking (the example asserts nothing about how any jurisdiction treats parallel debt; fact sheet t-security-civil-law, "Do not state"). Senior claims at enforcement in October 2025: ECA-covered tranche USD 182.6 million, DFI tranche USD 134.1 million, commercial tranche USD 231.7 million, hedge close-out USD 12.9 million; total USD 561.3 million. Parallel debt owed to the security agent equals USD 561.3 million. Onshore enforcement proceeds USD 421.9 million less enforcement costs USD 6.2 million = USD 415.7 million, distributed pro rata (assuming hedges rank pari passu; Chapter 53 owns the alternatives) by Equation 52.1: ECA 135.2, DFI 99.3, commercial 171.6, hedge 9.6 (9.55); recovery 74.1%. Each payment discharges the parallel debt and the corresponding principal claim by the same amount; parallel debt remaining USD 145.6 million. **Example 52.3 One semiannual period through the waterfall (Illustrative).** Scenario: Cañada Rosalejo Solar S.L. **Example 52.5 Where the sweep sits: one period under two drafts (Illustrative).** (New; appended label `ex:52.5`.) Same project and period as Example 52.3. ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 52.1 Security package control test** (`fw:security-control-test`), original. Purpose: judge a security package by what it lets lenders control, not by its length. For each asset ask: (1) on a default, who controls it and how fast; (2) can it be enforced without stopping the plant; (3) is it perfected against third parties and in insolvency, and is any part of it inside a hardening period; (4) what consents does enforcement need; (5) what does it cost to take and keep. Mark gaps; fix the gaps that affect the going concern first (shares, project contracts, accounts, land rights). When to use: term sheet stage and legal due diligence.

**Framework 52.2 Trace-a-dollar test** (`fw:trace-a-dollar`), original. Purpose: audit a priority of payments. Steps: take each category of receipt (tariff, insurance, LDs, compensation, cure, disposal, VAT refund, hedge receipt), trace it through each account to its end use; check that every step has a clause, an actor and a date; check that model and document agree; list any receipt with no route. When to use: drafting and model audit (cross-reference Chapter 44).

## 9. Real cases

- **t-security-civil-law** (delivered): OHADA Uniform Act on Securities (adopted December 15, 2010; in force May 15, 2011; arts 5 to 11 on the agent des sûretés); France's agent des sûretés (Ordonnance 2017-748, in force October 1, 2017; Civil Code arts 2488-6 to 2488-12, amended by the PACTE law with effect from May 24, 2019) and the fiducie (Civil Code art. 2011, Law 2007-211); England's s.72A abolition of administrative receivership for qualifying floating charges created on or after September 15, 2003, with the s.72E project-finance exception; hardening periods under IA 1986 ss.240 and 245 and French Commercial Code arts L631-8 and L632-1. Lesson: the security agent's legal form decides whether security survives a changing syndicate and the agent's own insolvency; late security is the most exposed. - **sh130**: the 2016 plan cancelled the sponsors' equity and gave the lenders new membership interests (plan effective June 28, 2017); USDOT converted its TIFIA loan into about 34% of the equity, a USD 15 million payment and an USD 87 million subordinated loan. ## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: security package by asset class; perfection steps and deadlines; acknowledgments needed from each counterparty; accounts opened and mandates signed; block notice templates agreed; permitted investments list; security agent appointment checked against the local statute's mandatory contents where one exists (for example OHADA AUS art. 6).
- Red flags: receivables pledged without counterparty notice where local law requires it; accounts outside the security net (petty cash creep); land rights that cannot be mortgaged (for example an unregistered lease); permits that lapse on change of control; a waterfall that pays subordinated or affiliate amounts before reserve top-ups; model and accounts agreement disagreeing on timing; top-up security taken during a waiver for existing debt inside a hardening period.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: take security over everything, but spend negotiating capital on the shares, the contracts, the accounts and the land; perfection items that need a government act belong on the CP list or a short CS list with a longstop; parallel debt is common in civil-law deals without a statutory agent, but its insolvency recognition is a legal-opinion point in every new jurisdiction (indicative, practice in EMEA civil-law project financings 2015 to 2025).
- Common mistakes: treating security as a sale-value calculation; forgetting the hedge receipts and VAT refunds in the waterfall; leaving the insurance proceeds threshold undefined in currency; mismatched test dates between the CTA and the accounts agreement.
- Questions experts ask: If we enforce on Monday, who runs the plant on Tuesday? Which consents does a share-pledge sale need? Which account receives the offtaker's payment if the offtaker ignores the notice? What can the account bank refuse to do?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are lenders' counsel on an illustrative 450 MW combined-cycle plant in an unnamed civil-law state whose codes follow the French model (Illustrative; imply no real country's registry). The target close is Thursday, November 19, 2026. Five days before, local counsel reports that registration of the mortgage over the land lease will take five months because the registry needs a ministerial certificate. The sponsor asks you to close and treat the mortgage as a condition subsequent; the EPC contractor's price validity expires in ten days (expiry would cost about USD 4.6 million in escalation, a figure given in the drill as Illustrative).
**Reasoning it through.** What the mortgage protects (the land under the plant; step-in and enforcement depend on it); what else covers the gap (share pledge over a company that holds the lease right; direct agreement with the lessor; the government's undertaking); the cost of not closing; the hardening point (a mortgage registered months after close secures money advanced at close: is it "new value" under the local insolvency law, or exposed as security for existing debt?); options: close with a CS and a longstop, a drawstop on drawdowns beyond a cap until registration, a sponsor guarantee of the gap, a legal opinion on interim priority and on hardening. Expert answer: close with the mortgage as a CS with a 180-day longstop, drawdowns capped at the equity-funded and advance-payment amount until registration, a lessor acknowledgment delivered at close, and a local-counsel opinion that the delayed registration is not exposed in a suspect period.
**What would change the answer.** A registry with a record of losing priority to later filings; an insolvency hardening period that the delay would put the mortgage inside; a lessor with power to terminate the lease on change of control; ECA cover conditional on perfected security at first drawdown.

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** A modern project financing has creditors whose interests diverge exactly when things go wrong: an ECA-covered lender who is paid by its insurer, an uncovered bank that is not, a DFI with policy obligations and preferred creditor status, hedge banks whose claim swings with interest and exchange rates, bondholders who vote slowly through a trustee, mezzanine lenders and Islamic financiers who own assets rather than lend against them. This chapter teaches how the intercreditor agreement settles ranking, sharing, voting, entrenched rights, standstills and enforcement among them, and how new creditors accede later. It is the document that decides who controls the project after a default.

**What you will be able to do** (binding):
- Design an intercreditor decision matrix for a multi-tranche financing and compute how any decision is carried or blocked (Capabilities 5 and 6).
- Explain the rights an ECA, a DFI, a hedge counterparty and a bondholder group need and what each will trade (Capabilities 5 and 8).
- Compute recoveries by class under different ranking, sharing and standstill arrangements (Capability 11).

**Example 53.2 Mezzanine recovery under subordination (Illustrative).** Scenario: Pecos Peaking Power LLC (fictional), a 437 MW gas peaker in Reeves County, Texas, sold on enforcement in 2025. ## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: creditor classes; vote counting for each; entrenched matters; individual policy rights; hedge ranking and voting (swaps and currency hedges); standstill periods; enforcement instruction mechanics; accession conditions; application of proceeds.
- Red flags: a class that can block every waiver; hedge close-out super senior without a cap tied to the hedging policy; bondholder votes needing a meeting with a long notice for routine waivers; DFI policy rights drafted so widely that they block enforcement; no deemed-consent mechanism for slow voters; no rule for what happens when an ECA pays a claim and is subrogated.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: covered lenders vote through their ECA; B-loan participants vote through the lender of record; hedge banks vote only after close-out and only for their close-out amount; juniors stand still for a defined period (often 90 to 180 days, indicative, EMEA and US project financings 2015 to 2025) unless seniors enforce first.
- Common mistakes: drafting the ICA after the CTA is final; forgetting the standby facility, the VAT facility and the currency hedges in the ranking; treating bondholders like a bank in the timetable for consents.
- Questions experts ask: Who can stop a waiver? Who can force an enforcement? What happens to the hedges on a prepayment? Can a new creditor join without unanimous consent?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are the intercreditor agent on an illustrative 820 MW wind portfolio in the Aegean region of Turkey financed in 2021 by an ECA-covered tranche (35%), a DFI A/B loan (30%) and commercial banks (35%) (sponsors and lenders fictional). A DSCR event of default occurred on the December 31, 2025 test date because of a one-off grid curtailment; the commercial banks and the DFI want to waive it for a 0.25% fee; the ECA says it needs eight weeks and its ministry's sign-off. The next payment date, when the distribution conditions will be tested, is in ten days. The ICA requires a super-majority of 66⅔% for financial covenant waivers and ECA consent for any waiver while ECA-covered loans are outstanding.
**Reasoning it through.** What happens if nothing is done (no acceleration is likely; cash is trapped; the risk is a cross-default under the turbine supplier's service contract if the waiver is not given); options: a short standstill letter signed by all classes pending ECA approval; a waiver by the other classes with an ECA reservation; a covenant holiday conditional on ECA consent; restructuring the ask to fit the ECA's delegated authority (a waiver below a size the ECA's underwriter can sign). Expert answer: a 60-day standstill and reservation of rights signed by all, plus a waiver request drafted to fit the ECA's delegated authority.
**What would change the answer.** The breach being recurring, not one-off; the ECA having already paid a claim (subrogation changes who votes); a hedge bank threatening to terminate; the sponsor offering an equity cure.

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** Every contract in the web names a law and a forum, and those choices decide whether a right on paper becomes cash. This chapter teaches how to choose governing law for each document, when to litigate and when to arbitrate, how to choose a seat and rules (ICC, LCIA, ICSID, UNCITRAL, SIAC, in their current versions), how awards are enforced under the New York and ICSID Conventions, how sovereign immunity and its waiver work at the suit and execution stages, what stabilization and economic-equilibrium clauses can and cannot do, and how investment treaties protect foreign investors and how sponsors structure to obtain that protection. The method throughout is to work backwards from where the counterparty's assets are.

**What you will be able to do** (binding):
- Choose governing law and a dispute forum for each contract in a project's web by working backwards from enforcement (Capabilities 6 and 8).
- Draft an arbitration clause, an immunity waiver and a stabilization clause, and say where each usually lands (Capability 6).
- Test whether an investor's structure is protected by an investment treaty and what a claim or award is worth after time, cost and collection risk (Capabilities 8 and 15).

## 5. Opening

On March 4, 2026 the UK Supreme Court held unanimously, in *Kingdom of Spain v Infrastructure Services Luxembourg* and *Republic of Zimbabwe v Border Timbers* ([2026] UKSC 9), that a state that has agreed to Article 54(1) of the ICSID Convention cannot claim immunity from the registration of an ICSID award in the UK, while immunity from execution remains. Five weeks later, on April 8, 2026, the High Court of Australia held in *CCDM Holdings v Republic of India* ([2026] HCA 9) that ratifying the New York Convention does not by itself waive a state's immunity. Open on that pair (fact sheet t-arbitration-treaties): two investors holding awards against states, one able to turn its award into a judgment and the other stopped at the courthouse door, because of a choice made in a treaty and a contract years before. Then the rule: choose law and forum by working backwards from enforcement.

**Example 54.1 Expected recovery under three forums (Illustrative).** Scenario: Kerio Gorge Hydro Ltd (fictional), the project company of a 600 MW hydro plant in an unnamed East African state that is party to both Conventions, holds a USD 186.4 million claim, filed in January 2026, against the state-owned offtaker; discount rate 11% a year. Options: (a) host-country courts: probability of winning 0.55, years to cash 6.5, costs USD 3.2 million, probability of collecting a judgment 0.40; (b) ICC arbitration seated in Paris: win 0.70, 3.5 years, costs USD 9.8 million, collection 0.55 (New York Convention plus an express immunity waiver); (c) ICSID: win 0.65 times survival of annulment 0.85 = 0.5525, 4.5 years, costs USD 12.1 million, collection 0.60. Present value of expected recovery less costs: (a) USD 17.6 million (expected cash 41.0, PV 20.8); (b) USD 40.0 million (expected 71.8, PV 49.8); (c) USD 26.5 million (expected 61.8, PV 38.6). Equation 54.1: $\mathrm{EV} = C \times p_{\mathrm{win}} \times p_{\mathrm{collect}}/(1+r)^{T} - K$. Teaching point: time and collection probability move the answer more than the probability of winning; the ICSID route overtakes ICC only if its collection probability rises to about 0.81 (Exercise 54.9). Simplifications stated: costs incurred upfront; no interest on the award; no cost recovery from the losing side.

**Example 54.2 Award value with interest (Illustrative).** Scenario: an ICC award of USD 72.3 million issued on June 30, 2024 in favor of Bahari Wind Ltd (fictional) against the same unnamed state's utility, carrying interest at 5.85% a year compounded semiannually and collected on December 31, 2029, 5.5 years later: USD 99.3 million (99.28). **Example 54.6 The cost of a forum mismatch between a PPA and its guarantee (Illustrative).** (New; appended label `ex:54.6`, placed at step 9 of the walkthrough in `sec:54.7`.) Scenario: Mto Mawe Hydro Ltd (fictional), a 180 MW hydro IPP in an unnamed East African state, claims a USD 47.3 million termination payment in March 2026. If the PPA and the government guarantee share an ICC seat with consolidation: one proceeding, costs USD 6.4 million, 3.0 years to cash, probability of collecting 0.62. If the guarantee sends disputes to the host state's courts: two proceedings, extra costs USD 2.9 million, 4.5 years to cash, and a collection probability that falls to 0.48 because the two forums can reach inconsistent findings. At an 11% discount rate (Equation 54.1): aligned forums USD 15.0 million; mismatched forums USD 4.9 million; the mismatch costs USD 10.1 million of expected value. Teaching point: forum alignment across the web is worth money that can be computed at the drafting stage.

## 7. Walkthroughs

**Walkthrough 54.7: drafting the dispute clauses for a project's contract set.** Artifact: the illustrative contract web of Exhibit 54.1 plus a blank clause. Steps: (1) list each counterparty and where its assets are; (2) for each contract, choose governing law (mandatory local law first), and choose the law of each arbitration agreement expressly; (3) choose the forum for each by Framework 54.1; (4) align the seats, rules and language so that related disputes can be joined, consolidated or coordinated; add expert determination for technical disputes with a deadline; (5) add the state's consent to ICSID jurisdiction in the IA, with the agreement that the foreign-controlled local project company is treated as a national of another Contracting State (the Convention's foreign-control provision), and an UNCITRAL fallback if ICSID jurisdiction fails; (6) add the waiver of immunity from suit and execution, with the list of assets the waiver reaches; (7) check that direct agreements and the guarantee follow the same forum; (8) check the finance documents' asymmetric clause; (9) run a "Who pays if...?" style trace of one dispute (cross-reference Chapter 28's framework) through the forums to confirm nothing falls between them, and price the cost of any mismatch (Example 54.6). ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 54.1 Enforcement-first forum choice** (`fw:enforcement-first-forum`), original, following the teaching angle in fact sheet t-arbitration-treaties. Steps: (1) where are the counterparty's assets, and are they commercial assets; (2) is the counterparty a state, a state entity, or private; (3) which Conventions bind the asset states; (4) is there an express waiver of immunity from suit and from execution; (5) what is the time to cash and the cost in each forum; (6) what is the annulment or set-aside risk at the candidate seats; (7) choose the forum with the best expected recovery (Equation 54.1), then make the clause consistent across the web. When to use: drafting every project contract with a state or foreign counterparty.

**Framework 54.2 Treaty protection test** (`fw:treaty-protection-test`), original. Steps: (1) is there a treaty in force between the host state and the investor's state at the date of each investment; (2) is the claimant an investor (nationality, denial-of-benefits clause, substance); (3) is the asset an investment under the treaty's definition; (4) what standards apply (FET, expropriation, umbrella, transfer); (5) what consent to arbitration (ICSID, UNCITRAL, other) and preconditions (waiting periods, local litigation); (6) carve-outs (tax, necessity, non-precluded measures); (7) sunset and termination status; (8) intra-EU or similar objections. When to use: structuring the holding chain before the investment and re-testing before any restructuring.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: governing law and forum per contract; express law of each arbitration agreement; seat and rules aligned; expert determination for technical issues; joinder, consolidation and coordination; ICSID consent and foreign-control agreement; immunity waiver from suit and execution; treaty chain tested at each investment date; sunset and denial-of-benefits review; third-party funding disclosure rules of the chosen institution.
- Red flags: a state counterparty with a local-seat arbitration and local-court review; an immunity waiver from suit only; a holding company inserted after a dispute is foreseeable; forum mismatch between the PPA and the guarantee; an expert-determination clause with no deadline; an intra-EU structure relied on for treaty protection; a New York-law contract with a London seat and no express choice for the arbitration agreement.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: finance documents under English or New York law with an asymmetric clause; state contracts under local law with international arbitration; ICSID where available, UNCITRAL as fallback; stabilization by compensation, not exemption (indicative, emerging-market IPPs and PPPs 2010 to 2026).
- Common mistakes: choosing a seat for convenience rather than its courts' record; forgetting that execution immunity survives a waiver of suit; assuming a tax treaty is an investment treaty; drafting the GSA and PPA forums independently.
- Questions experts ask: Where would we enforce, against which assets, and how long would it take? Does the guarantee follow the PPA's forum? What did the host state's courts do the last time a foreign award came home?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are sponsor's counsel on an illustrative 300 MW solar-plus-storage IPP in an unnamed Central Asian state (sponsors and lenders fictional). Two weeks before the IA is due to be signed on Tuesday, April 14, 2026, the ministry refuses ICSID arbitration and offers arbitration seated in the capital under the national arbitration law, with review by the national courts, and no waiver of immunity from execution. The lenders' ECA and DFI term sheets require "internationally recognized arbitration outside the host country". The host state is party to the New York Convention and to ICSID.
**Reasoning it through.** What the lenders need (a forum whose award they can enforce abroad; the DFI's halo); what the ministry fears (sovereignty optics; precedent for every later IPP); options: a neutral seat under UNCITRAL Rules administered by an institution; ICSID with a cooling-off and amicable settlement tier; local seat but ICC Rules with a narrow court review and an execution waiver limited to commercial assets; reliance on a BIT for the sponsor (not enough for lenders). Expert answer: propose UNCITRAL Rules with a neutral seat and the Permanent Court of Arbitration as appointing authority, a six-month negotiation tier, and a waiver of immunity from suit and from execution against commercial assets; trade the location of hearings and the language for the seat.
**What would change the answer.** A DFI that is itself a lender of record (halo raises the cost to the state of breach); a BIT with consent to ICSID (sponsor protection independent of the IA); a guaranteeing ministry with no commercial assets abroad (execution waiver worth less; PRI matters more, Chapter 60); a recent episode of the host courts enjoining arbitration.

## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 55.1 Financing strategy selector** (`fw:financing-strategy-selector`), original. Questions in order: (1) Does the sponsor need certainty of funds at bid or signing (yes points to underwriting or committed club)? (2) Is construction risk material and the investor base for construction-risk bonds thin (points to banks or ECA/DFI-led)? (3) Is the size beyond the club's appetite (points to underwriting plus syndication, or multi-source)? (4) Are ECA or DFI lenders available and needed for country risk (points to an ECA/DFI-anchored structure)? (5) Is the asset rated investment grade or close, and is a long fixed rate valuable (points to a bond or private placement, possibly after construction; `fw:bond-or-loan-test`)? (6) What does delay cost against fee savings, and where are the banks in their budget year? Output: a ranked strategy with the reason. When to use: at mandate and when market conditions change.

**Framework 55.2 Critical path to close** (`fw:critical-path-to-close`), established method (the critical path method from project scheduling, credited as an established technique; no attribution details unless a fact sheet verifies them), applied to financings. Steps: list tasks, durations, dependencies; forward pass for earliest dates; backward pass for latest dates; float; identify the critical path; assign owners; re-run weekly; spend money only on critical-path tasks.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: strategy memo; role allocation; IM and model frozen versions; credit approval validity dates and conditions; syndication timetable against bank budget cycles; issues list owner; CP tracker; signature logistics; funds flow; hedge execution plan (interest rate and currency); closing memorandum; CS tracker.
- Red flags: credit approvals expiring before the realistic close date; an approval condition that depends on a report not yet delivered; an IM and model that disagree; CPs owned by third parties with no escalation path; a hedge executed before CP satisfaction is certain; a funds flow that depends on same-day cross-border wires after cut-off; a CS list longer than one page.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: start the CP tracker at mandate, not at signing; freeze the model and base case before credit committees, not after; plan for each government consent to take twice the promised time (indicative, emerging-market IPPs 2010 to 2025); keep the critical path visible weekly.
- Common mistakes: treating legal documents as the critical path when reports and approvals are; forgetting KYC on new shareholders; not budgeting for re-approval after a material change; executing swaps in a market spike to meet a date.
- Questions experts ask: Which three tasks set the close date? What is the cost of one week's delay? Who can sign each CP off? What happens if one bank's approval lapses on closing day?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are the MLA on an illustrative 700 MW CCGT on the Gulf of Suez, Egypt, with six banks (sponsors and banks fictional). Two days before financial close, set for Wednesday, September 16, 2026, one bank (11% of the commercial tranche) tells you its credit approval expired yesterday and re-approval needs two weeks. The sponsor's EPC price validity expires in five days; extension would cost USD 1.9 million (Illustrative). The swaps are scheduled to be executed at close.
**Reasoning it through.** Options: close without the bank (the other banks increase pro rata, needing their own approvals; or the MLA takes the share on its book temporarily, needing its own approval); close with a delayed accession of the bank (commitment signed later, its share funded by others in the meantime); delay close two weeks and pay for the EPC extension; partial close. Consider hedge execution (do not execute swaps for a notional that may not fund), CP satisfaction certainty, and the relationship. Expert answer: close on time with the MLA's temporary hold under its standing approval, if available, and an accession mechanism for the lapsed bank within 30 days; execute swaps on the full notional only if the MLA's hold is confirmed.
**What would change the answer.** The lapsed bank's approval being uncertain rather than procedural; the MLA lacking headroom; an ECA policy naming each covered lender; a sponsor able to provide an equity bridge for the gap.

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** Every number and clause in Chapters 35 to 55 was the outcome of a negotiation. This chapter teaches the reader to negotiate them: to know what "market" means for a term and where the evidence for it comes from, to know the levers each party holds, to convert any term into money in a common unit so trades can be compared, to sequence a negotiation and escalate it without losing the deal, to read how terms move with the cycle, and to adapt all of this to governments, contractors and offtakers, whose constraints differ from lenders'. The centerpiece is a full term-sheet markup walkthrough in which the reader sees an arranger's draft, a sponsor's markup, the response and the landing, term by term, with the reason each term lands where it does; the chapter then gives the full senior-debt term sheet in headings (Exhibit 56.6) and has the reader draft one from a deal summary.

**What you will be able to do** (binding):
- Draft a project finance term sheet from a deal summary and mark one up from the sponsor's or the lender's chair, predicting where each term lands (Capability 6).
- Convert any proposed term into its cost in debt, equity return or tariff, and use those numbers to build trades (Capabilities 5 and 6).
- Plan the sequence, concessions and escalation of a financing negotiation, and adapt them to a government, a contractor or an offtaker (Capabilities 9 and 15).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned

- What "market" means: evidence sources (closed precedents, live competing offers, surveys, public filings, rating and capital constraints), why norms differ by sector, region and cycle, and how to test a claim that a term is "market".
- Levers: competition among lenders, timing and deadlines, alternative capital, credit approvals, underwriting capacity, ECA and DFI policy, government procurement rules, contractor capacity and price, offtaker tariff and regulation.
- Converting terms into money: a common currency (debt quantum, equity IRR, NPV to sponsor, tariff in USD/kW-month or USD/MWh, expected loss to lender).
- Trade-offs on every main term: debt size (DSCR, gearing), tenor and profile, pricing and fees, hedging, reserves, sweeps and lock-ups, covenants and events of default, CPs, sponsor support, change of control, transfers, market flex.
- Negotiation method: BATNA (Fisher and Ury 1981) and reservation points, the zone of possible agreement (the analysis of reservation prices and the zone of agreement credited to Raiffa 1982; ZOPA as the later conventional label), anchoring (Tversky and Kahneman 1974) and first offers as anchors (Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001), ranges and multiple equivalent simultaneous offers as ways to anchor high without backlash (Galinsky, Rodriguez-Mincey and Grayson 2026), packaging, issues lists, sequencing (economics first or legal first), concession patterns, escalation to principals, deadlocks and how they break.
- How terms move with the market cycle: liquidity, base rates, credit events, competition, documentation creep.
- Negotiating with governments, contractors and offtakers: political and procurement constraints, precedent effects, public scrutiny, standard documents that cannot be changed (REIPPPP, Gulf IWPP programs).
- The term sheet as a drafted document and the term-sheet markup as a craft: heads, drafting notes, comment conventions, the response, the landing.
- Frameworks: Market check (Framework 56.1); Trade-off ledger (Framework 56.2); Escalation ladder (Framework 56.3).

## 5. Opening

## 6. Worked examples

All Illustrative; every named party is fictional. Simplifications stated in each. Numbers verified in Python on October 3, 2026.

**Example 56.1 A 0.05x DSCR change in debt, equity IRR, and tariff (Illustrative).** Scenario: Anwiaso Power Company Ltd (fictional), a 450 MW CCGT near Takoradi, Ghana, negotiating its term sheet in March 2026; total cost USD 846.3 million; flat CFADS USD 92.4 million a year for 20 operating years; debt is a 16-year annuity at 7.20% sized to the DSCR target, capped at 80% gearing; equity paid at time 0; no tax; no construction period (stated simplifications). Results:

| DSCR target | Debt service (USD m/yr) | Debt (USD m) | Gearing | Equity (USD m) | Equity IRR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.30x | 71.08 | 662.6 | 78.3% | 183.7 | 12.72% |
| 1.35x | 68.44 | 638.1 | 75.4% | 208.2 | 12.28% |
| 1.40x | 66.00 | 615.3 | 72.7% | 231.0 | 11.94% |

Moving from 1.35x to 1.30x adds USD 24.5 million of debt and 0.44 percentage points of equity IRR. To reach 12.72% at 1.35x instead, CFADS must rise by USD 0.76 million a year, equivalent for a 450 MW plant to USD 0.14/kW-month of capacity charge, which is the reason a sponsor should spend negotiating capital on the DSCR before the tariff. ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 56.1 Market check** (`fw:market-check`), original. Steps: (1) find three closed precedents in the same sector, region and contract type within 24 months; (2) compare with live competing offers; (3) check the counterparty's written constraints (credit policy, ECA rules, procurement law); (4) adjust for the deal's specific risk; (5) state the market range with dates and sources. When to use: before making or answering any claim that a term is "market".

**Framework 56.2 Trade-off ledger** (`fw:trade-off-ledger`), original. Steps: list every open term; estimate its value to you and its cost to the other side in money; rank by ratio; ask first for high-ratio concessions; offer first low-cost-to-you concessions that the other side values; package trades; record every concession with its money value. When to use: from first term sheet to final issues list.

**Framework 56.3 Escalation ladder** (`fw:escalation-ladder`), original. Rungs: working teams (drafting points); deal leads (structural points); principals (economics and walk-away points); credit committee or investment committee (limits); a defined walk-away. Rules: escalate an issue only with a written options paper; never escalate two rungs at once; agree before escalating what will happen if principals do not agree. When to use: when an issue has stayed open through two turns of the issues list.

Established concepts credited in text (fact sheet t-negotiation-sources): BATNA (Fisher and Ury 1981; the second edition of 1991 added Bruce Patton); reservation prices and the zone of agreement (Raiffa 1982); anchoring (Tversky and Kahneman 1974, *Science* 185); first offers as anchors (Galinsky and Mussweiler 2001, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 81(4)); flexible ambition (Galinsky, Rodriguez-Mincey and Grayson 2026, *Current Opinion in Psychology*). The critical path method appears in Chapter 55.

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You lead the sponsor team on an illustrative 650 MW CCGT in Long An province, Vietnam, with the state utility as offtaker (sponsors and arranger fictional). Ten days before your investment committee on Monday, November 9, 2026, the arranger offers to move the sizing DSCR from 1.35x to 1.30x (worth about USD 21.8 million of extra debt and 0.4 percentage points of equity IRR on your model; Illustrative figures given in the drill) if you accept a 100% cash sweep after year 7 and a fixed 90% hedge with no band. Your equity IRR is 0.3 percentage points below the committee's hurdle without the change.
**Reasoning it through.** Price each component with the trade-off ledger: the extra debt and IRR now; the sweep's cost (it cuts distributions from year 8 and effectively shortens tenor: model the IRR effect); the fixed 90% hedge's cost (over-hedging risk on prepayments and the execution charge); the lenders' cost of the DSCR change (higher expected loss) and why they want the sweep (tail risk and refinancing). Options: accept; counter with a sweep that applies only if a refinancing has not occurred by year 8 (a soft mini-perm); counter with a 75% to 100% hedge band; accept 1.32x with a 50% sweep. Expert answer: counter with a soft mini-perm sweep and a hedge band, accepting a smaller DSCR move if needed; take it to committee with the IRR computed under the sweep, not without it.
**What would change the answer.** A refinancing market that is deep and liquid (the sweep matters less); an offtaker with a weak credit (lenders' sweep is justified; accept); a competing arranger offering 1.32x without the sweep (use it); a committee that values IRR over cash yield.
