## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** By Chapter 51 the reader has sized the debt (Chapter 36), designed its covenants and reserves (Chapter 37) and priced it (Chapter 38). This chapter turns those decisions into enforceable words. It maps the full document set from mandate letter to financial close, then teaches the clauses that do the work: conditions precedent, representations, information, positive and negative covenants, financial covenants as drafted, permitted debt and distributions, events of default and remedies, equity cures, change of control, transfers, and amendments and voting. It shows which parts come from LMA, LSTA or APLMA chassis wording and which are bespoke project terms where the negotiation happens, and it trains the reader to read a common terms agreement as a system of linked definitions rather than a stack of clauses.

**What you will be able to do** (style sheet 4.1 list, two to four items):
- Map a project financing's document set and say which document holds each term and who drafts it (Capability 6).
- Read and mark up the conditions precedent, covenants, events of default and equity cure in a common terms agreement, and compute what each costs the sponsor (Capabilities 6 and 10).
- Draft a permitted-debt test, a distribution condition and an event of default with grace periods and materiality set where the market sets them (Capability 6).
- Spot the linked definitions that change a ratio covenant's meaning before signing (Capability 15).

## 6. Worked examples

All Illustrative unless stated. Numbers verified in Python.

**Example 51.1 Sorting terms between the common terms agreement and the facility agreements (Illustrative).** Scenario: a 418 MW gas-fired plant in Bangladesh financed by three tranches: an ECA-covered loan, a DFI loan and an uncovered commercial loan. Fourteen terms to place: margin; commitment fee; availability period; final maturity; repayment profile (common sculpted profile); conditions precedent to first drawdown; information covenants; DSCR covenants; events of default; ECA premium payment mechanics; DFI E&S policy covenants (ESAP compliance); DFI sanctions and anti-corruption policy undertaking; tax gross-up; mandatory prepayment events. Result: common in the CTA: CPs, information covenants, DSCR covenants, EoDs, mandatory prepayment events, repayment profile; tranche-specific in facility agreements: margin, commitment fee, availability (if different), ECA premium mechanics, tax gross-up (because withholding exemptions differ by lender type); DFI policy undertakings sit in the CTA but are entrenched as DFI individual rights (forward reference Chapter 53). Final maturity: common if the profile is common; the writer explains the exception where an ECA tenor rule forces a shorter ECA tranche.

**Example 51.2 A cost-to-complete test on a drawdown request (Illustrative).** Scenario: 212 MW wind farm in Vietnam, month 19 of 28. Inputs: remaining budgeted costs to complete per IE USD 143.7 million; IE's estimate of cost to complete USD 151.2 million; undrawn senior commitments USD 118.4 million; undrawn committed equity USD 26.9 million; contingency remaining USD 4.1 million (already inside the budget). Test: sources available (118.4 + 26.9 = 145.3) against IE cost to complete (151.2): shortfall USD 5.9 million, so the drawdown condition fails. Remedies: sponsor funds an additional equity commitment of USD 5.9 million (or a standby LC) before the drawdown; or lenders waive by Majority decision if the CTA allows. Show that the test uses the IE's estimate, not the sponsor's budget (the drafting point).

**Example 51.3 An incurrence test for additional senior debt (Illustrative).** Scenario: an operating 286 MW solar-plus-storage project in Chile wants USD 45.0 million of additional senior debt to fund an augmentation, 7-year annuity at 6.85% a year (annual periods for simplicity). CFADS for the next seven years: 58.4, 60.1, 61.7, 59.8, 62.9, 64.3, 63.5 (USD m). Existing debt service USD 39.6 million a year. Permitted-debt test: projected minimum DSCR pro forma at least 1.40x (the level Port Arthur LNG's filing uses for supplemental senior debt; state it as that deal's term, not a norm). Results: existing minimum DSCR 1.47x (58.4 / 39.6); new annuity USD 8.31 million a year; pro forma debt service 47.91; pro forma minimum DSCR 1.22x: fails. Maximum additional debt that passes: headroom 58.4 / 1.40 - 39.6 = USD 2.11 million a year, PV at 6.85% over 7 years = USD 11.5 million (11.45). Equation 51.1: maximum additional debt $= \min_t(\mathrm{CFADS}_t / \mathrm{DSCR}^{\mathrm{inc}} - \mathrm{DS}^{\mathrm{exist}}_t) \times$ annuity factor. Excel row-based implementation on a Ratios-style sheet (writer gives the formula, e.g. `=MIN(J12:P12/$F$20-J14:P14)*PV(F21,7,-1)` entered as an array or via a helper row; prefer a helper row per style sheet 5.4).

**Example 51.4 Testing a distribution request (Illustrative).** Scenario: 96 MW run-of-river hydro plant in Georgia (the country), semiannual test date. Facts: historic DSCR = 47.62 / 38.71 = 1.23x (passes a 1.20x condition); projected DSCR = 45.10 / 37.88 = 1.19x (fails 1.20x); DSRA fully funded; MMRA short by USD 0.40 million; the audited accounts were delivered 12 days late and the 30-day grace period has not expired, so a Default (not an Event of Default) is continuing. Result: three conditions fail (projected DSCR, MMRA, no Default). The lesson: a sponsor that negotiated "no Event of Default" instead of "no Default" would have passed the third condition; lenders draft "no Default" so cash cannot leave during a grace period.

**Example 51.5 Equity cure arithmetic (Illustrative).** Scenario: 164 MW onshore wind farm in Brazil, 12-month historic DSCR test. Inputs: CFADS USD 41.37 million; scheduled principal USD 24.60 million; interest USD 14.32 million; debt service USD 38.92 million; debt outstanding at the start of the test period USD 268.40 million; interest rate 5.35% a year; default level 1.10x. Results: DSCR 1.06x (1.063). CFADS cure (Equation 51.2): $1.10 \times 38.92 - 41.37 = $ USD 1.44 million. Prepayment cure applied pro rata to remaining installments and deemed made at the start of the period (Equation 51.3): required debt service 41.37 / 1.10 = 37.61; reduction needed 1.31; reduction per dollar prepaid = 24.60 / 268.40 + 0.0535 = 0.1452; cure amount USD 9.03 million. Prepayment applied in inverse order of maturity: only interest falls, so the cure is 1.31 / 0.0535 = USD 24.50 million. Check: 41.37 / (38.92 - 9.03 x 0.1452) = 1.10x. Teaching point: the same breach costs USD 1.4 million, USD 9.0 million or USD 24.5 million depending on two drafting choices (CFADS versus prepayment cure, and the application order), which is why sponsors and lenders fight over the definition, not the ratio.

**Example 51.6 Voting arithmetic in a seven-bank club (Illustrative).** Commitments USD m: 112.5, 87.0, 74.3, 61.8, 48.2, 35.6, 22.9; total 442.3. Majority Lenders defined as more than 66⅔%: need more than USD 294.9 million. The three largest hold 273.8 (61.9%): not enough; the four largest hold 335.6 (75.9%). Blocking stake: at least 33⅓% (USD 147.4 million); the two largest together (199.5, 45.1%) block; lenders 3, 4 and 7 (159.0, 35.9%) block. With a defaulting-lender clause disenfranchising lender 2 (87.0), the denominator falls to 355.3 and the threshold to 236.9: lenders 1, 3 and 4 (248.6) now pass. Shares: 25.4%, 19.7%, 16.8%, 14.0%, 10.9%, 8.0%, 5.2%.

## 7. Walkthroughs

**Walkthrough 51.8: reading a common terms agreement in one sitting.** Artifact: an illustrative CTA table of contents (Exhibit 51.7: clause number, clause name, what to check, link to other clauses). Steps:
1. Read the definitions of the ratios (CFADS, Debt Service, Historic DSCR, Projected DSCR, LLCR) and the calculation clause first, with the model open; list every exclusion and every "as determined by the Agent".
2. Read the events of default next, marking grace periods and materiality qualifiers on the severity grid (Framework 51.2).
3. Read the distribution conditions and compare each with the lock-up design of Chapter 37: check "Default" versus "Event of Default", test dates, and which reserves must be full.
4. Read the CP schedule against the project's actual status, flagging items that cannot be satisfied by the target date (feeds Chapter 55's tracker).
5. Read permitted debt, permitted security and permitted disposals together, then the negative pledge, to see what the sponsor can do without consent.
6. Read change of control and transfers against the shareholders' agreement (Chapter 26).
7. Read amendments and voting, and list the entrenched matters.
8. Read the equity cure and its interaction with the ratio definitions (does a cure count as CFADS for later tests?).
9. Finish with the boilerplate (tax gross-up, increased costs, agency, set-off) using the Boilerplate-or-bargain sort to decide what needs only a check against the chassis.
10. Write a one-page issues list ranked by money at stake (format shown).

## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 51.1 Boilerplate-or-bargain sort** (`fw:boilerplate-or-bargain`), original. Purpose: decide where negotiation time goes in a long document. Steps: (1) identify the chassis (LMA English-law, LSTA New York-law, APLMA Singapore or Hong Kong law, or a firm precedent); (2) sort each clause into chassis wording, adapted chassis, or bespoke project term; (3) check chassis clauses only for deviations from the published form; (4) check adapted clauses for project fit (for example, tax gross-up for DFI exemptions); (5) put the team's time into bespoke clauses ranked by money at stake. When to use: first read of any draft finance document. Credit: the "chassis, not the car" framing follows the t-loan-docs fact sheet's teaching angle; the sort itself is original.

**Framework 51.2 Event of default severity grid** (`fw:eod-severity-grid`), original. Purpose: compare drafts and markups of the EoD clause. For each EoD record: trigger; materiality qualifier; grace or cure period; threshold amount; whether it is a Default during the grace period; remedy available (drawstop only, cash trap, acceleration); who can waive (Majority, all lenders, ECA). Score each EoD from 1 (hair trigger) to 4 (heavily softened); compare sponsor and lender markups; negotiate the gaps that matter (usually cross-default thresholds, MAE, project document termination, permit loss). When to use: every EoD negotiation and every new-deal credit review.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: first-read checklist of a CTA (ratio definitions, EoDs, distributions, CPs, permitted debt, change of control, voting, cure).
- Red flags: "as determined by the Agent" without a dispute mechanism; ratio definitions that count equity cures as CFADS indefinitely; cross-default threshold below a single monthly O&M invoice; MAE event of default without objective tests; CPs that depend on a government act with no longstop; "no Event of Default" in the distribution conditions (from the lender's chair); unanimous consent for routine permitted-debt baskets.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: grace periods (payment three to five business days; other covenants 30 days, with project document defaults tracking the counterparty's cure period); cross-default thresholds sized to a few days of revenue, not to round numbers; equity cures limited to two or three over the life and never consecutive. Each rule states the market and period where it holds (mark as indicative per D-011) and when it breaks.
- Common mistakes: negotiating the ratio level and ignoring the definitions; forgetting that LDs and insurance proceeds flow through the definitions; letting the model and the CTA define CFADS differently; leaving the hedging policy in a side letter.
- Questions experts ask: Which definition would I change to move this covenant by 0.05x? Which CP is in a third party's hands? What happens on the day after an EoD before anyone accelerates? Which matters need the ECA?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are sponsor's counsel on an illustrative 360 MW CCGT in Tanzania (do not name or imply a real project). Nine days before signing, the lenders add a material adverse effect event of default ("any event that in the opinion of the Majority Lenders has or is reasonably likely to have a Material Adverse Effect"). The sponsor's investment committee meets in four days. The rest of the EoD list is agreed.
**Reasoning it through.** What the MAE EoD does in a non-recourse deal (subjective trigger, drawstop leverage during construction); what lenders fear (an event the specific EoDs miss, such as a sovereign rating collapse); what sponsors fear (drawstop used to reprice); the options: delete (rarely achieved with ECA/DFI lenders), objectivize ("reasonably" plus specific limbs: ability to pay, validity of security, project documents), restrict to construction, require all-lender or super-majority decision, make it a drawstop only, not an acceleration trigger. Expert answer: accept an objective MAE limited to the project company's ability to perform payment obligations and the validity of the finance documents, operating only as a drawstop before completion, with Majority Lenders acting reasonably; trade it for a longer grace on project document defaults.
**What would change the answer.** An investment-grade offtaker and sovereign guarantee (push harder for deletion); a lender group dominated by ECAs that cannot sign without it (accept drawstop-only); a construction period longer than three years (lenders' need is real); a precedent from the same lenders on the same program (cite it).

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** Chapter 51 gave the lenders rights; this chapter gives them control. It teaches why project lenders take security over everything (to control the project and keep other creditors out, not to sell a power plant for scrap), what the package contains, how common-law and civil-law systems deliver it (security trusts and floating charges against pledges, the accessory principle and parallel debt), how security is perfected and ranked, and how enforcement keeps the project running. It then teaches the accounts structure and the cash waterfall in full as legal machinery: which accounts exist, who may sign on them, how every dollar moves from the offtaker to the distribution account, and how special flows (insurance, compensation, liquidated damages, cures, disposals) are routed.

**What you will be able to do:**
- Design a security package for a project in a common-law or civil-law jurisdiction and say what each element is for and how it is perfected (Capabilities 6 and 8).
- Compare going-concern enforcement with liquidation and explain why the share pledge and step-in come first (Capabilities 6 and 11).
- Draft and audit an accounts structure and a priority of payments, and trace any receipt through it to its destination (Capabilities 4 and 6).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned

- Purpose of security in project finance: control, protection against third-party creditors, leverage in restructuring, step-in enablement; contrasted with liquidation recovery.
- The security package: share pledge over the project company, fixed assets and land rights, receivables, bank accounts, assignment by way of security of project contracts and insurances, intellectual property and licences where assignable, subordination and assignment of shareholder loans, sponsor-level security; what cannot be secured (some permits, public land).
- Common-law security: fixed and floating charges, the security trust and the security trustee, appointment of a receiver or administrator as a lender tool (principles; jurisdiction detail per fact sheet).
- Civil-law security: pledges (nantissements) over shares, receivables, accounts and the business as a going concern; mortgages over registered land rights; the accessory principle; the parallel debt undertaking and the security agent; statutory security-agent regimes (per fact sheet request t-security-civil-law).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 52.1 Going-concern versus liquidation recovery (Illustrative).** Scenario: a 310 MW coal-to-gas converted plant in Indonesia in payment default with senior debt of USD 410.0 million. Going-concern sale of the shares under a share pledge: buyer values CFADS of USD 52.3 million a year for 14 remaining PPA years at 9.75%: enterprise value USD 390.6 million, recovery 95.3% of debt (before costs). Liquidation of the equipment after PPA termination: realizable USD 96.4 million less costs USD 11.8 million = USD 84.6 million, recovery 20.6%. Lesson: security is valuable because it lets lenders sell or run the going concern, which depends on the PPA surviving the enforcement (direct agreement, Chapter 28). Simplifications stated: flat CFADS, no taxes on sale, buyer's discount rate given.

**Example 52.2 Parallel debt and the distribution of enforcement proceeds (Illustrative).** Scenario: a civil-law jurisdiction without a security trust; senior claims at enforcement: ECA-covered tranche USD 182.6 million, DFI tranche USD 134.1 million, commercial tranche USD 231.7 million, hedge close-out USD 12.9 million; total USD 561.3 million. Parallel debt owed to the security agent equals USD 561.3 million. Onshore enforcement proceeds USD 418.7 million less enforcement costs USD 6.2 million = USD 412.5 million, distributed pro rata (assuming hedges rank pari passu, Chapter 53 owns the alternatives): ECA 134.2, DFI 98.6, commercial 170.3, hedge 9.5; recovery 73.5%. Each payment discharges the parallel debt and the corresponding principal claim by the same amount; parallel debt remaining USD 148.8 million. Teaching point: the parallel debt never increases the total recoverable; it gives the agent its own claim so the security has a creditor it can follow.

**Example 52.3 One semiannual period through the waterfall (Illustrative).** Scenario: 138 MW solar plant in Andalusia, Spain, period ending June 30. Receipts into the proceeds account USD 31.84 million. Step 1 operating costs and taxes 7.31; CFADS 24.53. Step 2 senior agency and account fees 0.41. Step 3 senior interest 6.93 and net scheduled hedge payment 0.37 (pari passu). Step 4 senior principal 9.15. Debt service total 16.86; DSCR 1.45x (1.455). Step 5 DSRA top-up to the six-month target: target 16.84, balance 15.22, top-up 1.62. Step 6 MMRA contribution 1.10. Remaining 4.95. Step 7 cash sweep 25% of the remaining: 1.24 (1.2375). Step 8 distribution account 3.71 (3.7125), released only if the distribution conditions are met (Chapter 51). Table reconciles: receipts 31.84 = 7.31 + 16.86 + 1.62 + 1.10 + 1.24 + 3.71. Units USD m; state that the solar plant's PPA and tax are simplified.

**Example 52.4 Insurance proceeds and the reinstatement test (Illustrative).** Scenario: 240 MW wind farm in Texas; a transformer fire causes a property loss of USD 23.7 million; deductible USD 2.5 million; property damage proceeds USD 21.2 million. Accounts agreement: proceeds above a USD 15.0 million threshold go to the insurance proceeds account; release for reinstatement requires an IE certificate that reinstatement is technically and economically feasible within the BI indemnity period, a reinstatement budget fully funded, and a projected DSCR of at least 1.30x after reinstatement; otherwise proceeds prepay senior debt. Reinstatement budget USD 22.94 million: funding gap USD 1.74 million plus the USD 2.5 million deductible to be funded by the project company from the operating account or by the sponsor. Business interruption proceeds go to the proceeds account as revenue substitutes. Teaching point: who decides between reinstatement and prepayment, and why lenders accept reinstatement when the project's value depends on it.

## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 52.1 Security package control test** (`fw:security-control-test`), original. Purpose: judge a security package by what it lets lenders control, not by its length. For each asset ask: (1) on a default, who controls it and how fast; (2) can it be enforced without stopping the plant; (3) is it perfected against third parties and in insolvency; (4) what consents does enforcement need; (5) what does it cost to take and keep. Mark gaps; fix the gaps that affect the going concern first (shares, project contracts, accounts, land rights). When to use: term sheet stage and legal due diligence.

**Framework 52.2 Trace-a-dollar test** (`fw:trace-a-dollar`), original. Purpose: audit a priority of payments. Steps: take each category of receipt (tariff, insurance, LDs, compensation, cure, disposal, VAT refund, hedge receipt), trace it through each account to its end use; check that every step has a clause, an actor and a date; check that model and document agree; list any receipt with no route. When to use: drafting and model audit (cross-reference Chapter 44).

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: security package by asset class; perfection steps and deadlines; acknowledgments needed from each counterparty; accounts opened and mandates signed; block notice templates agreed; permitted investments list.
- Red flags: receivables pledged without counterparty notice where local law requires it; accounts outside the security net (petty cash creep); land rights that cannot be mortgaged (for example an unregistered lease); permits that lapse on change of control; a waterfall that pays subordinated or affiliate amounts before reserve top-ups; model and accounts agreement disagreeing on timing.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: take security over everything, but spend negotiating capital on the shares, the contracts, the accounts and the land; perfection items that need a government act belong on the CP list or a short CS list with a longstop; parallel debt is standard in civil-law deals but its insolvency recognition is a legal-opinion point in every new jurisdiction.
- Common mistakes: treating security as a sale-value calculation; forgetting the hedge receipts and VAT refunds in the waterfall; leaving the insurance proceeds threshold undefined in currency; mismatched test dates between the CTA and the accounts agreement.
- Questions experts ask: If we enforce on Monday, who runs the plant on Tuesday? Which consents does a share-pledge sale need? Which account receives the offtaker's payment if the offtaker ignores the notice? What can the account bank refuse to do?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are lenders' counsel on an illustrative 450 MW combined-cycle plant in an unnamed civil-law state whose codes follow the French model (Illustrative; imply no real country's registry). Five days before the target close, local counsel reports that registration of the mortgage over the land lease will take five months because the registry needs a ministerial certificate. The sponsor asks you to close and treat the mortgage as a condition subsequent; the EPC contractor's price validity expires in ten days (expiry would cost about USD 4.6 million in escalation, a figure given in the drill as Illustrative).
**Reasoning it through.** What the mortgage protects (the land under the plant; step-in and enforcement depend on it); what else covers the gap (share pledge over a company that holds the lease right; direct agreement with the lessor; the government's undertaking); the cost of not closing; options: close with a CS and a longstop, a drawstop on drawdowns beyond a cap until registration, a sponsor guarantee of the gap, a legal opinion on interim priority. Expert answer: close with the mortgage as a CS with a 180-day longstop, drawdowns capped at the equity-funded and advance-payment amount until registration, and a lessor acknowledgment delivered at close.
**What would change the answer.** A registry with a record of losing priority to later filings; an insolvency hardening period that the delay would put the mortgage inside; a lessor with power to terminate the lease on change of control; ECA cover conditional on perfected security at first drawdown.

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** A modern project financing has creditors whose interests diverge exactly when things go wrong: an ECA-covered lender who is paid by its insurer, an uncovered bank that is not, a DFI with policy obligations and preferred creditor status, hedge banks whose claim swings with interest rates, bondholders who vote slowly through a trustee, mezzanine lenders and Islamic financiers who own assets rather than lend against them. This chapter teaches how the intercreditor agreement settles ranking, sharing, voting, entrenched rights, standstills and enforcement among them, and how new creditors accede later. It is the document that decides who controls the project after a default.

**What you will be able to do:**
- Design an intercreditor decision matrix for a multi-tranche financing and compute how any decision is carried or blocked (Capabilities 5 and 6).
- Explain the rights an ECA, a DFI, a hedge counterparty and a bondholder group need and what each will trade (Capabilities 5 and 8).
- Compute recoveries by class under different ranking, sharing and standstill arrangements (Capability 11).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned

- Purpose and parties of an intercreditor agreement (ICA); the intercreditor agent; how the ICA sits with the CTA (sometimes merged).
- Ranking: senior pari passu, super senior (for example hedges or working-capital facilities), subordinated and mezzanine debt, shareholder loans; payment blockage, turnover, subordination of shareholder loans as an intercreditor matter.
- Pro rata sharing among senior creditors; the sharing clause and turnover of set-off recoveries.
- Voting across classes: instructing group; pooled versus tranche voting; voting of covered lenders through the ECA; B-loan voting through the lender of record; hedge counterparties' votes before and after close-out; bondholder voting through a trustee or representative; thresholds for waivers, amendments, acceleration and enforcement.
- Entrenched and individual rights: economics, maturity, security release, waterfall changes; ECA and DFI policy rights (sanctions, anti-corruption, E&S, preferred creditor status preservation, disclosure).
- ECA rights: ECA majority concepts; subrogation after a claim; the ECA's consent to amendments; the agent role.
- DFI rights: A/B loan voting, individual policy rights, mandate-driven carve-outs.
- Hedge counterparties: ranking of scheduled payments and close-out amounts, voting by close-out amount, restrictions on early termination, novation and over-hedging on prepayment.
- Standstills: periods during which junior or minority creditors may not enforce; conduct of enforcement; application of enforcement proceeds.
- Accession of new creditors: refinancing debt, additional senior debt, bondholders; accession deeds; permitted senior debt tests at the intercreditor level (ratio-tested additions, as in Sadara).
- The conventional–Islamic interface in the ICA: pari passu treatment of ijara rentals and interest, pro rata sharing with asset-owning financiers, service agency and late payment amounts paid to charity, enforcement over assets owned by Islamic financiers (structures themselves owned by Chapter 33).
- Framework: Intercreditor decision matrix (Framework 53.1).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 53.1 Sharing after one lender's set-off (Illustrative).** Three senior lenders with claims USD 88.0, 64.5 and 47.9 million (total 200.4) on a defaulted 180 MW hydro project in Peru. Lender 3, also the project company's local bank, sets off USD 14.6 million of deposits. Under the sharing clause the recovery is shared pro rata: lender 1 6.41, lender 2 4.70, lender 3 3.49 (USD m); lender 3 pays USD 11.11 million to the agent for redistribution and its claim against the project company is restored by that amount. Equation 53.1: share$_k$ = recovery $\times C_k/\sum C$.

**Example 53.2 Mezzanine recovery under subordination (Illustrative).** A 412 MW gas peaker in Texas is sold on enforcement. Case A: proceeds after costs USD 457.3 million; senior claims USD 431.8 million; mezzanine claim USD 61.5 million. Seniors are paid in full; the mezzanine lender receives USD 25.5 million (41.5%). Case B: proceeds after costs USD 424.6 million; seniors are short by USD 7.2 million and the mezzanine lender receives nothing. During a 120-day payment blockage before enforcement the mezzanine lender had received USD 1.9 million of interest in breach of the blockage; the turnover clause makes it pay that sum to the senior agent, cutting the senior shortfall to USD 5.3 million. Teaching point: turnover matters only when seniors are short, which is exactly when juniors are tempted to take cash.

**Example 53.3 One waiver vote under three counting rules (Illustrative).** Four-tranche financing of a 1,050 MW CCGT in Vietnam: ECA-covered tranche USD 312.7 million (votes cast by the ECA for the covered lenders), DFI A-loan 148.3, DFI B-loan 96.2 (lender of record votes per participants' instructions), uncovered commercial tranche 266.9; total 824.1. Shares: 37.94%, 18.00%, 11.67%, 32.39%. A waiver of a DSCR event of default is supported by the commercial lenders and the B-loan participants and opposed by the ECA; the DFI abstains then joins the waiver. Results: (a) pooled vote, Majority above 50%: commercial plus B = 44.06% fails; with the A-loan 62.06% passes; (b) pooled, super-majority 66⅔%: 62.06% fails without the ECA; (c) tranche voting with each tranche a single vote and three of four tranches required: passes with commercial, A and B. Teaching point: the counting rule decides the outcome more than the numbers.

**Example 53.4 Hedge claims pari passu or super senior (Illustrative).** Enforcement proceeds USD 512.0 million; senior loan claims USD 791.6 million; hedge close-out USD 23.8 million. Pari passu: recovery 62.8% for all; loans 497.1, hedges 14.9. Super senior hedges: hedges 23.8 in full, loans 488.2 (61.7%). Difference to loans USD 8.9 million; explain why hedge banks ask for super senior status and why it is usually limited to hedges within the permitted hedge ratio.

**Example 53.5 Enforce now or wait (Illustrative).** A 64 km availability-payment road in Poland after a contractor default. Enforce now: share-pledge sale proceeds USD 448.0 million less costs 9.3 = 438.7. Standstill 180 days while a replacement contractor is procured: 60% chance of a restructured sale at 512.0, 40% chance value falls to 401.0; costs 4.1; discount at 9.0% a year for half a year. Expected gross 467.6; present value (467.6 - 4.1) / 1.09^0.5 = 443.95. Waiting is worth about USD 5.3 million more in expectation but carries the 40% downside; Equation 53.2 gives the expected-value comparison; discuss which classes prefer which answer (fully covered ECA lenders indifferent, uncovered banks risk-averse, mezzanine prefers waiting).

**Example 53.6 Conventional and ijara facilities sharing enforcement proceeds (Illustrative).** An illustrative 1,200 MW independent water and power project in the Gulf, financed with conventional and Islamic tranches: conventional senior loans USD 541.7 million; ijara facility claim USD 161.5 million (outstanding acquisition cost 157.6 plus accrued rental 3.9); late payment amounts of USD 0.6 million owed to the Islamic financiers are payable to charity and excluded from sharing. Net proceeds USD 580.9 million (586.2 less costs 5.3) from enforcement, of which part is the value of assets the Islamic financiers own: the ICA treats them as contributed to a common pool. Pro rata: recovery 82.6%; conventional 447.5, ijara 133.4. Explain the mechanics that make this pro rata possible (asset sale undertaking, purchase price equal to the claim, service agency costs), cross-referencing Chapter 33 for the structures.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: creditor classes; vote counting for each; entrenched matters; individual policy rights; hedge ranking and voting; standstill periods; enforcement instruction mechanics; accession conditions; application of proceeds.
- Red flags: a class that can block every waiver; hedge close-out super senior without a cap tied to the hedging policy; bondholder votes needing a meeting with a long notice for routine waivers; DFI policy rights drafted so widely that they block enforcement; no deemed-consent mechanism for slow voters; no rule for what happens when an ECA pays a claim and is subrogated.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: covered lenders vote through their ECA; B-loan participants vote through the lender of record; hedge banks vote only after close-out and only for their close-out amount; juniors stand still for a defined period (often 90 to 180 days, indicative) unless seniors enforce first.
- Common mistakes: drafting the ICA after the CTA is final; forgetting the standby facility and VAT facility in the ranking; treating bondholders like a bank in the timetable for consents.
- Questions experts ask: Who can stop a waiver? Who can force an enforcement? What happens to the hedges on a prepayment? Can a new creditor join without unanimous consent?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are the intercreditor agent on an illustrative 820 MW wind portfolio in Turkey financed by an ECA-covered tranche (35%), a DFI A/B loan (30%) and commercial banks (35%). A DSCR event of default occurred on the last test date because of a one-off grid curtailment; the commercial banks and the DFI want to waive it for a 0.25% fee; the ECA says it needs eight weeks and its ministry's sign-off. The next payment date, when the distribution conditions will be tested, is in ten days. The ICA requires a super-majority of 66⅔% for financial covenant waivers and ECA consent for any waiver while ECA-covered loans are outstanding.
**Reasoning it through.** What happens if nothing is done (no acceleration is likely; cash is trapped; the risk is a cross-default under the turbine supplier's service contract if the waiver is not given); options: a short standstill letter signed by all classes pending ECA approval; a waiver by the other classes with an ECA reservation; a covenant holiday conditional on ECA consent; restructuring the ask to fit the ECA's delegated authority (a waiver below a size the ECA's underwriter can sign). Expert answer: a 60-day standstill and reservation of rights signed by all, plus a waiver request drafted to fit the ECA's delegated authority.
**What would change the answer.** The breach being recurring, not one-off; the ECA having already paid a claim (subrogation changes who votes); a hedge bank threatening to terminate; the sponsor offering an equity cure.

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** Every contract in the web names a law and a forum, and those choices decide whether a right on paper becomes cash. This chapter teaches how to choose governing law for each document, when to litigate and when to arbitrate, how to choose a seat and rules (ICC, LCIA, ICSID, UNCITRAL), how awards are enforced under the New York and ICSID Conventions, how sovereign immunity and its waiver work at the suit and execution stages, what stabilization and economic-equilibrium clauses can and cannot do, and how investment treaties protect foreign investors and how sponsors structure to obtain that protection. The method throughout is to work backwards from where the counterparty's assets are.

**What you will be able to do:**
- Choose governing law and a dispute forum for each contract in a project's web by working backwards from enforcement (Capabilities 6 and 8).
- Draft an arbitration clause, an immunity waiver and a stabilization clause, and say where each usually lands (Capability 6).
- Test whether an investor's structure is protected by an investment treaty and what a claim is worth after time, cost and collection risk (Capabilities 8 and 15).

## 3. Concepts

### Concepts owned

- Governing law: choice of law for contracts; mandatory local law for land, security, concessions, insolvency and regulation; why finance documents use English or New York law; consistency across the contract web.
- Forum: national courts versus arbitration in detail; jurisdiction clauses, exclusive and asymmetric (optional) clauses; multi-tier clauses (negotiation, expert determination, arbitration); seat, institution, rules, tribunal, language; emergency arbitrators and expedited procedures (only what a fact sheet verifies about each rule set); joinder and consolidation across project contracts.
- Arbitral institutions and rules: ICC, LCIA, ICSID (Convention and Additional Facility), UNCITRAL Rules (ad hoc and administered); when to choose each.
- Enforcement of awards: New York Convention (scope, Article V refusal grounds); ICSID Convention (Articles 53 to 55, annulment, registration as a final judgment, execution subject to local immunity law); set-aside at the seat; annulment risk.
- Sovereign immunity: immunity from suit and from execution; commercial-activity and commercial-use exceptions in principle; express waivers; the 2026 decisions on ICSID versus New York Convention ratification as waiver (UK Supreme Court, High Court of Australia, US cert denials) as dated facts.
- Stabilization clauses: freezing, economic equilibrium, hybrid; their limits (a contract cannot bind a legislature; compensation instead of exemption); interaction with PPA change-in-law pass-through (Chapter 18) and IA support (Chapter 17).
- Investment treaties: BITs and the ECT; protected investor and investment; standards (fair and equitable treatment, expropriation including indirect, full protection and security, umbrella clauses, free transfer); shareholder standing; defenses (necessity, non-precluded measures); legitimate expectations and timing; treaty structuring through holding companies; denial of benefits; timing and abuse of process in principle; the intra-EU collapse (Achmea, Komstroy, termination agreement, ECT withdrawals, sunset clause).
- Frameworks: Enforcement-first forum choice (Framework 54.1); Treaty protection test (Framework 54.2).

## 5. Opening

On March 4, 2026 the UK Supreme Court held unanimously, in *Kingdom of Spain v Infrastructure Services Luxembourg* and *Republic of Zimbabwe v Border Timbers* ([2026] UKSC 9), that a state that has agreed to Article 54(1) of the ICSID Convention cannot claim immunity from the registration of an ICSID award in the UK, while Article 55 still preserves immunity from execution. Five weeks later the High Court of Australia held in *CCDM Holdings v Republic of India* ([2026] HCA 9) that ratifying the New York Convention does not by itself waive a state's immunity. Open on that pair (fact sheet t-arbitration-treaties): two investors holding awards against states, one able to turn its award into a judgment and the other stopped at the courthouse door, because of a choice made in a treaty and a contract years before. Then the rule: choose law and forum by working backwards from enforcement.

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 54.1 Expected recovery under three forums (Illustrative).** Scenario: a USD 186.4 million claim by a 600 MW hydro project company against a state-owned offtaker in a country that is party to both Conventions; discount rate 11% a year. Options: (a) host-country courts: probability of winning 0.55, years to cash 6.5, costs USD 3.2 million, probability of collecting a judgment 0.40; (b) ICC arbitration seated in Paris: win 0.70, 3.5 years, costs USD 9.8 million, collection 0.55 (New York Convention plus an express immunity waiver); (c) ICSID: win 0.65 times survival of annulment 0.85 = 0.5525, 4.5 years, costs USD 12.1 million, collection 0.60. Present value of expected recovery less costs: (a) USD 17.6 million (expected cash 41.0, PV 20.8); (b) USD 40.0 million (expected 71.8, PV 49.8); (c) USD 26.5 million (expected 61.8, PV 38.6). Equation 54.1: $\mathrm{EV} = C \times p_{\mathrm{win}} \times p_{\mathrm{collect}}/(1+r)^{T} - K$. Teaching point: time and collection probability move the answer more than the probability of winning; the ICSID route wins if collection probability or speed improves (sensitivity left to Exercise 54.9). Simplifications stated: costs incurred upfront; no interest on the award; no cost recovery from the losing side.

**Example 54.2 Award value with interest (Illustrative).** An award of USD 72.3 million carrying interest at 5.85% a year compounded semiannually, collected 5.5 years after the award date: USD 99.3 million (99.28). Show that interest at a commercial rate compensates for time but not for collection risk, and that a state's annulment application delays payment while interest accrues.

**Example 54.3 Pricing a change in law under an equilibrium clause (Illustrative).** A 400 MW wind project in Morocco (Illustrative) with revenue of USD 148.6 million a year for 18 remaining years; a new law imposes a 3.0% levy on revenue: USD 4.46 million a year. Two compensation routes: (a) tariff uplift grossed up for the levy on the uplift: $x = 4.458/(1-0.03) = $ USD 4.60 million a year, an uplift of 3.09% on revenue; (b) lump sum equal to the PV of the levy at 10.0%: USD 36.6 million. Equation 54.2: $x = L/(1-\ell)$. Discuss why the government prefers the tariff route (no cash today), why lenders prefer the lump sum to prepay debt, and why the clause must say which. Simplification: income tax effects ignored.

**Example 54.4 Treaty timing and the sunset clause (Illustrative).** A fictional treaty between two states (State A, State B): investment made by subscription on March 14, 2019 and a further capital injection on June 30, 2021; State A gives notice of withdrawal on September 1, 2024, effective after 12 months on September 1, 2025; 20-year sunset clause. Investments made before September 1, 2025 remain protected until September 1, 2045; a further injection on November 15, 2025 is not protected; an insertion of a new holding company in the treaty state in 2024, after a regulatory dispute began, faces an abuse-of-process objection (principle). ## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 54.1 Enforcement-first forum choice** (`fw:enforcement-first-forum`), original, following the teaching angle in fact sheet t-arbitration-treaties. Steps: (1) where are the counterparty's assets, and are they commercial assets; (2) is the counterparty a state, a state entity, or private; (3) which Conventions bind the asset states; (4) is there an express waiver of immunity from suit and from execution; (5) what is the time to cash and the cost in each forum; (6) what is the annulment or set-aside risk at the candidate seats; (7) choose the forum with the best expected recovery (Equation 54.1), then make the clause consistent across the web. When to use: drafting every project contract with a state or foreign counterparty.

**Framework 54.2 Treaty protection test** (`fw:treaty-protection-test`), original. Steps: (1) is there a treaty in force between the host state and the investor's state at the date of each investment; (2) is the claimant an investor (nationality, denial-of-benefits clause, substance); (3) is the asset an investment under the treaty's definition; (4) what standards apply (FET, expropriation, umbrella, transfer); (5) what consent to arbitration (ICSID, UNCITRAL, other) and preconditions (waiting periods, local litigation); (6) carve-outs (tax, necessity, non-precluded measures); (7) sunset and termination status; (8) intra-EU or similar objections. When to use: structuring the holding chain before the investment and re-testing before any restructuring.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: governing law and forum per contract; seat and rules aligned; expert determination for technical issues; joinder and consolidation; ICSID consent and foreign-control agreement; immunity waiver from suit and execution; treaty chain tested at each investment date; sunset and denial-of-benefits review.
- Red flags: a state counterparty with a local-seat arbitration and local-court review; an immunity waiver from suit only; a holding company inserted after a dispute is foreseeable; forum mismatch between the PPA and the guarantee; an expert-determination clause with no deadline; an intra-EU structure relied on for treaty protection.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: finance documents under English or New York law with an asymmetric clause; state contracts under local law with international arbitration; ICSID where available, UNCITRAL as fallback; stabilization by compensation, not exemption.
- Common mistakes: choosing a seat for convenience rather than its courts' record; forgetting that execution immunity survives a waiver of suit; assuming a tax treaty is an investment treaty; drafting the GSA and PPA forums independently.
- Questions experts ask: Where would we enforce, against which assets, and how long would it take? Does the guarantee follow the PPA's forum? What did the host state's courts do the last time a foreign award came home?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are sponsor's counsel on an illustrative 300 MW solar-plus-storage IPP in Central Asia. Two weeks before the IA is due to be signed, the ministry refuses ICSID arbitration and offers arbitration seated in the capital under the national arbitration law, with review by the national courts, and no waiver of immunity from execution. The lenders' ECA and DFI term sheets require "internationally recognized arbitration outside the host country". The host state is party to the New York Convention and to ICSID.
**Reasoning it through.** What the lenders need (a forum whose award they can enforce abroad; the DFI's halo); what the ministry fears (sovereignty optics; precedent for every later IPP); options: a neutral seat under UNCITRAL Rules administered by an institution; ICSID with a cooling-off and amicable settlement tier; local seat but ICC Rules with a narrow court review and an execution waiver limited to commercial assets; reliance on a BIT for the sponsor (not enough for lenders). Expert answer: propose UNCITRAL Rules with a neutral seat and the Permanent Court of Arbitration as appointing authority, a six-month negotiation tier, and a waiver of immunity from suit and from execution against commercial assets; trade the location of hearings and the language for the seat.
**What would change the answer.** A DFI that is itself a lender of record (halo raises the cost to the state of breach); a BIT with consent to ICSID (sponsor protection independent of the IA); a guaranteeing ministry with no commercial assets abroad (execution waiver worth less; PRI matters more, Chapter 60); a recent episode of the host courts enjoining arbitration.

## 2. Purpose and capability statement

**Purpose.** Parts V to X taught what the financing contains. This chapter teaches how it gets done: choosing a financing strategy (club, underwritten or best efforts; bank, bond or both), appointing and using a financial advisor and arrangers, preparing the information memorandum and lender presentation, getting credit approval inside a bank, syndicating and selling down, managing a documentation process with a dozen law firms, satisfying conditions precedent, separating signing from financial close, running the funds flow on closing day, and managing the critical path that decides when the money arrives. It closes the Case P financing on July 17, 2018.

**What you will be able to do:**
- Choose and defend a financing strategy for a project and compute what an underwriting or a club costs and earns each party (Capabilities 5 and 6).
- Build a critical-path timetable to financial close and identify the tasks that set the date (Capability 10).
- Run a conditions-precedent tracker and a closing-day funds flow that reconciles to the cent (Capabilities 6 and 10).
- Explain how a bank's credit approval works and what makes a committee say yes (Capabilities 14 and 15).

## 6. Worked examples

**Example 55.1 Underwriting economics (Illustrative).** An arranger underwrites USD 412.5 million of senior debt for a 520 MW solar-plus-storage project in Saudi Arabia (Illustrative). Borrower pays: underwriting fee 0.50% and arrangement fee 0.75% (both kept by the arranger on the full amount) and a participation fee pool of 1.00% (paid to participants on their tickets): total USD 9.28 million (2.25%). Target hold USD 85.0 million. Full sell-down: arranger keeps 412.5 x 1.25% = 5.16 plus 1.00% on its hold 0.85 = USD 6.01 million, a fee yield of 7.07% on its hold. Failed sell-down (only USD 236.0 million placed; hold 176.5): keeps 5.16 + 1.77 = USD 6.92 million, a yield of 3.92% on a hold twice its credit limit. Clearing the deal by paying 1.25% to participants on the USD 327.5 million sold costs USD 0.82 million from its own fees: keeps USD 5.19 million (6.10% on its hold). Equation 55.1: retained fees $= U(f_u + f_a) + H f_p - S\max(0, f_p^{\mathrm{paid}} - f_p)$. Teaching point: underwriting is a fee for carrying syndication risk; the failed case shows why arrangers flex (Chapter 38) or why sponsors choose a club.

**Example 55.2 Allocating an oversubscribed syndication (Illustrative).** USD 327.5 million to sell; nine banks bid USD 612.5 million (coverage 1.87x): bids 120.0, 95.0, 85.0, 75.0, 60.0, 55.0, 50.0, 40.0, 32.5. Pro rata scale-back at 53.47%: allocations 64.2, 50.8, 45.4, 40.1, 32.1, 29.4, 26.7, 21.4, 17.4 (sum 327.5). Then a second method: relationship banks named by the sponsor receive a floor, and the writer shows how floors shift allocations (writer computes with stated floors). Teaching point: allocation is a sponsor-relationship decision as well as arithmetic, and the IM's minimum ticket matters.

**Example 55.3 A closing-day funds flow (Illustrative).** A 280 MW wind farm in Mexico closing on a Thursday. Uses on closing day: repay development bridge loan 38.24; reimburse sponsor development costs 24.75; arrangement and upfront fees 18.63; ECA premium first installment 9.41; lenders' advisers and legal 6.87; EPC advance payment (10%) 52.16; initial owner's costs account funding 3.12; total USD 153.18 million. Sources: equity contribution 96.30 (equity first in this deal) and first senior drawdown 56.88. Check: sources minus uses 0.00. Show payment instructions, value dates, cut-off times, and the order of confirmations (equity received before the lenders fund).

**Example 55.4 Critical path to close (Illustrative).** Tasks from term sheet signing (week 0), durations in weeks and predecessors: B finance documents negotiated 14 (A); C IE final report 6 (A); D model audit 5 (C); F legal due diligence 7 (A); E credit approvals 4 (D, F); G ECA cover approval 10 (C); I permit transfers and land registration 11 (A); J signing 1 (B, E); K security perfection 3 (J, I); L CP satisfaction and close 2 (K, G). Forward and backward pass: close in week 21; critical path A-C-D-E-J-K-L; float: B 1 week, F 4, G 3, I 5. Equation 55.2: float = LS - ES. Teaching point: the documents are not on the critical path; the IE report and the model audit are, so a two-week IE delay moves close by two weeks while a two-week legal delay moves it by one.

**Example 55.5 The cost of an eight-week slip (Illustrative).** An illustrative 390 MW wind farm in Mexico with an EPC price of USD 612.0 million. Development costs continue at USD 0.42 million a week: 3.36. EPC price validity extension at 0.15% a month for 1.84 months: 1.69. If swap rates rise 18 bps during the slip on an average hedged notional of USD 380.0 million over a 7.6-year weighted average life, at a 5.00% discount rate: about USD 4.2 million (0.684 a year). Total about USD 9.3 million, roughly 1.5% of the EPC price. Teaching point: a sponsor should be willing to pay for critical-path acceleration up to that amount.

## 8. Frameworks

**Framework 55.1 Financing strategy selector** (`fw:financing-strategy-selector`), original. Questions in order: (1) Does the sponsor need certainty of funds at bid or signing (yes points to underwriting or committed club)? (2) Is construction risk material and the investor base for construction-risk bonds thin (points to banks or ECA/DFI-led)? (3) Is the size beyond the club's appetite (points to underwriting plus syndication, or multi-source)? (4) Are ECA or DFI lenders available and needed for country risk (points to an ECA/DFI-anchored structure)? (5) Is the asset rated investment grade or close, and is a long fixed rate valuable (points to a bond or private placement, possibly after construction)? (6) What does delay cost against fee savings? Output: a ranked strategy with the reason. When to use: at mandate and when market conditions change.

**Framework 55.2 Critical path to close** (`fw:critical-path-to-close`), established method (the critical path method from project scheduling, credited as an established technique; no attribution details unless a fact sheet verifies them), applied to financings. Steps: list tasks, durations, dependencies; forward pass for earliest dates; backward pass for latest dates; float; identify the critical path; assign owners; re-run weekly; spend money only on critical-path tasks.

## 11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: strategy memo; role allocation; IM and model frozen versions; credit approval validity dates; syndication timetable; issues list owner; CP tracker; signature logistics; funds flow; hedge execution plan; closing memorandum; CS tracker.
- Red flags: credit approvals expiring before the realistic close date; an IM and model that disagree; CPs owned by third parties with no escalation path; a hedge executed before CP satisfaction is certain; a funds flow that depends on same-day cross-border wires after cut-off; a CS list longer than one page.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: start the CP tracker at mandate, not at signing; freeze the model and base case before credit committees, not after; plan for each government consent to take twice the promised time (indicative); keep the critical path visible weekly.
- Common mistakes: treating legal documents as the critical path when reports and approvals are; forgetting KYC on new shareholders; not budgeting for re-approval after a material change; executing swaps in a market spike to meet a date.
- Questions experts ask: Which three tasks set the close date? What is the cost of one week's delay? Who can sign each CP off? What happens if one bank's approval lapses on closing day?

## 12. Judgment drill

**The situation.** You are the MLA on an illustrative 700 MW CCGT in Egypt with six banks. Two days before financial close, one bank (11% of the commercial tranche) tells you its credit approval expired yesterday and re-approval needs two weeks. The sponsor's EPC price validity expires in five days; extension would cost USD 1.9 million (Illustrative). The swaps are scheduled to be executed at close.
**Reasoning it through.** Options: close without the bank (the other banks increase pro rata, needing their own approvals; or the MLA takes the share on its book temporarily, needing its own approval); close with a delayed accession of the bank (commitment signed later, its share funded by others in the meantime); delay close two weeks and pay for the EPC extension; partial close. Consider hedge execution (do not execute swaps for a notional that may not fund), CP satisfaction certainty, and the relationship. Expert answer: close on time with the MLA's temporary hold under its standing approval, if available, and an accession mechanism for the lapsed bank within 30 days; execute swaps on the full notional only if the MLA's hold is confirmed.
**What would change the answer.** The lapsed bank's approval being uncertain rather than procedural; the MLA lacking headroom; an ECA policy naming each covered lender; a sponsor able to provide an equity bridge for the gap.
