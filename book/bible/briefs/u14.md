## 66-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Chapter 7 taught the reader to read one project company's accounts. This chapter moves up a level, to the sponsor that owns the project company and to the accounting choices that decide what a project looks like to the sponsor's shareholders, its rating agency, the lenders, and the host government. The reader learns the five questions that settle a project's accounting outcome: who consolidates it; whether the asset on the project company's books is plant, a financial asset, an intangible, or a lease receivable; whether the hedges and power contracts qualify for hedge accounting; how much expected credit loss sits against the offtaker's receivables; and what happens when the sponsor sells down. The chapter then shows how sponsors bend ownership percentages, governance rights, and hedge designs to reach the outcome they want, and what lenders and governments should ask when they see that happening. Its running-case installment ends on a result most readers do not expect: Kilnworth sells 24% of Bélanou Power above its tax cost and still books an accounting loss of USD 82.0 million (P-F26), because a concession accounted for as a financial asset front-loads profit.

What you will be able to do (2 to 4 items, for the `\section*`):

- Decide whether a sponsor consolidates, equity-accounts, or proportionately accounts a project company, and show what each answer does to its reported gearing and earnings (Capabilities 12 and 15).
- Classify a concession, PPA or toll contract under IFRIC 12, IFRS 16 or their US counterparts, and explain the effect on the project company's balance sheet and covenant definitions (Capabilities 3 and 4).
- Set up cash flow hedge accounting for a project swap and an expected-credit-loss allowance for offtaker receivables, and read both in a set of accounts (Capabilities 4 and 10).
- Spot when an ownership split, a governance right, or a hedge design exists for an accounting reason, and judge whether it creates a real risk (Capabilities 14 and 15).

## 66-5. Opening

Real deal moment from `neom-hydrogen`: in its filings Air Products, owner of one third of the votes in NEOM Green Hydrogen Company, carries the whole company on its consolidated balance sheet, about USD 5.1 billion of long-term debt included, although the lenders cannot reach Air Products' general credit (fact sheet facts 12 and 16). ## 66-7. Walkthrough

Section 66.11, Walkthrough: writing the accounting position paper for a new project. Artifact: the four-page memo a sponsor's group reporting team writes before signing the shareholders' agreement and the hedges for a new project, and that its auditor reviews. Steps:

1. Identify the reporting entities (project company, each sponsor, intermediate holding companies) and their frameworks (IFRS, US GAAP, local GAAP).
2. Control analysis: list every relevant activity and who decides it, reserved matter by reserved matter (use Exhibit 66.1's format); conclude consolidation, joint venture, joint operation or associate for each sponsor.
3. Asset classification at the project company: IFRIC 12 scope test, then lease test, then PP&E (Exhibit 66.2 decision tree); where the asset is PP&E, the decommissioning estimate that goes into its cost (Section 66.4.7).
4. Revenue and contract analysis: own-use or derivative for each power, fuel or commodity contract.
5. Hedge accounting plan: which swaps, designation, hypothetical derivative, drawdown-slippage tolerance, re-designation plan.
6. ECL approach for offtaker receivables and shareholder loans.
7. Expected effect on the sponsor's headline metrics, using the format of Example 66.1.
8. Sensitivities: what change in governance or ownership flips the answer.
9. Covenant check: which finance-document definitions use accounting numbers; frozen GAAP clause (Clause 66.1).
10. Sign-off and audit pre-clearance.

The walkthrough applies the steps to Brindabella Energy's 49% stake in Moorabool Peaking Partners, the Illustrative 410 MW gas tolling plant near Geelong of Section 66.8.5, as the memo stood in February 2026, a month before financial close, and shows the finished memo's conclusions table (one row per reporting entity: control conclusion, asset classification, hedge designation, ECL approach, headline metric effect, and the clause that would flip the answer).

## 66-8. Frameworks

- **Framework 66.1, Accounting outcome map** (`fw:accounting-outcome-map`), original. Purpose: predict, before documents are signed, what a project will look like in each owner's accounts and which document terms drive that result. Steps: (1) Who controls? (consolidation, joint venture, associate); (2) What is the asset? ## 66-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist:
- Obtain the reserved-matters schedule and classify each item before agreeing ownership percentages; a deconsolidation target is a drafting task, not a percentage.
- Confirm each sponsor's reporting framework and year-end; IFRS and US GAAP can give different answers for the same project.
- Run the IFRIC 12 scope test on every contract with a public counterparty that sets price and takes the asset at expiry.
- Agree the hedge designation documentation and hypothetical derivative before the swaps trade at financial close.
- Check every finance-document definition that uses an accounting number and attach a frozen GAAP clause.
- Agree the ECL methodology for offtaker receivables with auditors before arrears appear.

Red flags:
- A 49% sponsor that provides O&M, asset management and all sponsor support, with every substantive decision reserved to it in practice.
- Protective rights drafted so broadly that they become participating rights (budget veto, dispatch strategy).
- A construction-period swap sized to an optimistic drawdown curve.
- Covenants written on EBITDA in an IFRIC 12 financial-asset project.
- A corporate offtaker that insists on contract changes late in negotiation "for accounting reasons" (it may be avoiding lease or derivative accounting at the project's expense).

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Joint control requires unanimous consent on relevant activities; any majority-of-two-out-of-three rule defeats it. Limit: board composition and casting votes can change the answer.
- Ring-fenced incorporated project companies are joint ventures, not joint operations. Limit: unincorporated upstream and mining joint operations, and some take-all-output arrangements.
- Availability-payment concessions point to the financial asset model; user-pay concessions to the intangible model. Limit: classification follows contract terms (t-accounting "Do not state": not all availability PPPs fall under the financial asset model).

Common mistakes:
- Treating the equity percentage as the control test.
- Forgetting that loss of control remeasures the retained stake and recycles OCI.
- Assuming a guarantee or LC reduces ECL when it is a separate contract not integral to the receivable.
- Reading IFRIC 12 project EBITDA as comparable with a PP&E project's EBITDA.
- Reading an accounting loss on a sell-down as proof of a bad price, when the financial asset model has already booked the profit (Example 66.9; Ellomay, t-accounting-2 fact 33).

Questions experts ask:
- Who would decide the next refinancing if the sponsors disagreed?
- Which accounting outcome is the sponsor's board expecting, and which clause delivers it?
- What happens to the hedge designation if commercial operation slips six months?
- How is the guarantee reflected in the receivable's loss estimate, and does the auditor agree?

## 66-12. Judgment drill

The situation: It is October 2026. You are the head of project finance at Weserland Energie AG (fictional), a Frankfurt-listed utility reporting under IFRS, and your CFO wants the EUR 2,180.0 million of non-recourse debt of a 640 MW offshore wind project in the German North Sea off the consolidated balance sheet before the year-end rating review. The plan is to sell 50.5% to a pension fund partner and keep 49.5%, while your company keeps the construction management, O&M and asset management contracts and the power marketing role. The partner wants a vote on budgets above EUR 5 million and on refinancing, and nothing else. The lenders' term sheet requires your company to remain "Controlling Sponsor". The partner's bid is due in ten days.

Reasoning outline: (1) apply Framework 66.1: with all operating decisions and power marketing in your hands and only budget-threshold and refinancing votes for the partner, the auditor is likely to conclude that you still direct the relevant activities, so the sale may not deconsolidate; (2) to deconsolidate, governance must give the partner substantive participation (joint control: unanimous approval of the business plan, dispatch and marketing strategy, key contracts), which changes how the project is run and may lower the price you receive for operating services; (3) the lenders' "Controlling Sponsor" definition and the rating agency's analytical adjustment for non-recourse debt may make the accounting win worthless; (4) the expert answer: decide whether the goal is a reported number or a real change in risk; if the rating agency already treats the debt as non-recourse, accept consolidation and sell at the better price; if deconsolidation is required, negotiate joint control explicitly, rewrite the lenders' sponsor definition to "Sponsors acting jointly", and price the governance concession.

What would change the answer: an agency methodology that consolidates proportionately anyway (deconsolidation has little value); a partner willing to take joint control only at a lower price (compare the price cost with the rating benefit); lender refusal to change the Controlling Sponsor definition (keep consolidation); a US GAAP reporting parent (VIE analysis may consolidate regardless).

## 67-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. A project earns one stream of cash, and up to five tax authorities want a share of it: the host country through corporate income tax, withholding taxes and VAT; the holding company's country; the sponsor's home country; and, since 2024, any country entitled to collect a global minimum top-up tax. Each slice is small; together they decide whether a 16% target equity return arrives as 16% or 11%. This chapter teaches the reader to map where tax leaks between the project and the investor, to choose a holding structure and capital structure that keep the leakage to what the law intends, to draft and price the gross-up lenders demand, and to read the rules that now limit the old tools: thin capitalization, earnings-based interest caps, transfer pricing, Pillar Two, and in the United States the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's credit cliffs and foreign-entity tests. It treats tax as a negotiation with a host government as well as a calculation, because the holiday granted at bid stage is the first thing a later government revisits. The mechanics of how a project computes its tax (taxable income, losses, withholding, a thin-capitalization ratio, an interest cap) were taught in Chapter 7 (ssec:7.6.4); this chapter is about the choices those rules force on sponsors, lenders and governments.

What you will be able to do:

- Map the tax leakage between a project's pre-tax cash flow and the investor, and choose a holding and funding structure that reduces it within the law (Capabilities 8 and 12).

## 67-7. Walkthrough

Section 67.13, Walkthrough: the tax structure paper for a cross-border project. Artifact: the tax structure paper that sponsors' tax advisers write before bid submission and update before financial close, and that lenders' tax due diligence (Chapter 49) tests. Steps:

1. Describe the project, the host tax regime, and the incentive package claimed, each with its legal source and date.
2. Draw the proposed structure (project company, holding company, sponsors) and every cash flow between them.
3. Run the leakage map (Framework 67.1) for each route considered; state the base-case leakage in percentage points of equity IRR.
4. Test the funding mix against thin capitalization, interest limitation and transfer pricing; show disallowances by year.
5. Test treaty eligibility and substance; list what the holding company must have (board, staff, decisions) and its cost.
6. Run the Pillar Two screen (Framework 67.2) for each sponsor group.
7. Model VAT and duties during construction and the refund lag; size any VAT facility.
8. Identify exit taxes on a future sale of the project company or the holding company.
9. List the change-in-law protections in the PPA, implementation agreement and finance documents, and the gaps.
10. Conclude with the recommended structure, the leakage, the main risks with probabilities, and the decisions needed from the sponsors' boards.

## 67-8. Frameworks

- **Framework 67.1, Tax leakage map** (`fw:tax-leakage-map`), original. Purpose: trace one unit of pre-tax project cash to each investor and record every tax taken on the way. Steps: (1) list each cash route (dividend, shareholder-loan interest, fees, capital repayment, sale proceeds); (2) for each route, record the host tax, the withholding, the holding-company tax and the home-country tax, with credits; (3) add indirect taxes and transfer taxes that do not depend on profit; (4) total the leakage per route and in equity IRR points; (5) mark which entries depend on an incentive, a treaty or an election that can be withdrawn, and date each. When to use: at bid stage to set the tariff and at each refinancing or sale.
- **Framework 67.2, Pillar Two screen** (`fw:pillar-two-screen`). Five steps adapted from the t-pillar-two teaching angle (credit the OECD GloBE Model Rules for the underlying tests; the sequence is the book's): (1) is each sponsor group in scope (EUR 750 million) or an excluded entity; (2) what is the jurisdictional ETR after the holiday; (3) how much income does the SBIE shelter; (4) which jurisdiction collects the top-up (QDMTT, IIR, UTPR); (5) does the incentive qualify under the SBTI safe harbor, or is it an income-based incentive that does not. When to use: whenever a host offers an income-based holiday to a project whose sponsor is a large multinational.

## 67-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist:
- Confirm the legal source and date of every incentive in the bid model, and whether it is stabilized.
- Obtain the treaty text and any synthesized text; test the holding company's substance against what the treaty requires.
- Run thin-cap and interest-limitation tests for every year of the base case and the downside case.
- Price related-party contracts with documented benchmarks before signing, and delineate each shareholder loan (would an independent lender have advanced it on these terms?) before pricing it.
- Model VAT refunds with a realistic lag and a haircut.
- Run the Pillar Two screen for every sponsor group above EUR 750 million.
- For US credit projects, test ownership, lender identity and supply chain against the prohibited-foreign-entity rules each taxable year.

Red flags:
- A holiday granted by ministerial letter rather than by statute or a stabilized agreement.
- A holding company with no directors or decisions in its jurisdiction.
- Shareholder-loan rates set to the sponsors' target equity return.
- A tariff bid that assumes the holiday's full value passes to equity.
- Lender transfer clauses with no restriction on transfers to specified foreign entities in a US credit-dependent project.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Treaty access is worth the difference between domestic and treaty withholding on the distribution stream, discounted at the equity rate. Limit: substance costs and anti-abuse rules can remove it.
- A 30% EBITDA cap binds in early years for projects geared above about 70% with interest at 6% or more. Limit: depends on the EBITDA margin and amortization profile (Example 67.4).
- A full gross-up adds w/(1 - w) of the interest, about 11% of the interest bill at a 10% withholding rate (Example 67.2). Limit: lender credit capacity, exemptions and lending-office choice can remove it.

Common mistakes:
- Counting the holiday at its headline rate without minimum taxes, deferred depreciation rules or Pillar Two.
- Ignoring the refund lag when sizing construction funding.
- Treating a grandfathering clause as permanent when a refinancing that increases principal or term would lose it.
- Treating July 4, 2026 as the end of US wind and solar credits.
- Relying on a bank's letter quoting a rate as evidence that a shareholder-loan rate is arm's length (t-transfer-pricing fact 17).
- Quoting the OECD Model's 5%, 15% and 10% caps as if they were the rates of a given treaty.

Questions experts ask:
- Which tax in this structure depends on a decision a future government can reverse?
- What happens to our treaty rate if the treaty is renegotiated or the principal purpose test is applied?
- Who bears a change in withholding tax under the PPA, the facility agreement and the implementation agreement?
- If the holiday is clawed back by a minimum tax, does the tariff adjust?

## 67-12. Judgment drill

The situation: It is October 2026. You are head of tax at Lindqvist Kraft AB (fictional), a Stockholm-listed utility group, bidding for a 420 MW combined-cycle IPP in a Southeast Asian host (a composite country; its tax terms are Illustrative and describe no real state). The host offers an eight-year income tax holiday in the implementation agreement. Your group has EUR 4.1 billion of revenue and its parent is in an EU member state that applies an IIR. The host has announced but not enacted a QDMTT. The bid is due in three weeks, and the commercial team has priced the full holiday into a levelized tariff of USD 71.40/MWh. Your modeling shows that without the holiday the tariff would need to be USD 74.15/MWh for the same equity IRR.

Reasoning outline: apply Framework 67.2; holiday is income-based, so the SBTI safe harbor does not cover it; SBIE shelters part of the income in early years when carrying values are high; top-up arises in later holiday years as the asset depreciates; if the host enacts a QDMTT, the top-up stays in the host; if not, your home IIR collects it; either way part of the holiday does not reach equity. Expert answer: price the bid on the holiday net of expected top-up (compute with Equation 67.1 year by year), ask the host in clarification whether the holiday will be preserved through an expenditure-based credit (which can qualify) and whether a QDMTT is coming, and include a change-in-law tariff adjustment for minimum taxes in the PPA markup.

What would change the answer: host converts the holiday to an investment allowance (expenditure-based): more value survives; group revenue below the threshold for two of four years: out of scope; sponsor is a pension fund UPE: excluded entity (check the limits); competitor bidders out of scope will bid the full holiday: you lose the bid unless the tariff includes a minimum-tax adjustment.

## 68-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Chapter 29 introduced banks, ECAs, DFIs and institutional investors as lenders, and Chapter 38 taught how project debt is priced. This chapter supplies the reason behind much of both: a regulator's capital charge sets a floor under the margin a bank can accept and a spread an insurer can accept, and it does so differently for construction and operation, for slotting grades and standardized weights, for the EU, the UK and the United States, and for banks and insurers. The reader learns to convert a project's features into a risk weight or a capital charge, and that charge into a minimum price; to see which features (reserves, covenants, availability or take-or-pay revenue, step-in, ECA cover, MDB participation) move capital; and to predict who will hold a project's debt at each stage. All rules are stated as of October 3, 2026, with the US rules as proposals only.

What you will be able to do:

- Compute the capital a bank holds against a project loan under the standardised approach, supervisory slotting and the output floor, and the minimum margin that capital implies (Capabilities 5 and 14).

- Structure a project bond or loan to qualify for lower insurer capital under Solvency II or the UK matching adjustment, and judge whether the saving is worth the constraints (Capabilities 5 and 6).

## 68-8. Frameworks

- **Framework 68.1, Capital-to-price bridge** (`fw:capital-to-price-bridge`), original. Purpose: convert a project's features into the lowest margin a regulated lender can accept. Steps: (1) identify the lender type and jurisdiction; (2) find the capital route and the risk weight or capital factor for the project's phase and grade; (3) apply credit risk mitigation for covered portions; (4) compute capital per unit of exposure and its cost at the lender's hurdle; (5) add expected loss and operating cost; (6) compare with the market margin, and list the features that would move the weight one step. When to use: when choosing between bank and bond or insurer debt, when a sponsor asks why the margin will not fall, and when designing a refinancing.
- Credited established tools: Basel supervisory slotting criteria (BCBS; EU CRR Article 153(5)); Solvency II qualifying-infrastructure criteria (Delegated Regulation 2015/35 as amended).

## 68-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist:
- Confirm each lender's regulator, capital approach and whether it applies slotting or SA to project finance.
- Map the project's features against the high-quality and slotting criteria before the term sheet is final.
- Confirm eligibility of every credit risk mitigant the lenders count (ECA cover, MDB guarantee, PRI).
- Model capital by year, with the drop at completion.
- Draft the increased-costs clause with an express position on known future rule changes.

Red flags:
- A lender whose pricing depends on a transitional relief that ends during the loan's life (EU LGD-floor transitional to 2028; output floor phase-in to 2030).
- PRI counted as capital relief without a regulatory eligibility opinion.
- A US bank's pricing based on the March 2026 proposal as if it were final.
- An insurer's appetite that assumes infrastructure qualification for a merchant or demand-risk project.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- Construction-phase bank debt costs roughly 30% more capital than the same debt in operation under the SA ladder (130% to 100%), and more than 60% more than high-quality operational (130% to 80%). Limit: IRB and slotting banks differ.
- Insurers' sweet spot is investment-grade senior debt with availability, regulated or take-or-pay revenue. Limit: internal-model insurers and matching-adjustment portfolios follow their own rules.

Common mistakes:
- Confusing slotting EL rates with annual expected loss for pricing.
- Applying the output floor to a single loan rather than to total RWA.
- Assuming the 2025 to 2027 Solvency II review changed the infrastructure factors.
- Ignoring that the same loan may carry different capital in different jurisdictions of the same syndicate.

Questions experts ask:
- What grade does this project get at construction and at completion, and what moves it?
- Which tranche does the bank actually want to hold, and why?
- Will the bond qualify for the insurers' infrastructure treatment, and who checks it?
- Who bears a change in capital rules during the loan's life?

## 68-12. Judgment drill

The situation: You are the lead arranger on a EUR 1,240.0 million financing for a 900 MW battery-plus-solar project in Spain under a 12-year availability-style capacity contract with the system operator (Illustrative). Construction is 26 months. The sponsor wants a single 20-year bank loan at a construction margin of 175 bps and an operating margin of 150 bps, and asks why you are proposing instead a construction loan with a mini-perm structure and a refinancing into a private placement for insurers at completion. Credit committee meets in two weeks.

Reasoning outline: apply Framework 68.1; construction at 130% SA or a Satisfactory slot gives a minimum margin above 175 bps for most EU banks; at completion, high-quality operational treatment (80%, if Article 501a not applied) or the infrastructure supporting factor cuts capital; insurers can accept long tenors at lower spreads if the debt is investment grade and qualifies; a 20-year bank loan ties up capital the bank cannot price at 150 bps; the expert answer is the construction loan plus a refinancing plan with a margin step-up if refinancing is delayed (soft mini-perm, Chapter 36), and features designed now for insurer qualification (covenants, reserves, rating targets).

What would change the answer: ECA or EIB participation in construction (capital relief cuts the construction margin); a merchant tail after the capacity contract (insurer qualification fails; banks keep the tail); US-regulated lenders under a final rule with no output floor; rising rates that make the refinancing uncertain (keep a longer bank tenor).

## 84-2. Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Labels now sit on a large share of project debt: green loans and bonds, sustainability-linked facilities, European Green Bonds, and since November 2025 climate transition bonds. Each label is a promise about how money is used or how a borrower will perform, set by a market body or a legislator and checked by a reviewer. Climate risk, meanwhile, has moved from the E&S appendix into the credit case: a flood at a coastal plant, a hotter cooling-water intake, or a carbon price on a gas plant change CFADS. This chapter teaches the reader to tell the labels apart by who sets them, what they certify and who checks them; to structure a labeled project financing and keep it labeled; to assess physical and transition climate risk in numbers a credit committee can use; to judge carbon credits as a revenue line; and to recognize when a label creates legal and credit risk instead of a lower price.

What you will be able to do:

- Choose and structure the right sustainable label for a project financing, from a green loan to a European Green Bond, and keep it compliant through the life of the debt (Capabilities 5 and 6).
- Quantify a project's physical and transition climate risks and carry them into the base case, the downside case and the credit paper (Capabilities 2, 4 and 14).
- Value carbon credits as project revenue under Article 6 and the voluntary market, with the deductions and integrity risks that cut them (Capabilities 12 and 13).
- Spot greenwashing risk in a framework, a KPI or a claim, and explain why it becomes credit risk (Capability 15).

**Example 84.2 The economics of a sustainability-linked margin ratchet (Illustrative)** (`ex:84.2`). ## 84-7. Walkthrough

Section 84.11, Walkthrough: reading a green financing framework and its second-party opinion. Artifact: an Illustrative green financing framework for a portfolio borrower (written for the book, not reproduced from any real issuer) and the SPO's summary. Steps:

1. Identify the standard claimed (GBP, GLP, EuGB) and the edition.
2. Read the eligible categories and exclusions; check each against the borrower's actual assets and capex plan.
3. Read the selection process: who decides, with what E&S screen (Chapter 50).
4. Read management of proceeds: tracking account or ledger, treatment of unallocated proceeds, look-back period for refinancing.
5. Read the reporting commitments: allocation and impact metrics, frequency, external verification.
6. Read the SPO: scope, alignment conclusion, the reviewer's own reservations, and what it did not review.
7. List the gaps between framework and facility documents (Clause 84.1).
8. Decide whether the label adds investors, and at what cost.

## 84-8. Frameworks

- **Framework 84.1, Three-question label test** (`fw:label-test`), original (built on the fact sheet's teaching angle). Steps: (1) Who sets the standard: a market association or a legislator? (2) What does the label certify: a process, a use of proceeds, a performance against targets, or alignment with a legal taxonomy? (3) Who checks it, when, and with what liability: a second-party opinion, a verifier, an ESMA-registered reviewer? Then (4) what happens to the borrower if the label fails. When to use: before choosing a label, and before buying or lending into one.
- **Framework 84.2, Project climate risk screen** (`fw:project-climate-screen`), original, using the TCFD physical and transition categories (credit the TCFD categories in the prose; EP4 aligns its climate change risk assessment with them, t-es-standards fact 12). Steps: (1) list the hazards at the site (acute and chronic) and the transition channels for the product; (2) for each, estimate exposure and vulnerability over the debt tenor, not the project life only; (3) quantify the top three in cash-flow terms (expected annual loss, derate, carbon cost); (4) check who bears each in the contracts (PPA change in law, insurance, EPC design); (5) build a climate downside case and test DSCR and LLCR; (6) set mitigants and covenants (design margins, insurance, reserves, reporting). When to use: at screening for every long-lived asset, and in the IE's report for lenders.

## 84-11. Practitioner's notebook (outline)

Checklist:
- Decide the label with Framework 84.1 before mandating an SPO provider.
- Map every use of proceeds to an eligible category and keep the evidence.
- Put the reporting obligations in the facility agreement and the accounts structure.
- Run the project climate risk screen and build the climate downside case before credit approval.
- For carbon revenue, confirm methodology status, host authorization and corresponding adjustments.

Red flags:
- A framework whose eligible categories are broader than the borrower's actual assets.
- SPTs that the borrower already meets at signing.
- Carbon credits counted in the sizing case without a fixed-price offtake.
- A coastal or riverine asset with no flood study beyond historical data.
- Claims in marketing materials that go further than the framework.

Rules of thumb and their limits:
- In single-asset project finance, the green label follows the asset; sustainability-linked structures belong at holdco or portfolio level. Limit: portfolio opcos with real operational KPIs.
- A label widens the investor base more reliably than it lowers the price. Limit: no verified greenium data in the fact sheets; check current market data.
- Physical risk should be assessed over the debt tenor with a scenario that changes hazard frequency, not only over historical records. Limit: scenario choice drives the answer.

Common mistakes:
- Treating an SPO as a guarantee of compliance.
- Confusing EuGB alignment with GBP alignment.
- Ignoring chronic physical risk because no single event is large.
- Counting gross emission reductions instead of credits issued after deductions.

Questions experts ask:
- What exactly does the label certify, and who is liable if it is wrong?
- Which assets would the borrower like to add later, and would they break the label?
- What does the climate downside case do to the minimum DSCR?
- Who bears a carbon price under the offtake contract?

## 84-12. Judgment drill

The situation: You are the lead arranger on a USD 860.0 million refinancing for a portfolio of eight operating wind and solar projects in Mexico (Illustrative). The sponsor wants a sustainability-linked loan with three KPIs (availability above 97%, a local hiring percentage, and annual GWh generated) and a 7.5 bps total ratchet, and says this will attract two ESG-focused banks. Your E&S team notes that two of the three KPIs are already met. Pricing is due Friday.

Reasoning outline: apply Framework 84.1 and the 2025 SLLP "shall be ambitious"; KPIs already met fail ambition and expose all lenders to greenwashing criticism; for a portfolio of eligible renewable assets a green loan under the GLP is the cleaner label; if the sponsor wants a performance link, choose KPIs material and not yet met (for example biodiversity or community outcomes under a credible baseline) and accept a verification cost; the expert answer is a green loan with optional SLL features only for ambitious, verifiable targets.

What would change the answer: KPIs recalibrated to stretch levels with an external baseline; a lender base that requires a performance-linked product for its own targets; new acquisitions of non-eligible assets planned (green label at risk; SLL fits a changing portfolio better).
