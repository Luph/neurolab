### 1.A.8 Sizing outcome

- DSCR test alone: present value at 5.40% of (CFADS_t / 1.30) over 2018 to 2035 = USD 51,840 thousand.
- Gearing test: 75% x 65,078 = USD 48,809 thousand (total uses themselves depend on debt through fees, IDC and DSRA; solved iteratively).
- The smaller wins: debt USD 48,809 thousand; gearing binds; the DSCR test has USD 3,031 thousand of slack, so the sculpted DSCR is 1.38x every year, not 1.30x.
- LLCR at COD (CFADS 2018 to 2035 discounted at 5.40%, divided by debt) = 1.38x. Ch 1 shows this number only if the writer introduces LLCR intuitively with a forward reference to Ch 35; optional.
- OY1 downside checks (same debt service of 4,715): one-year P90 output 123.0 GWh, revenue 7,158, tax 75, CFADS 5,997, DSCR 1.27x; one-year P99 output 113.4 GWh, revenue 6,601, tax 0, CFADS 5,516, DSCR 1.17x (passes the 1.00x P99 test).
- OY1 output at which DSCR hits the lock-up (1.15x): 111.8 GWh (83.0% of P50); default (1.05x): 103.7 GWh (77.0%); 1.00x: 99.7 GWh (74.0%).

### 1.2 Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Give a reader who knows nothing a complete, coherent picture of one small project financing before the book takes it apart. The reader follows Llano Pardo Solar, a 50 MW solar plant in the fictional Republic of Corredana, from an option on a patch of plateau in 2014 through a government tender, the PPA, the EPC contract, a simple model, debt sizing, the term sheet, financial close, construction, a disappointing first year, and repayment to 2035. Every number reconciles and every concept is introduced in plain words with a pointer to the chapter that teaches it in full, so each later chapter has a place on this map to attach to. The chapter ends with the opening of Case P.

What you will be able to do (binding; print these three lines):

- Explain, stage by stage, how a contracted solar plant is developed, financed, built, and repaid, and who carries which risk at each stage (Capability 1).

### 1.11 Practitioner's notebook (`sec:1.13`)

- Checklist: eight questions to ask of any small contracted renewable deal at first sight (who buys, at what price, for how long; how good is the resource record; who builds, at what fixed price, with what LDs; what is the binding sizing test; how much equity is at risk before close; what happens to cash in a P90 year; when does the debt end relative to the contract; what does the sponsor's return depend on that lenders ignore).
- Red flags: a PPA shorter than tenor plus two years; less than 12 months of on-site resource data; delay LDs below daily revenue plus daily interest; letter of credit sized below the offtaker's normal payment cycle plus cure period; a bid return that clears the hurdle only with a merchant tail; an O&M guarantee that excludes the losses most likely at the site.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: delay LDs sized to daily revenue plus daily interest (limit: ignores fixed costs and PPA penalties, which Ch 22 adds); debt sized at the lower of DSCR capacity and gearing cap (limit: a third test, such as P99 or LLCR, can bind instead); a two-year tail between maturity and contract expiry (limit: varies with lender type and contract quality, Ch 36).
- Common mistakes: treating the facility amount in the term sheet as the loan; forgetting that fees, IDC and the DSRA increase total uses and so the gearing-capped debt; reading DSCR from P50 only.
- Questions experts ask: "What breaks first in a bad year, the lock-up or the DSRA?"; "Which of these contracts would you want to read tonight?"; "Who loses money if the grid connection is six months late?"

### 1.12 Judgment drill (`sec:1.14`)

Situation (second person, deadline): You are Tallisford's investment director, October 2015, five days before bids close. Your model shows an equity IRR of 10.2% at USD 58.20/MWh with the merchant tail and 8.9% without. Grupo Arismendi's chief executive wants to bid USD 56.40/MWh because a competitor is rumored at USD 57.00. Your investment committee hurdle is 10.0% including the tail. Figures the reader is given (all computed; gearing binds at every price shown, so debt stays at about USD 48.81 million):

| Tariff (USD/MWh) | Equity IRR, PPA only | Equity IRR with tail |
|---|---|---|
| 55.00 | 6.9% | 8.6% |
| 56.40 | 7.8% | 9.3% |
| 57.00 | 8.2% | 9.6% |
| 57.75 | 8.7% | 10.0% |
| 58.20 | 8.9% | 10.2% |
| 60.00 | 10.1% | 11.2% |

Reasoning to outline: the IRR falls fast with the tariff because gearing, not DSCR, binds, so every dollar of lost revenue comes off equity and a lower tariff buys no extra debt; the merchant tail is unverifiable and lenders give it no value, so a bid that clears the hurdle only with the tail is already a judgment call; winning at a price below the hurdle is the winner's curse (forward to Ch 47). The expert answer: do not go below USD 57.75/MWh, the price at which the IRR with tail is exactly 10.0%; prefer to hold USD 58.20; before cutting price, look for value in the EPC price, operating costs and gearing. What would change the answer: a firm EPC price reduction (raises the IRR at every tariff); lenders agreeing to gearing of about 80% (helps only while DSCR capacity, USD 51.84 million at USD 58.20, exceeds the higher cap, which it does up to gearing of roughly 80%; the writer states "roughly 80%" and computes nothing further); a strategic reason to enter Corredana, such as a pipeline of later tenders, that justifies a lower first-deal return and must be approved as such, not hidden in the tail.

### 2.2 Purpose and capabilities

The chapter turns Chapter 1's informal vocabulary into exact concepts (the project company and its ring-fence; non-, limited and full recourse; the contractual web; the principle of allocating each risk to the party best able to manage it), sets project finance against its six neighbors, and prices its benefits against its costs with numbers, so the reader can explain to a board or a finance minister why a sponsor would pay roughly 100 basis points a year more, and wait a year longer, to borrow this way.

What you will be able to do (binding):

### 2.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 2.1 The recourse ladder** (`fw:recourse-ladder`), original. Steps: list every route by which lenders can reach the sponsors (equity commitment, contingent equity, cost-overrun undertaking, completion guarantee, debt guarantee); for each, record trigger, cap and release date; plot the sponsor's maximum loss before and after completion; compare with the sponsor's appetite and its covenant and rating headroom. Use: whenever a term sheet asks for sponsor support. Demonstrated in Example 2.2.
- **Framework 2.2 The project finance fit test** (`fw:pf-fit-test`), original. Six questions, in order of how often they kill a deal: (1) is the asset legally and physically separable, with its own revenues; (2) is the revenue contracted or predictable enough for lenders to forecast it for longer than the debt; (3) can construction risk be fixed in price and date with a creditworthy contractor; (4) is the deal large enough to carry fixed transaction costs (show the ratio from Examples 2.4 and 2.5 rather than a threshold); (5) can the sponsors accept lender control and information demands; (6) can the legal system give lenders enforceable security and step-in. A "no" to (1) or (2) means another tool; a "no" to (3) to (6) means a fix or a price. Demonstrated on Llano Pardo (passes), the Nairobi rooftops (fails 4), and Northvolt Ett (strains 3 and 2: manufacturing ramp-up risk, and customer orders reported at USD 55 billion that gave no take-or-pay protection once the plant could not deliver on specification and on time; fact sheet `northvolt`).

- `sabine-pass` (`ssec:2.3.3`). - `hyperion-meta` (`ssec:2.4.5`). ### 2.11 Practitioner's notebook (`sec:2.10`)

- Checklist: questions to confirm a structure is truly ring-fenced (single purpose in the articles; no guarantees given for affiliates; separate bank accounts; independent director or reserved matters if required; arm's-length contracts with affiliates; no commingling of cash; sponsor support documents listed with caps and expiry).
- Red flags: affiliate contracts without market terms (EPC or O&M by a sponsor affiliate with weak LDs); a sponsor that needs the project's distributions to service its own debt; "non-recourse" debt with a long list of sponsor undertakings; cross-default from the project to the sponsor's corporate facilities.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: transaction costs above about 5% of capex make project finance hard to justify (limit: derived from the examples here, not market data; scale effects vary by jurisdiction and lender type); recourse should end at completion (limit: technology and resource risk can justify support beyond it).
- Common mistakes: confusing off-balance-sheet with off-consolidated accounts; treating the risk allocation principle as "give risk to whoever signs"; ignoring that the counterparty must be able to absorb the risk it takes.
- Questions experts ask: "If the sponsor went bankrupt tomorrow, what would the project company stop receiving?"; "Which contract replaces the balance sheet for this risk?"; "What does the sponsor lose if this fails, in dollars, before and after completion?"

### 3.6 Worked examples

All four are `\illustrative`, with fictional parties; none is a historical example.

- **Example 3.1 How a production payment works** (`ex:3.1`). In January 2024 Tessaway Oil Company LLC (fictional), a producer in Oklahoma's Anadarko Basin, sells a production payment to Halvergate Energy Credit LP (fictional), a Houston credit fund. Halvergate advances USD 28.4 million against 45% of the field's oil output until it recovers the advance plus 9.5% a year; it has no claim on Tessaway beyond that oil. Output: 412,000; 361,000; 317,000; 279,000; 246,000; 218,000 bbl in years 1 to 6. Mechanics: each year the balance grows by 9.5% and is then reduced by the payment, which is the lesser of the balance and 45% of output times the realized price. At USD 62.40/bbl, payments are 11,569.0; 10,136.9; 8,901.4; 3,738.9 (USD k), with balances after payment of 19,529.0; 11,247.4; 3,414.6; 0: the advance is cleared in year 4. At USD 41.20/bbl the payments are 7,638.5; 6,692.9; 5,877.2; 5,172.7; 4,560.8; 4,041.7, and USD 4,354 thousand remains unpaid when the six-year production profile ends. Lesson: the lender carries price and reserve risk directly; this is the ancestor of reserve-based lending (forward to `sec:75.3`), and the same structure is still used (fact sheet `h-production-payments`, volumetric production payments). The example states that it is a modern illustration of the mechanism, not a historical deal.
- **Example 3.2 A dollar tariff meets a devaluation** (`ex:3.2`; written to echo but not reproduce Paiton; use no Paiton numbers). Selat Ombak Power Company (fictional) sells power to Lembaga Elektrik Pasundra (LEP, fictional), the state utility of the Republic of Pasundra (fictional South-East Asian republic; currency the Pasundran kati, code PSK, not an ISO 4217 code). LEP owes Selat Ombak USD 9.37 million a month and collects PSK 61.3 billion a month from customers. At PSK 2,437 per USD (June 1997) the bill is PSK 22.83 billion, 37.3% of collections; at PSK 10,815 per USD (June 1998) it is PSK 101.34 billion, 165.3% of collections, a 4.44-fold rise. Lesson: the currency risk the PPA removed from the lenders went to the party least able to bear it, then came back as payment risk. Forward to Ch 59.
- **Example 3.3 The debt trap** (`ex:3.3`). Concesionaria Túnel Cordillera Norte SA (fictional) holds a privately financed rail tunnel concession in the Republic of Corredana (the dollarized country of Chapter 1), scheduled for completion in March 2029. It will have USD 2,386.4 million of debt drawn at scheduled completion, with interest at 7.85% a year capitalized while there is no revenue. A delay adds interest of USD 91.9 million at 6 months, USD 187.3 million at 12 months and USD 286.4 million at 18 months (2,386.4 x (1.0785^(m/12) - 1)), before any lost revenue or cost overrun. Lesson: delay on a heavily geared project compounds into debt that later traffic must repay (Flyvbjerg's "debt trap", fact sheet `eurotunnel`). Forward to Ch 22 and Ch 61 on delay protection.
- **Example 3.4 What a longer tenor buys** (`ex:3.4`). In 1995 Selat Ombak Power Company (Example 3.2) borrows USD 186.3 million at 8.15% a year, repaid in equal annual installments of principal plus interest: over 12 years, USD 24.91 million a year; over 23 years, USD 18.18 million, 27.0% less. ### 3.7 Walkthrough: reading a post-mortem of a landmark deal (`sec:3.11`)

Artifact: a World Bank Implementation Completion Report (ICR), using the Hub Power ICR cited in fact sheet `hub-power`. Steps:

1. Identify what the document is, who wrote it, when, and for whom (the lender's own evaluation, written after the fact; note its institutional interest).
2. Find the project's stated objectives and compare them with outcomes (on-schedule COD on March 31, 1997; actual cost of about USD 1.6 billion against a budget of USD 1.8 billion including standby; never use the two figures interchangeably).
3. Extract the financing structure and identify what was new (first partial risk guarantee; subordinated long-term credit fund with 23-year maturities).
4. Read the disputes section and apply Framework 3.1: what risk materialized, which contract was meant to carry it, who paid (equity return cut, debt service protected).
5. Read the lessons section critically: the ICR's own lessons (start smaller, avoid the lender acting as promoter, check affordability) and what the report has reason to understate.
6. Write a five-line note of transferable lessons for a current deal (Case P is the obvious one).

### 3.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 3.1 The loss trace** (`fw:loss-trace`), original. Steps: (1) name the event (what happened, when); (2) name the risk category (forward to Ch 14's taxonomy); (3) find the contract or instrument that was meant to carry it; (4) find who actually paid, and how much where known; (5) explain the gap between (3) and (4) (counterparty could not pay, clause did not reach, a court or government overrode it); (6) record the practice the market adopted afterwards. Use: on any failed or renegotiated deal, before using it as a precedent. Demonstrated on Dabhol (affordability; PPA and guarantees; foreign lenders and equity bought out in 2005 by Indian state-owned interests; gap: guarantees depended on the same fiscal capacity; practice: competitive procurement and sizing to demand) and on Eurotunnel (construction and demand; no state support by design; shareholders and lenders through two restructurings; practice: conservative traffic cases, larger contingencies, smaller syndicates). Ch 28's "Who pays if...?" trace (`fw:who-pays-if`) is the forward-looking twin; cite it. Ch 28 and Ch 64 cite this framework (R-111).

### 3.11 Practitioner's notebook (`sec:3.13`)

- Checklist: questions to ask of any precedent a counterparty cites ("this structure worked on X"): date and market; who was the offtaker and its credit then; what the currency and tariff basis were; whether it was ever stress-tested; what happened to equity and to debt.
- Red flags: a structure whose only precedent closed in a different interest-rate or currency regime; reliance on a sovereign or state guarantee without evidence the guarantor can pay in the stress case; a megaproject syndicate so large that waivers need hundreds of consents; a first-of-a-kind technology financed as if mature; a funding channel that depends on one class of credit enhancer (the 2008 monolines).
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "the lenders' downside case should include the last crisis in that country" (limit: the next crisis is rarely the same shape); "debt survives renegotiation better than equity" (limit: true at Hub and Paiton, false at Eurotunnel, where junior debt converted to equity).
- Common mistakes: reading history as morality instead of mechanism; citing a case from secondary press when the fact sheet says otherwise; assuming contractual protection equals recovery.
- Questions experts ask: "When did this structure last fail, and who paid?"; "What would have to be true for this deal to repeat Dabhol?"; "Which party here plays the role WAPDA, MSEB or PLN played?"

### 4.2 Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Teach the reader to sit in every chair. The chapter introduces every party to a project financing, and for each one says how it earns money, what it fears, what it will trade, and how it behaves when the project goes wrong, with a real case of that party under stress wherever a fact sheet allows. It then lays out the project lifecycle from origination to decommissioning or handback as a sequence of gates, each with a decision, money at risk, the documents that matter and the parties in control. The reader leaves able to predict how each party will react to a given event, and to say at any date in a project's life what has been committed, what can still be lost, and who decides.

What you will be able to do (binding):

- Name every party to a project financing and explain how each earns money, what each fears, and how each behaves under stress (Capabilities 1 and 15).
- Place any project on its lifecycle, say what must be true to pass the next gate, and estimate what is at risk if it fails (Capabilities 2 and 10).
- Predict which party moves first, and how, when a project hits a delay, a payment default or a contractor failure (Capability 15).

- Red flags: a sponsor that is also the EPC contractor or O&M operator without independent oversight; an advisor paid by one party but relied on by another without a reliance letter; a lender group so large or so mixed (banks, ECAs, DFIs, bondholders) that no one can give a quick waiver; an offtaker whose payment depends on a budget line no one has checked; development spending ahead of the gate it serves.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: development spending is usually a small fraction of total cost but entirely at risk (Llano Pardo: 4.1% of total uses; limit: offshore wind, nuclear and LNG are far higher); refinancing follows de-risking at completion and after a track record (limit: market rates can make it uneconomic).
- Common mistakes: treating "the government" as one party; assuming an agent or trustee will exercise judgment; forgetting that insurers and hedge providers are creditors with their own interests; confusing COD with completion.
- Questions experts ask: "Who on this list gets paid even if the project fails?"; "Who can stop this deal at the next gate?"; "If the contractor disappeared tomorrow, who would finish the plant, and with whose money?"
