### 1.A.8 Sizing outcome

- DSCR test alone: present value at 5.40% of (CFADS_t / 1.30) over 2018 to 2035 = USD 51,840 thousand.
- Gearing test: 75% x 65,078 = USD 48,809 thousand (total uses themselves depend on debt through fees, IDC and DSRA; solved iteratively).
- The smaller wins: debt USD 48,809 thousand; gearing binds; the DSCR test has USD 3,031 thousand of slack, so the sculpted DSCR is 1.38x every year, not 1.30x.
- LLCR at COD (CFADS 2018 to 2035 discounted at 5.40%, divided by debt) = 1.38x. Ch 1 shows this number only if the writer introduces LLCR intuitively with a forward reference to Ch 35; optional.
- OY1 downside checks (same debt service of 4,715): one-year P90 output 123.0 GWh, revenue 7,158, tax 75, CFADS 5,997, DSCR 1.27x; one-year P99 output 113.4 GWh, revenue 6,601, tax 0, CFADS 5,516, DSCR 1.17x (passes the 1.00x P99 test).
- OY1 output at which DSCR hits the lock-up (1.15x): 111.8 GWh (83.0% of P50); default (1.05x): 103.7 GWh (77.0%); 1.00x: 99.7 GWh (74.0%).

- Estimate how much debt one year of cash flow can carry at a given cover ratio, and see why lenders apply more than one sizing test (Capability 5).

### 1.11 Practitioner's notebook (`sec:1.13`)

- Checklist: eight questions to ask of any small contracted renewable deal at first sight (who buys, at what price, for how long; how good is the resource record; who builds, at what fixed price, with what LDs; what is the binding sizing test; how much equity is at risk before close; what happens to cash in a P90 year; when does the debt end relative to the contract; what does the sponsor's return depend on that lenders ignore).
- Red flags: a PPA shorter than tenor plus two years; less than 12 months of on-site resource data; delay LDs below daily revenue plus daily interest; letter of credit sized below the offtaker's normal payment cycle plus cure period; a bid return that clears the hurdle only with a merchant tail; an O&M guarantee that excludes the losses most likely at the site.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: delay LDs sized to daily revenue plus daily interest (limit: ignores fixed costs and PPA penalties, which Ch 22 adds); debt sized at the lower of DSCR capacity and gearing cap (limit: a third test, such as P99 or LLCR, can bind instead); a two-year tail between maturity and contract expiry (limit: varies with lender type and contract quality, Ch 36).
- Common mistakes: treating the facility amount in the term sheet as the loan; forgetting that fees, IDC and the DSRA increase total uses and so the gearing-capped debt; reading DSCR from P50 only.
- Questions experts ask: "What breaks first in a bad year, the lock-up or the DSRA?"; "Which of these contracts would you want to read tonight?"; "Who loses money if the grid connection is six months late?"

### 1.12 Judgment drill (`sec:1.14`)

Situation (second person, deadline): You are Tallisford's investment director, October 2015, five days before bids close. Your model shows an equity IRR of 10.2% at USD 58.20/MWh with the merchant tail and 8.9% without. Grupo Arismendi's chief executive wants to bid USD 56.40/MWh because a competitor is rumored at USD 57.00. Your investment committee hurdle is 10.0% including the tail. Figures the reader is given (all computed; gearing binds at every price shown, so debt stays at about USD 48.81 million):

| Tariff (USD/MWh) | Equity IRR, PPA only | Equity IRR with tail |
|---|---|---|
| 55.00 | 6.9% | 8.6% |
| 56.40 | 7.8% | 9.3% |
| 57.00 | 8.2% | 9.6% |
| 57.75 | 8.7% | 10.0% |
| 58.20 | 8.9% | 10.2% |
| 60.00 | 10.1% | 11.2% |

Reasoning to outline: the IRR falls fast with the tariff because gearing, not DSCR, binds, so every dollar of lost revenue comes off equity and a lower tariff buys no extra debt; the merchant tail is unverifiable and lenders give it no value, so a bid that clears the hurdle only with the tail is already a judgment call; winning at a price below the hurdle is the winner's curse (forward to Ch 47). The expert answer: do not go below USD 57.75/MWh, the price at which the IRR with tail is exactly 10.0%; prefer to hold USD 58.20; before cutting price, look for value in the EPC price, operating costs and gearing. What would change the answer: a firm EPC price reduction (raises the IRR at every tariff); lenders agreeing to gearing of about 80% (helps only while DSCR capacity, USD 51.84 million at USD 58.20, exceeds the higher cap, which it does up to gearing of roughly 80%; the writer states "roughly 80%" and computes nothing further); a strategic reason to enter Corredana, such as a pipeline of later tenders, that justifies a lower first-deal return and must be approved as such, not hidden in the tail.

### 2.2 Purpose and capabilities

Purpose. Define project finance precisely and teach the reader to decide when it is the right tool. The chapter turns Chapter 1's informal vocabulary into exact concepts (the project company and its ring-fence; non-, limited and full recourse; the contractual web; the principle of allocating each risk to the party best able to manage it), sets project finance against its six neighbors, and prices its benefits against its costs with numbers, so the reader can explain to a board or a finance minister why a sponsor would pay roughly 100 basis points a year more, and wait a year longer, to borrow this way.

What you will be able to do (suggested):

- Explain what project finance is, how ring-fencing and limited recourse work, and how it differs from corporate finance, leasing, reserve-based lending, acquisition finance, securitization and structured finance (Capability 1).
- Compare the cost, time and control burdens of project finance with balance-sheet borrowing for a given project, and say when project finance is the wrong tool (Capabilities 1 and 15).
- Place any risk on a project with the party best able to manage it, and recognize when that principle fails in practice (Capability 2).

### 2.3 Concepts owned and assumed

Owned (taught in full; first bold use here):

- Project finance (definition: debt repaid from the cash flows of a single ring-fenced project, secured on its assets and contracts, with limited or no recourse to the sponsors).

### 2.6 Worked examples

All `\illustrative` unless a real case.

- **Example 2.1 Two ways to fund a wind farm** (`ex:2.1`). A generator listed in Santiago, reporting in US dollars, with EBITDA of USD 182.4 million and net debt of USD 510.6 million (2.80x), and a bank covenant capping net debt at 3.50x EBITDA. It plans a 240 MW wind farm in the Biobío region costing USD 389.6 million, earning EBITDA of USD 41.3 million a year once running. Corporate route (all borrowed at parent): net debt 900.2, leverage 4.94x during construction (no project EBITDA yet) and 4.02x after COD (900.2 / 223.7): covenant breached in both. Headroom at 3.50x: 3.50 x 182.4 - 510.6 = USD 127.8 million. Project finance route: project company borrows 75% non-recourse, USD 292.2 million; sponsor equity USD 97.4 million, funded with parent debt: parent leverage (510.6 + 97.4) / 182.4 = 3.33x, inside the covenant (project debt excluded because it is non-recourse; state that rating agencies may still look through, forward to Ch 30). Then the failure case: serial gearbox failures make the project worthless to equity. Project finance: the sponsor loses USD 97.4 million and the project lenders bear the rest. Corporate route: the sponsor still owes USD 389.6 million with no asset behind it.
- **Example 2.2 Climbing the recourse ladder** (`ex:2.2`). Same wind farm; a cost overrun of 14%, USD 54.5 million, arises before completion. Sponsor's maximum loss on three rungs: full recourse (corporate route): USD 389.6 million plus the overrun; limited recourse with a cost-overrun undertaking capped at 15% of capex (USD 58.4 million): 97.4 + 54.5 = USD 151.9 million before completion, USD 97.4 million after; non-recourse: USD 97.4 million. Show the full completion guarantee as the rung between full recourse and the capped undertaking (the sponsor guarantees the USD 292.2 million of debt until completion; exposure falls to USD 97.4 million at completion). Instruments owned by Ch 26.
- **Example 2.3 Who should carry module soiling at Llano Pardo?** (`ex:2.3`). Apply the three tests to the soiling loss in `ex:1.9` (USD 441 thousand of lost distributions): the O&M operator controls cleaning frequency (control), can price extra cleaning cycles (pricing), has a balance sheet of a few million dollars (capacity); ELNACOR controls nothing; the EPC contractor's performance test is long passed. Conclusion: the O&M operator should carry it up to a cap, with the sponsors keeping weather-driven variation above the cap. Shows how the principle gives a precise answer and where capacity to absorb limits it. No new numbers beyond Ch 1's.
- **Example 2.4 What project finance costs on the wind farm** (`ex:2.4`). Project finance: lenders' advisors USD 3.86 million (legal 2.85, technical 0.62, model audit 0.18, insurance 0.21); sponsor's advisors 3.40; upfront fees 2.25% of 292.2 = 6.57; total 13.83 (3.55% of capex). Corporate bond route: issuance fee 0.45% of 389.6 plus legal 0.60 = 2.35. Spread over a 9.4-year average life, undiscounted and straight-line (state that Ch 38 does this properly): project finance 13.83 / (292.2 x 9.4) = 50.4 bps a year; corporate 2.35 / (389.6 x 9.4) = 6.4 bps. Margins: project loan 195 bps against the corporate bond's 135 bps credit spread (Illustrative, not market data). All-in difference about 104 bps a year (plus an agency fee of USD 75,000 a year, 2.6 bps), about USD 3.0 million a year on USD 292.2 million. Time to funds: about 14 months against about 3. All figures Illustrative; the writer must not present them as market norms.
- **Example 2.5 Too small for project finance** (`ex:2.5`). 214 rooftop solar systems on supermarkets and warehouses around Nairobi, 11.8 MWp, USD 18.6 million, each with its own 15-year contract. A stand-alone project financing would carry fixed transaction costs of about USD 2.9 million, 15.6% of capex. Alternatives: a corporate facility to the developer, a warehouse facility that aggregates assets until a portfolio is big enough, or a securitization once there are thousands of contracts (forward to Ch 31 and Ch 82 as relevant). Lesson: fixed costs, not risk, kill small project financings.

### 2.8 Frameworks

- **Framework 2.1 The recourse ladder** (`fw:recourse-ladder`), original. Steps: list every route by which lenders can reach the sponsors (equity commitment, contingent equity, cost-overrun undertaking, completion guarantee, debt guarantee); for each, record trigger, cap and release date; plot the sponsor's maximum loss before and after completion; compare with the sponsor's appetite and its covenant and rating headroom. Use: whenever a term sheet asks for sponsor support. Demonstrated in Example 2.2.
- **Framework 2.2 The project finance fit test** (`fw:pf-fit-test`), original. Six questions, in order of how often they kill a deal: (1) is the asset legally and physically separable, with its own revenues; (2) is the revenue contracted or predictable enough for lenders to forecast it for longer than the debt; (3) can construction risk be fixed in price and date with a creditworthy contractor; (4) is the deal large enough to carry fixed transaction costs (show the ratio from Examples 2.4 and 2.5 rather than a threshold); (5) can the sponsors accept lender control and information demands; (6) can the legal system give lenders enforceable security and step-in. A "no" to (1) or (2) means another tool; a "no" to (3) to (6) means a fix or a price. Demonstrated on Llano Pardo (passes), the Nairobi rooftops (fails 4), and Northvolt Ett (strains 3 and 2: manufacturing ramp-up and offtake that was not take-or-pay once the plant could not deliver; fact sheet `northvolt`).

### 2.9 Real cases

- `sunedison-terraform` (opening and `ssec:2.1.3`). Lesson: ring-fencing protected the operating assets' debt service from the sponsor's insolvency, but did not protect the listed vehicles from governance disputes and delisting threats, because the ring-fence was a matter of contracts and corporate separateness, and the sponsor's control rights (high-vote shares, incentive distribution rights) crossed it. Chapter 32 owns the yieldco structure; Ch 2 uses only the ring-fence angle.
- `sabine-pass` (`ssec:2.3.3`). Lesson: a merchant-minded developer made liquefaction financeable by contracting fixed fees from buyers that pay whether or not they lift cargoes; in 2020 buyers cancelled cargoes and kept paying, which is the contractual web working. Use the sheet's figures only (four 20-year SPAs signed October 2011 to January 2012; 115% of Henry Hub plus a fixed fee of USD 2.25 to 3.00 per MMBtu; Cheniere's USD 932 million of revenue from cancelled cargoes in the first nine months of 2020, consolidated and not attributed to Sabine Pass alone).
- `hyperion-meta` (`ssec:2.4.5`). Lesson: a structure can sit off a sponsor's balance sheet while the sponsor keeps large economic exposure (Meta's disclosed maximum exposure to loss of USD 46.03 billion at June 30, 2026); lenders underwrote a short lease plus a residual value guarantee, not a long-term offtake contract, which makes it structured finance with project features. Never say Meta guarantees the bonds; never state the bond size, coupon or rating.
- `northvolt` (`sec:2.7`). Lesson: project finance structure (ECA tranches, DSRA, hedging) cannot turn manufacturing ramp-up risk into contracted cash flow; the fit test's questions (2) and (3) failed. Ch 77 owns the full case.

### 2.11 Practitioner's notebook (`sec:2.10`)

- Checklist: questions to confirm a structure is truly ring-fenced (single purpose in the articles; no guarantees given for affiliates; separate bank accounts; independent director or reserved matters if required; arm's-length contracts with affiliates; no commingling of cash; sponsor support documents listed with caps and expiry).
- Red flags: affiliate contracts without market terms (EPC or O&M by a sponsor affiliate with weak LDs); a sponsor that needs the project's distributions to service its own debt; "non-recourse" debt with a long list of sponsor undertakings; cross-default from the project to the sponsor's corporate facilities.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: transaction costs above about 5% of capex make project finance hard to justify (limit: derived from the examples here, not market data; scale effects vary by jurisdiction and lender type); recourse should end at completion (limit: technology and resource risk can justify support beyond it).
- Common mistakes: confusing off-balance-sheet with off-consolidated accounts; treating the risk allocation principle as "give risk to whoever signs"; ignoring that the counterparty must be able to absorb the risk it takes.
- Questions experts ask: "If the sponsor went bankrupt tomorrow, what would the project company stop receiving?"; "Which contract replaces the balance sheet for this risk?"; "What does the sponsor lose if this fails, in dollars, before and after completion?"

### 2.12 Judgment drill (`sec:2.11`)

Situation: You are the CFO of the Santiago-listed generator in Example 2.1. Your relationship bank offers a corporate term loan of USD 389.6 million at 160 bps with a one-off covenant relaxation to 4.25x for three years, for a waiver fee of 0.25%. The board meets Thursday. Inputs: Examples 2.1, 2.2 and 2.4.

Reasoning to outline: at 4.25x the corporate route still breaches during construction (4.94x), so the offer does not work as stated unless the bank lifts the covenant further or equity is raised; even after COD (4.02x) the company would sit near the new limit, with no capacity for the next project; against this offer the corporate route is cheaper by about 85 bps a year (project finance all-in about 248 bps: 195 margin, 50.4 upfront costs annualized, 2.6 agency; the bank offer about 163 bps: 160 margin plus the 0.25% waiver fee spread over 9.4 years, 2.7 bps) and faster by about 11 months; the ring-fence matters most when the asset has a technology or resource risk the parent cannot afford to carry (gearbox history); the expert recommends project finance for this asset, and keeps the bank relationship by giving the bank a role in the project loan. What would change the answer: a share issue that restores headroom; a wind farm small relative to the group (under one year of EBITDA); a market where project finance terms are unavailable; a rating agency that consolidates the project debt anyway.
