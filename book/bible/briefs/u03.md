## 10.1 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A project company owns almost nothing except promises: a promise to buy power, to build a plant by a date, to supply gas, to repay. The reader cannot assess a single risk in Parts III and IV without knowing what a promise is worth in law, what happens when it is broken, which events excuse it, and which court or tribunal will decide. This chapter gives a reader with no legal training the working legal toolkit of a financier: how contracts create rights and remedies; the six kinds of term that do almost all the work in project contracts (representations, warranties, covenants, conditions, indemnities, and limits on liability); liquidated damages and the rule against penalties; force majeure, frustration, impossibility, and hardship; how common-law and civil-law systems differ in ways that change deal structure; what security and insolvency are for; and how governing law and forum choices decide whether a right can be turned into cash. It teaches the principles and the reading method; the documents themselves are taught in Parts IV and X.

"What you will be able to do" (for the `\section*`):

- Read any clause in a project contract, classify it as a representation, warranty, covenant, condition, indemnity, or limit, and state the remedy that follows a breach (Capability 3).
- Test a liquidated damages clause against the rule against penalties and a liability cap against its carve-outs, and say who bears the uncovered loss (Capabilities 3 and 15).
- Trace an event that stops performance up the excuse ladder, from the force majeure clause to frustration or judicial hardship, under English law and a French-derived civil code (Capabilities 3 and 8).
- Explain to a sponsor why the finance documents are under English or New York law while the security sits under local law, and what that split costs (Capabilities 6 and 8).

- 10.4.2 The penalty rule in English law (`ssec:10.4.2`). ## 10.5 Worked examples

All Illustrative unless marked. Currencies USD unless stated. Each example ends with the reconciliation line shown.

**Example 10.1. Damages for a repudiated pellet supply contract and the duty to mitigate** (`ex:10.1`, Illustrative; Section 10.1.2). A 48 MW biomass plant in the southeastern United States buys wood pellets under a fixed-price supply contract at USD 172.50/t. In month 5 of the contract year the supplier repudiates, with 61,400 t still to deliver that year. On the day of repudiation the spot price for equivalent pellets is USD 191.30/t. Compute expectation damages: 61,400 x (191.30 - 172.50) = USD 1,154,320 (USD 1.15 million). Then the plant's fuel manager waits six weeks, hoping prices fall, and buys at USD 204.10/t. The extra 61,400 x 12.80 = USD 785,920 is not recoverable because it flows from the failure to mitigate. Show also that the plant cannot claim lost electricity revenue if it kept running on replacement pellets (no loss). Reconciliation: 1,154,320 + 785,920 = 1,940,240 = 61,400 x (204.10 - 172.50).

**Example 10.2. Classifying six sentences from an O&M agreement** (`ex:10.2`, Illustrative; Section 10.2.4). Contractual example. The writer drafts six original sentences from an O&M agreement for a 96 MW open-cycle peaking plant: (1) "The Operator has all licenses required to perform the Services at the date of this Agreement"; (2) a promise to keep availability records for seven years; (3) a promise not to subcontract control-room operations without consent; (4) "This Agreement takes effect when the Owner notifies the Operator that the Financing Agreements have become unconditional"; (5) the Operator's indemnity for third-party claims caused by its personnel; (6) a cap of 100% of the annual fee. Apply Framework 10.1 to each: type, trigger, remedy, who pays, whether capped, which law and forum decide. Expected result table: (1) representation and warranty, damages (and misrepresentation remedies if it induced the contract), capped unless fraud; (2) positive (information) covenant, damages and termination only if material; (3) negative covenant, injunction available and damages; (4) condition precedent, no liability for non-satisfaction, long-stop date ends the contract; (5) indemnity, payable on a defined loss without proof of breach of a duty owed to the Owner, subject to the cap and carve-outs; (6) limit, operates on every claim above. No numbers to reconcile; the solution checks that each row names a remedy.

**Example 10.3. Warranty claim versus indemnity after a share purchase** (`ex:10.3`, Illustrative; Section 10.3.1). A fund buys 100% of the project company of a 74 MWac solar plant in Arizona for USD 84.6 million. Eight months after completion a tax authority assesses USD 3.47 million for a pre-completion period. Warranty route: the claim is subject to a de minimis of USD 50,000 per claim and a basket of 1% of the price (USD 0.846 million). With a deductible basket the buyer recovers 3.47 - 0.846 = USD 2.624 million; with a tipping basket it recovers USD 3.47 million; in both cases it must show its loss as a diminution in the value of the shares, which on a price set by discounted cash flow equals the liability one for one in this example (state the simplification). Indemnity route: a specific tax indemnity pays USD 3.47 million pound for pound, with no basket and no proof of diminution. Overall warranty cap of 20% of price (USD 16.92 million) does not bind. Reconciliation: 3.47 - 2.624 = 0.846 = 1% of 84.6. Forward reference: SPAs and warranty and indemnity insurance are taught in Chapter 47.

**Example 10.4. An O&M liability cap and its negligence carve-out** (`ex:10.4`, Illustrative; Section 10.3.2). The 96 MW peaking plant of Example 10.2. Annual O&M fee USD 4.86 million; the operator's aggregate liability is capped at 100% of the annual fee (USD 4.86 million). A control-room technician skips a lube-oil step and damages a gas turbine; the owner's loss is USD 7.92 million (repair plus lost capacity payments). Three wordings of the carve-out: (a) "fraud or wilful misconduct" only: recovery USD 4.86 million, gap USD 3.06 million; (b) "fraud, wilful misconduct or gross negligence": a single skipped step is unlikely to be gross negligence, so the same USD 4.86 million; (c) "fraud, negligence, gross negligence or wilful misconduct": on the Triple Point majority's reading of "negligence" as including negligent breach of a contractual duty of care, recovery is uncapped at USD 7.92 million. Discuss who holds the USD 3.06 million gap (owner's property insurance less its deductible, with waiver of subrogation forward-referenced to `\cref{ch:27}`). Reconciliation: 4.86 + 3.06 = 7.92. State that the Triple Point reading is English law on that contract's words and that drafting can choose either result.

**Example 10.5. Testing three delay LD rates against the penalty rule** (`ex:10.5`, Illustrative; Section 10.4.2). A 120 MW gas-engine IPP in East Africa must reach COD by a required date; if late, the state utility must hire emergency rental diesel. The utility's estimate: it replaces 36 MW of firm capacity at a 78% load factor, so 36 x 24 x 0.78 = 673.92 MWh/day, at a cost premium of USD 118.40 - 71.25 = USD 47.15/MWh over the contracted tariff, which is USD 31,775/day (31,775.33). Test three proposed daily LD rates: USD 26,500 (0.83 times the estimate), USD 41,800 (1.32 times), and USD 157,000 (4.94 times). Reasoning: the first is plainly enforceable and under-compensates; the second protects a legitimate interest (the utility's unquantified political and load-shedding costs) and is very likely enforceable under the Cavendish test; the third imposes a detriment out of all proportion to any interest shown and is at real risk of being struck down in England and moderated by a civil-law court. Writers must phrase outcomes as risk assessments, not predictions, and cite only `t-contract-law` for the legal test. Reconciliation: 26,500/31,775.33 = 0.834; 41,800/31,775.33 = 1.315; 157,000/31,775.33 = 4.941.

**Example 10.6. Who bears 63 days of force majeure** (`ex:10.6`, Illustrative; Section 10.5.1). A 210 MW onshore wind farm under construction in Brazil; a national port strike holds its transformers for 63 days without physical damage. Debt drawn at the time USD 146.3 million at an all-in 7.12% (actual/365); daily interest USD 28,539 (146.3 m x 0.0712 / 365 = 28,538.52), so 63 days of interest is USD 1.798 million; owner's fixed costs USD 6,140/day, so USD 0.387 million (386,820). Contract-by-contract: the EPC contractor gets an extension of time and no LDs, but no prolongation cost; the PPA moves the required COD by 63 days, but no payments are due before COD; there is no physical damage, so delay-in-start-up insurance does not respond (forward reference to `\cref{ch:27}`); the lenders do not waive interest. Result: the project company bears USD 2.185 million (1,797,927 + 386,820 = 2,184,747), funded from contingency or sponsor equity. Lesson: FM gives relief from liability, not money.

**Example 10.7. A gas price shock under three legal regimes** (`ex:10.7`, Illustrative; Section 10.5.4). A gas seller supplies 60,000 MMBtu/day (21.9 million MMBtu/year) at a fixed USD 4.12/MMBtu with seven years left. A regional supply shock moves the seller's replacement cost to USD 11.80/MMBtu; the seller loses 21.9 m x 7.68 = USD 168.2 million a year. Regime 1, English law, no hardship clause: no frustration; the seller performs or pays damages. Regime 2, a French-derived code with a hardship article like article 1195 that the contract has not excluded: the seller may request renegotiation and, failing agreement, ask a court to revise or end the contract. Regime 3, a contractual hardship clause: a 35% deviation for six consecutive months (threshold 4.12 x 1.35 = USD 5.562/MMBtu) triggers renegotiation and then an expert price reset. Then apply Framework 10.2 to place each regime on the ladder. Reconciliation: 11.80 - 4.12 = 7.68; 7.68 x 21.9 = 168.192.

**Example 10.8. Secured recovery in liquidation and in a going-concern sale** (`ex:10.8`, Illustrative; Section 10.8.3). A 160 MW CCGT project company in Southeast Asia defaults. Secured senior debt USD 141.2 million; unsecured trade creditors USD 6.4 million. Break-up (liquidation) value of assets USD 47.3 million, costs USD 3.9 million; going-concern sale value USD 128.6 million, costs USD 5.2 million. Liquidation: secured lenders recover 43.4 (30.7%); unsecured nil. Going-concern sale: secured lenders recover 123.4 (87.4%); unsecured nil because the secured claim is not paid in full. Show in a small untitled table inside the example. Lesson: the value of security is the power to keep the business whole, which is why lenders take shares, contracts, and accounts, not only the plant. Reconciliation: 47.3 - 3.9 = 43.4; 43.4/141.2 = 0.307; 128.6 - 5.2 = 123.4; 123.4/141.2 = 0.874.

## 10.6 Frameworks

- **Framework 10.1, the clause consequence test** (`fw:clause-consequence-test`; original). Purpose: read any clause by what happens when it bites. Steps (enumerate): (1) What kind of term is it: statement of fact, promise about conduct, gate, risk transfer, or limit? (2) What event triggers it, and who must prove the event? (3) What remedy follows: damages, payment of a sum, termination, suspension, injunction, or nothing? (4) Which caps, baskets, exclusions, or time bars reduce that remedy? (5) Which law governs and which forum decides, and can the result be enforced where the counterparty's assets are? One sentence on when to use it: on first read of any contract, and whenever an adverse event is traced through a contract (it is the per-contract step of the "Who pays if...?" trace that Chapter 28 builds, `fw:who-pays-if`).
- **Framework 10.2, the excuse ladder** (`fw:excuse-ladder`; original). Purpose: decide, in order, whether a party that cannot or will not perform is excused. Steps: (1) Does an express relief clause cover the event (FM, relief event, change in law, employer risk event), and were its notice and mitigation conditions met? (2) Does an express price or hardship mechanism adjust the bargain? (3) Does the governing law supply a default excuse: frustration (English), impracticability (New York), codified force majeure or judicial hardship (civil law), and has the contract excluded it? (4) If nothing applies, the party performs or pays damages, subject to caps. Use: whenever a sponsor, lender, or official says "that would be force majeure."
- Established material credited in prose, not boxed: the Cavendish legitimate-interest test (UK Supreme Court, 2015); the UNIDROIT Principles' hardship provisions (if verified in `t-contract-law`).

## 10.10 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: first read of any project contract: parties and capacity; conditions to effectiveness and long-stop; each obligation's standard; reps and repetition; covenants; remedies and LDs (rate, cap, end date, exclusivity); liability caps and carve-outs; consequential loss exclusion and what it excludes; FM definition, notice, effects, termination; hardship and change in law; governing law and forum; assignment to lenders and direct agreement hooks; survival of clauses after termination.
- Red flags: FM defined to include "economic" or "financial" circumstances; notice as an absolute condition with a short period; LDs described as "sole and exclusive remedy" with no termination right once the cap is reached; overall cap that silently includes LDs; carve-out drafted as "negligence" when "gross negligence" was intended (or the reverse); consequential loss exclusion that catches lost revenue and debt service; civil-law contract that does not address the code's hardship article; dispute clause pointing to local courts against a state counterparty; assignment clause without a security carve-out.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "LDs within the overall cap" is common in English-law EPC contracts but not universal; "FM gives time, not money" except where the contract says otherwise for political FM; "English law will not rescue a bad bargain" while civil codes may; caps at 100% of price or annual fee are a starting point (indicative, flagged for the reviewer).
- Common mistakes: treating a condition as a promise; suing on a warranty when an indemnity was available; assuming a pandemic, war, or sanctions is FM without reading the list; missing a notice deadline; forgetting mandatory local law on security; assuming an arbitral award is cash.
- Questions experts ask: What remedy does this give us, and what is it worth after the cap? Who proves what? Where are the counterparty's assets, and will an award be enforced there? If the event happens tomorrow, which clause do we invoke first, and by when?

## 10.11 Judgment drill

- The situation (Illustrative, second person): You are a senior associate on the lenders' counsel team for a 340 MW gas-fired IPP in a French-derived civil-law country (not Kessara). Ten days before the PPA is due to be initialed, the state utility's lawyers propose (i) deleting the clause that excludes the Civil Code's hardship article and (ii) adding a contractual hardship clause allowing either party to request tariff renegotiation if its "economic equilibrium" is disturbed by more than 15% for two consecutive quarters, with an expert reset. The sponsor's counsel is inclined to accept (ii) to get (i) withdrawn. The lead arranger wants your view by Friday.
- Reasoning it through: a hardship reset is a two-way door; the utility, not the project company, is the party most likely to invoke it when retail tariffs are frozen; a 15% threshold on "economic equilibrium" is undefined and therefore litigable; lenders size debt on a tariff that cannot be reopened, so any reset must be bounded (floor at the amount needed for debt service, or limited to the non-capital part of the tariff); if the code's article cannot validly be excluded in that jurisdiction, a well-drafted contractual hardship clause is better than the default rule because the parties set the trigger and the remedy. Expert answer: accept a narrow contractual hardship clause limited to fuel and O&M components, with an objective index-based trigger, no reset of the capital charge, an expert with defined terms of reference, and an express statement that the clause replaces the code's article to the extent the law allows; refuse the 15% "economic equilibrium" wording.
- What would change the answer: a mandatory hardship article that cannot be excluded (makes a defined contractual mechanism more valuable); a sovereign guarantee covering any reset shortfall (reduces lender concern); a merchant or partly indexed tariff (hardship cuts the other way); DFI participation whose policy accepts limited rebalancing.

## 11.1 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every power project's revenue is a quantity times a price, and both are set by physics and market rules before any contract is signed. A reader who does not know what a heat rate is, why a wind farm's net output is 15% below its gross, why a solar plant earns less per megawatt-hour than a baseload plant in the same market, or why prices go negative cannot judge a PPA, a merchant forecast, or an independent engineer's report. This chapter explains at financier depth how thermal, wind, solar, hydro, nuclear, and battery plants convert fuel or resource into energy; how grids carry it; and how wholesale markets turn bids into prices by merit order, location, and scarcity. It ends with capture prices and cannibalization, the market facts that decide renewable revenue. Contracts that tame these risks are Chapters 18 to 20; sector economics and financing terms are Chapters 69 to 74.

"What you will be able to do":

- Convert a plant's capacity, availability, dispatch, heat rate, and fuel price into annual energy and fuel cost per megawatt-hour, and compare technologies on levelized cost (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Explain to a credit committee how a wind or solar estimate gets from gross to net energy and what degrades it over time (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Build a merit-order stack, find the clearing price in a given hour, and show why nodal congestion, curtailment, and negative prices change what a plant earns (Capability 13).
- Compute a capture price and capture ratio from hourly data and explain cannibalization to a sponsor who quotes the baseload average (Capabilities 2 and 13).

## 11.6 Frameworks

- **Framework 11.1, the megawatt-to-revenue chain** (`fw:megawatt-to-revenue`; original). Purpose: trace any power asset's revenue from physics to cash and find where it leaks. Steps: (1) nameplate to net capacity at site conditions; (2) capacity to available capacity (planned and forced outages); (3) available capacity to energy (dispatch, resource, degradation); (4) energy produced to energy delivered and paid for (losses, curtailment, congestion); (5) price at the delivery point (marginal price at the node, capture ratio, negative hours); (6) contract overlay (what is fixed, what floats; Chapters 18 to 20). Use: first read of any power deal, and to locate which party bears each leak.
- Established tools credited in prose: merit order and uniform marginal pricing (standard market design), LCOE (standard engineering-economics measure; cite IEA or NREL methodology only through `t-power-tech-norms`).

## 11.10 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist (power asset first read): technology and fleet track record; net capacity at site conditions; availability basis and outage plan; dispatch or resource basis and its source; degradation; fuel or resource supply; grid connection status and who builds it; market model and price formation at the node; curtailment regime and compensation; capture ratio history and forecast; negative-price exposure.
- Red flags: heat rate quoted on HHV in one document and LHV in another; reference conditions far from site conditions; wind or solar losses added rather than multiplied; capacity factor quoted gross; hub prices used for a plant at a congested node; LCOE presented as a revenue forecast; battery revenue extrapolated from one high-priced year (Case R's 2023 to 2024 battery revenue fall, Part 3.5, shows why); a first-of-a-kind technology with a mature-technology yield haircut.
- Rules of thumb and their limits (indicative, dated, flagged): CCGT efficiency in the mid to high 50s percent LHV for F-class at site conditions; onshore wind net capacity factors from the high 20s to mid 40s percent by site; single-axis trackers adding yield over fixed tilt; battery round-trip efficiency mid 80s percent AC to AC; nuclear capacity factors above 90% in mature fleets. Each with the condition under which it fails.
- Common mistakes: confusing MW and MWh; treating availability as output; ignoring part-load heat rates; applying a system-average price to a solar plant; assuming curtailment is always compensated; treating a negative price as a pricing error.
- Questions experts ask: What is the marginal unit in the hours this plant runs? What is the nodal basis to the hub and how has it moved? Who builds and owns the interconnection, and what if it is late? What does the plant earn in the worst week of the year, not the average one?

## 11.11 Judgment drill

- The situation (Illustrative): You are the associate director on the credit team of a lender asked to provide a seven-year mini-perm to a 250 MWac solar plant in a nodal market. The sponsor's model applies a capture ratio of 0.92 to the hub forward curve and assumes 1.5% curtailment. Your market advisor's draft shows the plant's node has traded at a widening discount to the hub, that two more 300 MW solar projects connect to the same substation within 24 months, and that the area's transmission upgrade is not yet approved. The sponsor wants credit approval in three weeks and says the capture ratio "is in line with the hub's solar fleet."
- Reasoning: the hub's solar fleet average is not this node; the new projects will deepen cannibalization in exactly the plant's hours; uncompensated curtailment and nodal basis are separate losses that multiply; the lender's sizing case should use a node-specific capture ratio with a declining path (as Case R's assumptions do) and a curtailment step-up when the new projects connect; the expert asks for a nodal price study, a transmission-upgrade timeline with its approval status, and a contracted volume (Chapter 20) to cover the first years. Answer: approve only on a downside capture path (for example, starting several points below 0.92 and falling), curtailment rising after the new connections, and sizing on the node, or require a hedge or busbar-settled contract.
- What would change the answer: an approved and funded transmission upgrade with a firm date; co-located storage that shifts output; a PPA settled at the node; a market rule that compensates curtailment.

| curtailment | – | A reduction of a plant's output below what it could produce, ordered by the system operator or chosen because prices are low. | 11.11.3 |
| negative price | – | A market price below zero, arising when inflexible or subsidized generation exceeds demand. | 11.11.4 |
| capture price | – | The output-weighted average price a plant receives over a period. | 11.12 |
| capture ratio | – | Capture price divided by the baseload average price in the same market and period. | 11.12 |
| price cannibalization | – | The fall in a technology's capture price as more plants with the same output profile join the market. | 11.12 |

## 12.1 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapter 11 showed that a power plant's revenue is a physical quantity times a market price. Every other infrastructure asset has its own physics and its own demand driver: a gas field declines, a mine's grade varies block by block, a toll road's traffic ramps up slowly, a port's capacity is set by cranes and berths, a hospital is a building plus a service contract, a desalination plant is mostly an electricity bill, a fiber network earns only when homes sign up, and a data center sells power and cooling as much as space. This chapter gives the financier the working model of each asset: the units it is measured in, how it produces revenue, what the cost drivers are, and the two or three physical facts that decide its credit. Revenue contracts for these assets are Chapter 21; sector economics and financing terms are Chapters 75 to 83.

"What you will be able to do":

- Convert a gas field's reserves and plateau rate into a supply profile, and an LNG or mining chain into the quantity and value it delivers (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Build first-pass revenue for a toll road, a transit line, an airport, a port, a fiber network, and a data center from their physical drivers (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Map any resource or transport asset's physical chain from source to customer and mark the links that will decide bankability (Capabilities 2, 13, and 15).

## 12.4 Opening

Real deal moment from fact sheet `oyu-tolgoi`. **Example 12.1. A gas field's plateau, decline, and the plant it can feed** (`ex:12.1`; 12.1.2). Offshore gas field, 2P reserves 712.8 bcf (20.18 bcm at 0.0283168 bcm per bcf). Plateau 118.0 MMscfd for 9 years: 43.07 bcf a year, 387.6 bcf cumulative. Then exponential decline at 11.5% a year to an economic limit of 22.0 MMscfd (8.03 bcf a year): duration ln(43.07/8.03)/0.115 = 14.6 years; cumulative decline production (43.07/0.115) x (1 - e^{-0.115 x 14.6}) = 304.7 bcf. Total recovered 692.3 bcf, 97.1% of 2P; the rest lies below the economic limit. Energy: gas at 1,042 Btu/scf, so the plateau is 122,956 MMBtu/day. A 410 MW CCGT at 6,420 kJ/kWh LHV and 68% capacity factor needs 410 x 24 x 0.68 x 6,420 x 1,000 x 1.108 / 1,055,056 = 45,113 MMBtu/day, or 43.3 MMscfd: the field's plateau could feed about 2.7 such plants. Reconciliation: 387.6 + 304.7 = 692.3; 692.3/712.8 = 0.971. Note that a simple "years of reserves at plateau" figure (712.8 x 1,000 / 118 / 365 = 16.5 years) overstates plateau life; the writer shows that mistake.

## 12.6 Frameworks

- **Framework 12.1, the physical chain map** (`fw:physical-chain-map`; original). Purpose: lay out every physical link between the resource or demand source and the paying customer, so that the financier can see which link has no owner, no spare capacity, or no contract. Steps: (1) list the links in order (for gas-to-power: reservoir, wells and platform, processing, pipeline, metering, plant, substation, grid, offtaker); (2) for each link, record owner, capacity, current status (existing, under construction, planned), and expected life; (3) mark the binding capacity; (4) mark single points of failure; (5) name the contract that will govern each interface (detail in Chapters 21, 25, and 28); (6) mark links outside the project company's control and the remedy if each fails. Use: at origination and in diligence for any chain asset (gas, LNG, mining with rail and port, power with transmission).

## 12.10 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist (first look at a non-power asset): unit of output and its measurement; physical capacity and the binding constraint; demand or resource evidence and who certified it; ramp-up period; the free or competing alternative; construction physics (ground, marine, structures); operating cost drivers and lifecycle lumps; external links (grid, pipeline, port, rail) and who controls them; end-of-life obligations.
- Red flags: reserves reported with Inferred material inside; a mine plan whose cut-off grade uses a price above the current price; gas supply quoted in MMscfd without a heating value; traffic forecast without a ramp-up or with a free alternative ignored; port utilization above 80% with no expansion plan; fiber take-up assumed at the level of a mature competitor; data center with an unsigned grid connection; desalination tariff without an energy pass-through.
- Rules of thumb and their limits (indicative and flagged): greenfield toll roads ramp up over three to five years; RO desalination uses about 3 to 4 kWh/m3 for seawater; new hyperscale data centers target PUE well below 1.5; berth utilization above about 75% to 80% causes queues. Each with the case that breaks it.
- Common mistakes: confusing bcf and bcm, or MMscfd and MMBtu/day; treating years of reserves at plateau as field life; adding resources to reserves; using AADT on the busiest section for the whole road; treating a hospital's lifecycle cost as a flat annual amount; assuming a data center's power is a tenant problem.
- Questions experts ask: What is the binding constraint, and when does it bind? Who certified the resource or the forecast, under which code, and at what price? What does the free alternative cost the user? Which link in the chain is outside our control, and what happens on the day it fails?

## 12.11 Judgment drill

- The situation (Illustrative): You are the mandated lead arranger's head of mining finance for a USD 1.1 billion copper project in a stable mining jurisdiction. The sponsor's model schedules production from Proven and Probable reserves for 14 years and then from Inferred resources for another 9 years, and asks for an 11-year loan with a balloon refinanced against the later years. The sponsor argues that Inferred tonnes in this deposit "always convert" and cites its own experience at a neighboring mine. Credit committee is in ten days.
- Reasoning: Inferred resources have the lowest geological confidence and are not reserves; lenders size on reserves only and want a reserve tail (the mine life after final maturity), commonly about a third of reserves remaining at maturity (indicative; flag and verify in `t-market-norms`); a balloon refinanced against Inferred material is a bet on conversion drilling; the expert response is to size the 11-year loan on the reserve case with a full amortization profile and a reserve tail test, and to offer an accordion or later refinancing if drilling converts resources to reserves.
- What would change the answer: an infill drilling program completed before close that converts a defined share to Indicated and then Probable; a completion guarantee that extends beyond construction to a reserve test; a lower copper price deck that shrinks reserves; an offtaker prepayment that shifts risk.
