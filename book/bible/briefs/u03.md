## 10.1 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. A project company owns almost nothing except promises: a promise to buy power, to build a plant by a date, to supply gas, to repay. The reader cannot assess a single risk in Parts III and IV without knowing what a promise is worth in law, what happens when it is broken, which events excuse it, and which court or tribunal will decide. This chapter gives a reader with no legal training the working legal toolkit of a financier: how contracts create rights and remedies; the six kinds of term that do almost all the work in project contracts (representations, warranties, covenants, conditions, indemnities, and limits on liability); liquidated damages and the rule against penalties; force majeure, frustration, impossibility, and hardship; how common-law and civil-law systems differ in ways that change deal structure; what security and insolvency are for; and how governing law and forum choices decide whether a right can be turned into cash. It teaches the principles and the reading method; the documents themselves are taught in Parts IV and X.

"What you will be able to do" (for the `\section*`):

- Read any clause in a project contract, classify it as a representation, warranty, covenant, condition, indemnity, or limit, and state the remedy that follows a breach (Capability 3).
- Test a liquidated damages clause against the rule against penalties and a liability cap against its carve-outs, and say who bears the uncovered loss (Capabilities 3 and 15).
- Trace an event that stops performance up the excuse ladder, from the force majeure clause to frustration or judicial hardship, under English law and a French-derived civil code (Capabilities 3 and 8).
- Explain to a sponsor why the finance documents are under English or New York law while the security sits under local law, and what that split costs (Capabilities 6 and 8).

A representation induces the contract (remedy: rescission and damages for misrepresentation); a warranty is a contractual promise that a fact is true (remedy: damages). Conditions to effectiveness of a PPA (financing in place, permits) and the long-stop date. A condition subsequent in the general-law sense ends or modifies a right already in effect; one sentence noting that finance documents use the same words for something different (a condition lenders allow to be met after financial close by a set date), with a forward reference to `\cref{ssec:51.2.4}`. Waiver of a condition by the party it protects.

**10.3 Indemnities, liability caps, and exclusions** (`sec:10.3`)

- 10.3.1 Indemnities and how they differ from damages (`ssec:10.3.1`). - 10.3.2 Liability caps, sub-caps, and carve-outs (`ssec:10.3.2`). The cap stack (per-head sub-caps under an aggregate cap under an overall cap); why caps are expressed as a percentage of contract price or annual fee; the standard carve-outs (fraud, wilful misconduct, gross negligence, sometimes death and personal injury, sometimes negligence) and why each exists. Triple Point v PTT on whether LDs count toward the cap and on the "negligence" carve-out (fact sheet `triple-point`: unanimous that LDs counted toward the overall cap on that contract; 3-2 that "negligence" covered negligent breach of the contractual duty of care). Example 10.4. Clause 10.2.
- 10.3.3 Excluding indirect and consequential loss (`ssec:10.3.3`). What "consequential loss" means in contract drafting as distinct from ordinary usage, taught as the general rule (losses outside the ordinary consequences of a breach) without case names, because `t-contract-law` does not verify the English authorities on its meaning; why project contracts list lost revenue and debt service expressly as recoverable or excluded. The lender's question: if the O&M operator's error stops the plant, does the exclusion leave debt service unprotected?
- 10.3.4 Caps and carve-outs from each party's side (`ssec:10.3.4`). The four lenses in prose: the contractor and operator want low caps priced into a low fee; the sponsor wants the fee low and accepts the gap; the lenders want the gap covered by insurance, reserves, or sponsor support; the government as offtaker wants uncapped liability for fraud and corruption. **10.4 Liquidated damages and the rule against penalties** (`sec:10.4`)

- 10.4.1 Agreeing damages in advance (`ssec:10.4.1`). Certainty for both sides, no need to prove loss, a known number for lenders to size against, and the trade: LDs usually become the exclusive remedy for the failure they cover. Clause 10.3 (delay LDs in a PPA, payable by the project company to the offtaker) with annotations.
- 10.4.2 The penalty rule in English law (`ssec:10.4.2`). From "genuine pre-estimate of loss" to the Cavendish test (fact sheet `t-contract-law`, facts 1 to 8): the rule applies only to secondary obligations triggered by breach; the question is whether the provision imposes a detriment out of all proportion to any legitimate interest of the innocent party in performance; the strong initial presumption for negotiated contracts between properly advised parties of comparable bargaining power (para 35, which may be quoted). Obey the sheet's "Do not state": Cavendish did not abolish the genuine pre-estimate test outright (Lord Dunedin's tests usually suffice for a straightforward damages clause); the rule does not reach primary obligations or clauses not triggered by breach. Example 10.5.
- 10.4.3 Penalty clauses in civil law (`ssec:10.4.3`). The clause pénale is valid in principle and a judge may, even of their own motion, moderate or increase an agreed penalty that is manifestly excessive or derisory (French Civil Code article 1231-5, `t-contract-law` fact 27); the practical consequence that a civil-law LD can be cut, and also raised, by a court. The UNIDROIT Principles' power to reduce a grossly excessive agreed payment (article 7.4.13, fact 28). New York law at outline level, stated as a principle without case names or a quoted test, because no fact sheet verifies it (see Open requests).
- 10.4.4 LDs, caps, and termination (`ssec:10.4.4`). Triple Point v PTT: LDs accrue from the due date to termination and survive it; losses after termination are general damages; LDs counted toward the overall cap on that contract's wording (fact sheet `triple-point`, including its "Do not state" list: it was a software implementation contract, not EPC; do not state the net sum PTT recovered; the dissent was on the negligence issue). The financier's lesson: check the LD end date, whether LDs sit inside the overall cap, and what remedy remains if LDs hit their cap.

**10.5 Force majeure, frustration, impossibility, and hardship** (`sec:10.5`)

- 10.5.1 What a force majeure clause does (`ssec:10.5.1`). Definition by general words plus a list; the causation and "beyond reasonable control" tests; effects: suspension, relief from liability and LDs, extension of time, and no money unless the contract says so; notice periods as conditions of relief; mitigation; prolonged FM and termination with or without compensation. Natural versus political FM as the split used in emerging-market PPAs (home of both terms, R-031), with forward references to `\cref{ssec:17.3.3}` for compensation and to `\cref{ch:18}` for deemed payments. COVID-19 in one paragraph from `covid-transport`. Example 10.6.
- 10.5.2 Frustration in English law and impracticability in New York law (`ssec:10.5.2`). Frustration as the narrow default rule when no clause applies: performance "radically different" from what was undertaken (Lord Radcliffe in Davis Contractors v Fareham UDC [1956] AC 696, quotable from `t-contract-law` fact 16); Taylor v Caldwell (1863) as the origin; a price rise or a more expensive route does not frustrate (Davis; Tsakiroglou v Noblee Thorl [1962] AC 93 stated by principle and citation only, per the sheet's "Do not state"); Canary Wharf v EMA (2019) as a modern refusal. New York's impossibility and impracticability doctrines in one paragraph of principle, with no case names (see Open requests).
- 10.5.3 Force majeure and impossibility in civil codes (`ssec:10.5.3`). The codified definition in French law since October 1, 2016, article 1218 (`t-contract-law` fact 26: an event beyond the debtor's control, not reasonably foreseeable at contracting, whose effects cannot be avoided by appropriate measures, which prevents performance), temporary versus permanent impediment, and the parties' freedom to define FM differently in the contract.
- 10.5.4 Hardship and imprévision (`ssec:10.5.4`). Hardship is about performance becoming excessively onerous, not impossible. French Civil Code article 1195 (`t-contract-law` facts 22 to 24: renegotiation, then termination by agreement, a joint request to the judge, or, failing agreement within a reasonable time, judicial revision or termination at one party's request; quote only the first paragraph verbatim; do not state as settled that article 1195 is always excludable); the administrative-law origin in Gaz de Bordeaux (1916); the UNIDROIT Principles' hardship articles 6.2.1 to 6.2.3 (fact 28); contractual hardship or "economic equilibrium" clauses (the economic equilibrium concept itself is homed at `\cref{ssec:17.1.3}`; here only its contractual hardship use). Kessara's Civil Code as the running-case instance: article 1135-1 (inserted by Law No. 2011-038) lets a court adapt or end a contract after an unforeseeable change of circumstances that makes performance excessively onerous for a party that had not accepted the risk; in Kessara it is supplementary law, so parties may exclude it (Annex P 1.1.4; fictional law, stated as such). Example 10.7.
- 10.5.5 The excuse ladder (`ssec:10.5.5`). Framework 10.2, the excuse ladder (fw:excuse-ladder), presented and applied to Example 10.7 and to the Mundra facts in Section 10.6.

**10.6 Mundra and the limits of force majeure** (`sec:10.6`). Real case, fact sheet `mundra`. Two imported-coal plants (Tata Power's subsidiary CGPL's 4,000 MW Mundra UMPP; Adani Power's 4,620 MW Mundra plant) bid tariffs with partly indexed or unindexed fuel charges; Indonesia's 2010 to 2011 regulation required coal exports to be priced at or above government benchmark prices, even under existing contracts; CERC, then APTEL, then the Supreme Court of India on April 11, 2017 (Energy Watchdog v CERC): a rise in fuel price is commercial hardship, not force majeure, especially when the PPA excludes fuel-cost changes, and a change in Indonesian law is not a "change in law" under PPAs that cover only Indian law. Transferable lesson for this chapter: the excuse a party relies on must exist in the words or in a narrow default doctrine; commercial hardship is allocated by the contract's price mechanism, not by FM. Leave the tariff-design lesson to Chapter 18 and the fuel-contract lesson to Chapter 25 (one forward-reference sentence each). Obey the sheet's "Do not state" list (no claim that the Supreme Court granted relief for the Indonesian price rise; no claim that CGPL defaulted; the Indonesian rule "required benchmark pricing", not a ban or tax; capacity 4,000 MW, not 4,150 MW; no loss totals).

**10.7 Common law and civil law** (`sec:10.7`)

- 10.7.1 Where the law comes from (`ssec:10.7.1`). Statute, precedent, and the role of the judge in England, New York, and France-derived systems; codes as the starting point; why commercial parties worldwide choose English or New York law for finance documents (predictability, a deep body of commercial precedent, specialist courts) without asserting market shares.
- 10.7.2 Interpretation, good faith, and implied terms (`ssec:10.7.2`). English courts read words in their commercial context and imply terms only when necessary; civil codes impose good faith in negotiation and performance (stated as the general civil-law principle without an article number, because no fact sheet verifies French article 1104; see Open requests); New York implies a covenant of good faith and fair dealing (principle only). What this means for drafting: in civil-law contracts, the code fills gaps whether you like it or not.
- 10.7.3 Differences that change a project financing (`ssec:10.7.3`). The trust and the floating charge exist in English law and not in most civil codes, so a civil-law security package needs a parallel debt and a security agent (forward reference to `\cref{ch:52}`; `t-security-civil-law` may supply one verified sentence); mandatory local law for land, security, insolvency, and regulation; judicial hardship; penalty moderation. Exhibit 10.2 (comparison table) sits here.

**10.8 Security and insolvency in principle** (`sec:10.8`)

- 10.8.1 What security is (`ssec:10.8.1`). A security interest gives a creditor a right over an asset ahead of other creditors if the debtor fails to pay; mortgage, pledge, charge, and assignment by way of security described by what they attach to (land, movables, shares, receivables and bank accounts, contract rights); priority and registration in principle (perfection is Chapter 52).
- 10.8.2 What insolvency does (`ssec:10.8.2`). - 10.8.3 Security over everything as a means of control (`ssec:10.8.3`). Break-up value of a half-built or single-purpose plant is low; the value is in the operating whole, so security is for control: the right to step in, to sell the project company's shares, to keep contracts alive. **Example 10.1. Damages for a repudiated pellet supply contract and the duty to mitigate** (`ex:10.1`, Illustrative; Section 10.1.2). Satilla Bioenergy LLC owns a 48 MW biomass plant near Waycross, Georgia, United States. It buys wood pellets from Altamaha Pellet Company under a fixed-price supply contract for calendar 2024 at USD 172.50/t. On May 14, 2024 the supplier repudiates, with 61,400 t still to deliver that year. That day the spot price for equivalent pellets is USD 191.30/t. Expectation damages: 61,400 x (191.30 - 172.50) = USD 1,154,320 (USD 1.15 million). Satilla's fuel manager then waits six weeks, hoping prices fall, and buys on June 25, 2024 at USD 204.10/t. **Example 10.2. Classifying six sentences from an O&M agreement** (`ex:10.2`, Illustrative; Section 10.2.4). Contractual example. Fenwick Marsh Power Ltd owns the Fenwick Marsh peaking plant, a 96 MW open-cycle gas turbine plant in Lincolnshire, England, that holds a GB Capacity Market agreement. Its operator is Calder Turbine Services Ltd under an English-law O&M agreement dated April 3, 2023. **Example 10.3. Warranty claim versus indemnity after a share purchase** (`ex:10.3`, Illustrative; Section 10.3.1). On September 30, 2024 Redmesa Infrastructure Fund II buys 100% of Saguaro Flats Solar LLC, owner of a 74 MWac solar plant in Pinal County, Arizona, from Copperline Renewables for USD 84.6 million. In May 2025 a state tax assessment of USD 3.47 million arrives for a pre-completion period. Warranty route: the claim is subject to a de minimis of USD 50,000 per claim and a basket of 1% of the price (USD 0.846 million). With a deductible basket the buyer recovers 3.47 - 0.846 = USD 2.624 million; with a tipping basket it recovers USD 3.47 million; in both cases it must show its loss as a diminution in the value of the shares, which on a price set by discounted cash flow equals the liability one for one in this example (state the simplification). Indemnity route: a specific tax indemnity pays USD 3.47 million dollar for dollar, with no basket and no proof of diminution. Overall warranty cap of 20% of price (USD 16.92 million) does not bind. Reconciliation: 3.47 - 2.624 = 0.846 = 1% of 84.6. Forward reference: share purchase agreements and warranty and indemnity insurance are taught in `\cref{ssec:47.3.3}` and `\cref{ssec:47.3.5}` (R-029).

**Example 10.4. An O&M liability cap and its negligence carve-out** (`ex:10.4`, Illustrative; Section 10.3.2). The Fenwick Marsh plant of Example 10.2. Annual O&M fee GBP 4.86 million; Calder's aggregate liability is capped at 100% of the annual fee (GBP 4.86 million). In January 2025 a control-room technician skips a lube-oil step and damages a gas turbine; the owner's loss is GBP 7.92 million (repair plus Capacity Market payments lost while the unit is out). Three wordings of the carve-out: (a) "fraud or wilful misconduct" only: recovery GBP 4.86 million, gap GBP 3.06 million; (b) "fraud, wilful misconduct or gross negligence": a single skipped step is unlikely to be gross negligence, so the same GBP 4.86 million; (c) "fraud, negligence, gross negligence or wilful misconduct": on the Triple Point majority's reading of "negligence" as including negligent breach of a contractual duty of care, recovery is uncapped at GBP 7.92 million. Discuss who holds the GBP 3.06 million gap (owner's property insurance less its deductible; the insurer that pays would be subrogated to the owner's claim against Calder unless the policy waives subrogation, forward reference to `\cref{ssec:27.6.1}`). Reconciliation: 4.86 + 3.06 = 7.92. State that the Triple Point reading is English law on that contract's words and that drafting can choose either result.

**Example 10.5. Testing three delay LD rates against the penalty rule** (`ex:10.5`, Illustrative; Section 10.4.2). Térmica Río Seco S.A. is negotiating, in October 2026, the PPA for a 120 MW gas-engine IPP in Corredana (Chapter 1's fictional dollarized republic) with ELNACOR, the state single buyer; the lenders have asked for English law and the Ministry of Energy and Mines wants Corredanan law. Required COD is June 30, 2029; if the plant is late, ELNACOR must hire emergency rental diesel. ELNACOR's estimate: it replaces 36 MW of firm capacity at a 78% load factor, so 36 x 24 x 0.78 = 673.92 MWh/day, at a cost premium of USD 118.40 - 71.25 = USD 47.15/MWh over the contracted tariff, which is USD 31,775/day (31,775.33). **Example 10.6. Who bears 63 days of force majeure** (`ex:10.6`, Illustrative; Section 10.5.1). Complexo Eólico Chapada Alta, a 210 MW onshore wind farm under construction in Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil, owned by Chapada Alta Energia S.A. **Example 10.7. A gas price shock under three legal regimes** (`ex:10.7`, Illustrative; Section 10.5.4). In March 2019 Sorraia Gás Comercialização S.A. agreed to supply Ribafria Energia S.A., owner of a combined-cycle plant in Portugal, with 60,000 MMBtu/day (21.9 million MMBtu/year) at a fixed USD 6.37/MMBtu until 2029. In 2022, with seven years left, a European supply shock lifts the seller's replacement cost to an average of USD 31.84/MMBtu for six months (an illustrative level, not market data); the seller loses 21.9 m x 25.47 = USD 557.8 million a year. The example treats three alternative governing-law choices for the same contract. Regime 1, English law, no hardship clause: no frustration (Davis; a more expensive supply is not a radically different performance); the seller performs or pays damages. Then apply Framework 10.2 to place each regime on the ladder. Reconciliation: 31.84 - 6.37 = 25.47; 25.47 x 21.9 = 557.79; 6.37 x 1.35 = 8.5995.

## 10.6 Frameworks

- **Framework 10.1, the clause consequence test** (`fw:clause-consequence-test`; original). Purpose: read any clause by what happens when it bites. Steps (enumerate): (1) What kind of term is it: statement of fact, promise about conduct, gate, risk transfer, or limit? (2) What event triggers it, and who must prove the event? (3) What remedy follows: damages, payment of a sum, termination, suspension, injunction, or nothing? (4) Which caps, baskets, exclusions, or time bars reduce that remedy? (5) Which law governs and which forum decides, and can the result be enforced where the counterparty's assets are? One sentence on when to use it: on first read of any contract, and whenever an adverse event is traced through a contract (it is the per-contract step of the "Who pays if...?" trace that Chapter 28 builds, `fw:who-pays-if`).
- **Framework 10.2, the excuse ladder** (`fw:excuse-ladder`; original). Purpose: decide, in order, whether a party that cannot or will not perform is excused. Steps: (1) Does an express relief clause cover the event (FM, relief event, change in law, employer risk event; the clauses themselves are Chapter 17's), and were its notice and mitigation conditions met? (2) Does an express price or hardship mechanism adjust the bargain? (3) Does the governing law supply a default excuse: frustration (English), impracticability (New York), codified force majeure or judicial hardship (civil law), and has the contract excluded it? (4) If nothing applies, the party performs or pays damages, subject to caps. Use: whenever a sponsor, lender, or official says "that would be force majeure."
- Established material credited in prose, not boxed: the Cavendish legitimate-interest test (UK Supreme Court, 2015); the UNIDROIT Principles' hardship provisions (articles 6.2.1 to 6.2.3, 2016 edition).

## 10.10 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist: first read of any project contract: parties and capacity; conditions to effectiveness and long-stop; each obligation's standard; reps and repetition; covenants; remedies and LDs (rate, cap, end date, exclusivity); liability caps and carve-outs; consequential loss exclusion and what it excludes; waivers of subrogation; FM definition, notice, effects, termination; hardship and change in law; governing law and forum; assignment to lenders and direct agreement hooks; survival of clauses after termination.
- Red flags: FM defined to include "economic" or "financial" circumstances; notice as an absolute condition with a short period; LDs described as "sole and exclusive remedy" with no termination right once the cap is reached; overall cap that silently includes LDs; carve-out drafted as "negligence" when "gross negligence" was intended (or the reverse); consequential loss exclusion that catches lost revenue and debt service; civil-law contract that does not address the code's hardship article; dispute clause pointing to local courts against a state counterparty; assignment clause without a security carve-out.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: "LDs within the overall cap" is common in English-law EPC contracts but not universal; "FM gives time, not money" except where the contract says otherwise for political FM; "English law will not rescue a bad bargain" while civil codes may; caps at 100% of price or annual fee are a starting point (indicative under D-011, flagged for the reviewer).
- Common mistakes: treating a condition as a promise; suing on a warranty when an indemnity was available; assuming a pandemic, war, or sanctions is FM without reading the list; missing a notice deadline; forgetting mandatory local law on security; assuming an arbitral award is cash.
- Questions experts ask: What remedy does this give us, and what is it worth after the cap? Who proves what? Where are the counterparty's assets, and will an award be enforced there? If the event happens tomorrow, which clause do we invoke first, and by when?

## 10.11 Judgment drill

- The situation (Illustrative, second person): It is March 2026. You are a senior associate on the lenders' counsel team for Central Térmica Valle Hondo S.A., a 340 MW gas-fired IPP in Corredana (Chapter 1's fictional republic; its Civil Code, for this drill, contains a hardship article modeled on French article 1195). Ten days before the PPA is due to be initialed, ELNACOR's lawyers propose (i) deleting the clause that excludes the Civil Code's hardship article and (ii) adding a contractual hardship clause allowing either party to request tariff renegotiation if its "economic equilibrium" is disturbed by more than 15% for two consecutive quarters, with an expert reset. The sponsor's counsel is inclined to accept (ii) to get (i) withdrawn. The lead arranger wants your view by Friday, March 20.
- Reasoning it through: a hardship reset is a two-way door; the utility, not the project company, is the party most likely to invoke it when retail tariffs are frozen; a 15% threshold on "economic equilibrium" is undefined and therefore litigable; lenders size debt on a tariff that cannot be reopened, so any reset must be bounded (floor at the amount needed for debt service, or limited to the non-capital part of the tariff); if the code's article cannot validly be excluded in that jurisdiction, a well-drafted contractual hardship clause is better than the default rule because the parties set the trigger and the remedy. Expert answer: accept a narrow contractual hardship clause limited to fuel and O&M components, with an objective index-based trigger, no reset of the capital charge, an expert with defined terms of reference, and an express statement that the clause replaces the code's article to the extent the law allows; refuse the 15% "economic equilibrium" wording.
- What would change the answer: a mandatory hardship article that cannot be excluded (makes a defined contractual mechanism more valuable); a sovereign guarantee covering any reset shortfall (reduces lender concern); a merchant or partly indexed tariff (hardship cuts the other way); DFI participation whose policy accepts limited rebalancing.

## 11.1 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Every power project's revenue is a quantity times a price, and both are set by physics and market rules before any contract is signed. A reader who does not know what a heat rate is, why a wind farm's net output is 15% below its gross, why a solar plant earns less per megawatt-hour than a baseload plant in the same market, or why prices go negative cannot judge a PPA, a merchant forecast, or an independent engineer's report. This chapter explains at financier depth how thermal, wind, solar, hydro, nuclear, and battery plants convert fuel or resource into energy; how grids carry it; and how wholesale markets turn bids into prices by merit order, location, and scarcity. It ends with capture prices and cannibalization, the market facts that decide renewable revenue. Contracts that tame these risks are Chapters 18 to 20; sector economics and financing terms are Chapters 69 to 74.

"What you will be able to do":

- Convert a plant's capacity, availability, dispatch, heat rate, and fuel price into annual energy and fuel cost per megawatt-hour, and compare technologies on levelized cost (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Explain to a credit committee how a wind or solar estimate gets from gross to net energy and what degrades it over time (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Build a merit-order stack, find the clearing price in a given hour, and show why nodal congestion, curtailment, and negative prices change what a plant earns (Capability 13).
- Compute a capture price and capture ratio from hourly data and explain cannibalization to a sponsor who quotes the baseload average (Capabilities 2 and 13).

## 11.4 Opening

Real deal moment from fact sheet `chile-solar`: in 2015, at some nodes in Chile's northern grid, the spot price of electricity sat at zero on 192 days (attributed to Bloomberg reporting of grid-operator data). Developers had built in some of the world's best solar resource, more than 1,000 km from Santiago's demand. Seven years later María Elena Solar was insolvent: the plant was paid almost nothing where it injected power and had to buy power at high marginal costs where its customers withdrew it. Two or three paragraphs, ending on the question the chapter answers: how can a plant that works perfectly earn nothing?

## 11.6 Frameworks

- **Framework 11.1, the megawatt-to-revenue chain** (`fw:megawatt-to-revenue`; original). Purpose: trace any power asset's revenue from physics to cash and find where it leaks. Steps: (1) nameplate to net capacity at site conditions; (2) capacity to available capacity (planned and forced outages); (3) available capacity to energy (dispatch, resource, degradation); (4) energy produced to energy delivered and paid for (losses, curtailment, congestion); (5) price at the delivery point (marginal price at the node, capture ratio, negative hours); (6) contract overlay (what is fixed, what floats; Chapters 18 to 20). Use: first read of any power deal, and to locate which party bears each leak.
- Established tools credited in prose: merit order and uniform marginal pricing (standard market design), LCOE (standard engineering-economics measure; cite Lazard or IEA/NEA conventions only through `t-power-tech-norms`).

## 11.10 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist (power asset first read): technology and fleet track record; net capacity at site conditions; availability basis and outage plan; dispatch or resource basis and its source; degradation; fuel or resource supply; grid connection status and who builds it; market model and price formation at the node; curtailment regime and compensation; capture ratio history and forecast; negative-price exposure.
- Red flags: heat rate quoted on HHV in one document and LHV in another; reference conditions far from site conditions; wind or solar losses added rather than multiplied; capacity factor quoted gross; hub prices used for a plant at a congested node; LCOE presented as a revenue forecast; battery revenue extrapolated from one high-priced year (Case R's battery revenue path, Part 3.5, falls from USD 142.5/kW-year in 2023 to 61.8 in 2024, which shows why); a first-of-a-kind technology with a mature-technology yield haircut.
- Rules of thumb and their limits, each tied to `t-power-tech-norms` with source and year, never to an undated "industry standard": new-build CCGT heat rates far below fleet averages (Lazard 2026 against EIA 2024); US combined-cycle capacity factor 58.4% in 2025 against Lazard's 30% to 90% new-build range; nuclear capacity factors above 90% in the US fleet and 83% globally in 2024; battery round-trip efficiency in the low to high 80s percent. Each with the condition under which it fails. Single-axis tracker yield gains and wind capacity-factor ranges by site are stated qualitatively (no verified figure).
- Common mistakes: confusing MW and MWh; treating availability as output; ignoring part-load heat rates; applying a system-average price to a solar plant; assuming curtailment is always compensated; treating a negative price as a pricing error.
- Questions experts ask: What is the marginal unit in the hours this plant runs? What is the nodal basis to the hub and how has it moved? Who builds and owns the interconnection, and what if it is late? What does the plant earn in the worst week of the year, not the average one?

## 11.11 Judgment drill

- The situation (Illustrative): It is September 2026. You are the associate director on the credit team of a lender asked to provide a seven-year mini-perm to Sangamon Sun LLC's 250 MWac solar plant in central Illinois, in the MISO nodal market. The sponsor's model applies a capture ratio of 0.92 to the hub forward curve and assumes 1.5% curtailment. Your market advisor's draft shows the plant's node has traded at a widening discount to the hub, that two more 300 MW solar projects connect to the same substation within 24 months, and that the area's transmission upgrade is not yet approved. The sponsor wants credit approval by October 9 and says the capture ratio "is in line with the hub's solar fleet."
- Reasoning: the hub's solar fleet average is not this node; the new projects will deepen cannibalization in exactly the plant's hours; uncompensated curtailment and nodal basis are separate losses that multiply; the lender's sizing case should use a node-specific capture ratio with a declining path (as Case R's assumptions do) and a curtailment step-up when the new projects connect; the expert asks for a nodal price study, a transmission-upgrade timeline with its approval status, and a contracted volume (Chapter 20) to cover the first years. Answer: approve only on a downside capture path (for example, starting several points below 0.92 and falling), curtailment rising after the new connections, and sizing on the node, or require a hedge or busbar-settled contract.
- What would change the answer: an approved and funded transmission upgrade with a firm date; co-located storage that shifts output; a PPA settled at the node; a market rule that compensates curtailment.

## 12.1 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. Chapter 11 showed that a power plant's revenue is a physical quantity times a market price. Every other infrastructure asset has its own physics and its own demand driver: a gas field declines, a mine's grade varies block by block, a toll road's traffic ramps up slowly, a port's capacity is set by cranes and berths, a hospital is a building plus a service contract, a desalination plant is mostly an electricity bill and a capital charge, a fiber network earns only when homes sign up, and a data center sells power and cooling as much as space. This chapter gives the financier the working model of each asset: the units it is measured in, how it produces revenue, what the cost drivers are, and the two or three physical facts that decide its credit. Revenue contracts for these assets are Chapter 21; sector economics and financing terms are Chapters 75 to 83.

"What you will be able to do":

- Convert a gas field's reserves and plateau rate into a supply profile, and an LNG or mining chain into the quantity and value it delivers (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Build first-pass revenue for a toll road, a transit line, an airport, a port, a fiber network, and a data center from their physical drivers (Capabilities 2 and 13).
- Map any resource or transport asset's physical chain from source to customer and mark the links that will decide bankability (Capabilities 2, 13, and 15).

**Example 12.1. A gas field's plateau, decline, and the plant it can feed** (`ex:12.1`; 12.1.2). Corbeau Deep, an offshore gas field off Trinidad operated by Leeward Petroleum Ltd, sanctioned in 2024; 2P reserves 712.8 bcf (20.18 bcm at 0.0283168 bcm per bcf). Plateau 118.0 MMscfd for 9 years: 43.07 bcf a year, 387.6 bcf cumulative. Then exponential decline at 11.5% a year to an economic limit of 22.0 MMscfd (8.03 bcf a year): duration ln(43.07/8.03)/0.115 = 14.6 years; cumulative decline production (43.07/0.115) x (1 - e^{-0.115 x 14.6}) = 304.7 bcf. Total recovered 692.3 bcf, 97.1% of 2P; the rest lies below the economic limit. Energy: gas at 1,042 Btu/scf, so the plateau is 122,956 MMBtu/day. ## 12.6 Frameworks

- **Framework 12.1, the physical chain map** (`fw:physical-chain-map`; original). Purpose: lay out every physical link between the resource or demand source and the paying customer, so that the financier can see which link has no owner, no spare capacity, or no contract. Steps: (1) list the links in order (for gas-to-power: reservoir, wells and platform, processing, pipeline, metering, plant, substation, grid, offtaker); (2) for each link, record owner, capacity, current status (existing, under construction, planned), and expected life; (3) mark the binding capacity; (4) mark single points of failure; (5) name the contract that will govern each interface (detail in Chapters 21, 25, and 28); (6) mark links outside the project company's control and the remedy if each fails. Use: at origination and in diligence for any chain asset (gas, LNG, mining with rail and port, power with transmission).

## 12.10 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist (first look at a non-power asset): unit of output and its measurement; physical capacity and the binding constraint; demand or resource evidence and who certified it, under which code; ramp-up period; the free or competing alternative; construction physics (ground, marine, structures); operating cost drivers and lifecycle lumps; external links (grid, pipeline, port, rail) and who controls them; end-of-life obligations.
- Red flags: reserves reported with Inferred material inside; a mine plan whose cut-off grade uses a price above the current price; gas supply quoted in MMscfd without a heating value; traffic forecast without a ramp-up or with a free alternative ignored; a port close to capacity with no expansion plan; fiber take-up assumed at the level of a mature competitor; data center with an unsigned grid connection; desalination tariff without an energy pass-through.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: traffic at financial close has averaged about three-quarters of forecast in year 1 and has not caught up by year 5 in the S&P sample (`t-traffic-forecast-accuracy`; its limit: the sample is anonymized and dated 2002 to 2005); a field's plateau life is always shorter than reserves divided by plateau rate; energy is a quarter or more of desalinated water cost and the capital charge often more (from the chapter's own arithmetic, not a market statistic). No market figures for PUE, specific energy consumption, or berth utilization (no fact sheet).
- Common mistakes: confusing bcf and bcm, or MMscfd and MMBtu/day; treating years of reserves at plateau as field life; adding resources to reserves; using AADT on the busiest section for the whole road; treating a hospital's lifecycle cost as a flat annual amount; assuming a data center's power is a tenant problem.
- Questions experts ask: What is the binding constraint, and when does it bind? Who certified the resource or the forecast, under which code, and at what price? What does the free alternative cost the user? Which link in the chain is outside our control, and what happens on the day it fails?

## 12.11 Judgment drill

- The situation (Illustrative): It is August 2026. You are the mandated lead arranger's head of mining finance for a USD 1.1 billion copper project run by Mount Gawler Copper Pty Ltd in South Australia. The sponsor's model schedules production from Proven and Probable reserves for 14 years and then from Inferred resources for another 9 years, and asks for an 11-year loan with a balloon refinanced against the later years. The sponsor argues that Inferred tonnage in this deposit "always convert" and cites its own experience at a neighboring mine. Credit committee is on August 27.
- Reasoning: Inferred resources have the lowest geological confidence and are not reserves; lenders size on reserves only and want a reserve tail (mine life remaining after final maturity), whose size is negotiated and is stated here without a market figure (no fact sheet); mining debt typically amortizes over a short period after completion (`t-market-norms-2`: about six to eight years in its sample), so an 11-year tenor with a balloon is already long; a balloon refinanced against Inferred material is a bet on conversion drilling; the expert response is to size the loan on the reserve case with a full amortization profile and a reserve tail test, and to offer an accordion or later refinancing if drilling converts resources to reserves.
- What would change the answer: an infill drilling program completed before close that converts a defined share to Indicated and then Probable; a completion guarantee that extends beyond construction to a reserve test; a lower copper price deck that shrinks reserves; an offtaker prepayment that shifts risk.

## 13.1 Purpose and capability statement

Purpose. The book's models are built in Excel, and Part VII builds the Case P model cell by cell. Chapter 5 gave the reader a first spreadsheet (`\cref{ssec:5.2.2}`); this chapter turns that into the working habits that make a project workbook trustworthy: one place for each input, time across columns, one formula per row, flags instead of IF chains, functions that survive inserted rows and columns, and checks that turn red when something breaks. It teaches those habits on a small practice workbook for an illustrative Chilean wind farm, then the what-if tools (data tables and goal seek), the problem of circular references that every project model meets (interest during construction depends on debt, which depends on interest during construction), the minimal VBA needed to write a copy-paste macro, and the error checks that a lender's model auditor will look for. It sets the book's range-names policy. Model architecture to a modeling standard is Chapter 39; the circularity method used in the Case P model is fixed in Chapter 40.

"What you will be able to do":

- Lay out a workbook with separate inputs, time, calculation, and check sheets, and write formulas that copy across every period without editing (Capability 4).
- Build date rows, counters, and timing flags for construction, operations, and a stub period with EOMONTH, MIN, MAX, and AND logic (Capability 4).
- Use INDEX/MATCH, XLOOKUP, SUMPRODUCT, data tables, and goal seek, and replace fragile constructs such as OFFSET and INDIRECT (Capability 4).
- Resolve a simple interest-during-construction circularity in closed form, by iteration, and with a short macro you write yourself, and design error checks that a reviewer can trust (Capability 4).

In April 2013 Thomas Herndon, Michael Ash, and Robert Pollin of the University of Massachusetts Amherst tried to replicate Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's 2010 paper "Growth in a Time of Debt" and, using the authors' own working spreadsheet, found a formula that left five countries (Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark) out of an average, alongside selective exclusion of years and an unconventional weighting; corrected, average real growth for advanced economies with public debt above 90% of GDP was 2.2%, not the published -0.1%. ## 13.6 Frameworks

- **Framework 13.1, the formula robustness test** (`fw:formula-robustness-test`; original). Purpose: decide whether a formula is fit for a model that other people will change. Steps: (1) Does the same formula copy across the whole row unchanged? (2) Does every reference point to a labeled input or calculation, with no hard-coded numbers other than 0, 1, 12, and labeled unit conversions? (3) Will it still be right if a row or column is inserted anywhere on any sheet? (4) Can a reviewer trace its precedents with Excel's tools (no OFFSET, INDIRECT, or hidden range names)? (5) Are the units of every term consistent with the row's unit? (6) Does a check somewhere turn red if it goes wrong? Use: when writing each new row, and as the first pass of reviewing someone else's sheet (Chapter 44 extends it into the full review checklist).

## 13.10 Practitioner's notebook (outline)

- Checklist (before sending a workbook to anyone): every input in one place with units and sources; one formula per row; no hard-coded numbers; no OFFSET or INDIRECT; no range names except `Scenario`; flags not IFs; checks on every sheet and a master check at 0; iteration off unless the model's documented method needs it; data tables set to calculate; goal-seek results stored as inputs with checks; any macro's convergence check visible on the Checks sheet; file opens without external links; functions available in the counterparty's Excel version.
- Red flags in someone else's workbook: different formulas in the same row; numbers typed into formulas; iterative calculation switched on with no note; IFERROR wrapped around core calculations; hidden rows or sheets; merged cells; external links; OFFSET or INDIRECT; a macro that must be run before the numbers are right with no check showing whether it was; XLOOKUP in a file going to a counterparty on Excel 2016 or 2019.
- Rules of thumb and their limits: a formula longer than the screen is two rows; a check tolerance of USD 0.001 million suits a model in USD million (a model in units needs a different one); closed forms beat macros when the loop is linear.
- Common mistakes: anchoring the wrong part of a mixed reference; NPV off by one period; XIRR with unsorted or missing dates; approximate-match lookups on unsorted data; data tables left on manual; goal seek used as if it were live; a macro with no pass limit.
- Questions experts ask: Where does this number come from? What happens if I insert a column here? Is the circularity broken by design or by luck? Which check would turn red if this row were wrong?

## 13.11 Judgment drill

- The situation (Illustrative): It is June 2026. You are the model reviewer for the mandated lead arranger on a USD 410 million financing of Viento del Istmo S.A.P.I. de C.V.'s 297 MW wind farm in Oaxaca, Mexico (fictional). - Reasoning: the difference is a symptom of a loop that converges to settings-dependent answers when the data table recalculates under iteration; OFFSET hides precedents, so the model auditor cannot certify the Debt sheet; a full rebuild in nine days is not realistic, but targeted repairs are: replace OFFSETs with INDEX on the Debt sheet, break the IDC and fee loop with a closed form (or a copy-paste macro with a visible convergence check) and add a convergence check, move the data table to a sensitivity run that does not depend on iteration, and require the model auditor's sign-off on the repaired version as a condition precedent. Expert answer: do not sign on the current file; agree a 48-hour repair list with the sponsor's modeler, and size the debt from the repaired model.
- What would change the answer: if the difference were below the documented tolerance and traced to display rounding; if the model auditor had already tested and accepted the iterative method; if the debt were sized on a separate lender model (the sponsor's model then matters less).
