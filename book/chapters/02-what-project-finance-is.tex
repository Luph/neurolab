\chapter{What project finance is, and when to use it}\label{ch:2}

On April 21, 2016, SunEdison, Inc.\ filed for Chapter~11 bankruptcy protection, which let it keep operating while a court supervised its debts. Seven months earlier its consolidated balance sheet, the combined accounts of SunEdison and every company it controlled, had shown total liabilities of USD~16.1 billion, of which USD~11.7 billion was debt (SunEdison 2015). SunEdison filed with commitments for up to USD~300 million of new financing to keep its business running in bankruptcy (SunEdison 2016).

Two companies that SunEdison controlled were missing from the list of debtors. TerraForm Power and TerraForm Global were listed on NASDAQ and owned operating wind and solar plants, many of them built or bought by SunEdison. Approximately USD~3.8 billion of SunEdison's consolidated debt had been borrowed by the two TerraForm companies and the projects beneath them. Much of the rest had been borrowed by project companies and warehouse vehicles (companies that hold new projects until they are sold or refinanced) that SunEdison owned directly, without recourse to SunEdison itself (SunEdison 2015). Lenders to those borrowers could look only to them and to their projects. Neither TerraForm company was pulled into the bankruptcy.

Their governance was pulled in anyway. On November 20, 2015, five months before its own filing, SunEdison had used its voting control of TerraForm Power to remove that company's chief executive and chief financial officer (TerraForm Power 2016).

The Llano Pardo deal of \cref{ch:1} used the same structure and named its parts in passing: a company that owned one plant, lenders who could look only to that plant, and owners who could lose only what they had put in. The TerraForm companies had all three, and their sponsor's bankruptcy still reached them.

\section{The project company and its ring-fence}\label{sec:2.1}

\subsection{A company with one asset and no history}\label{ssec:2.1.1}

When Sterrenberg Bank's credit committee approved its half of the Llano Pardo loan in 2016, Llano Pardo Solar SA had no audited accounts, no revenue, no employees of note, and no track record. It had been incorporated in Corredana for one purpose: to build and own a 50~MW solar plant on 152 hectares of leased plateau and sell its output to the state utility, ELNACOR, for 20 years. A lender to an established company would have asked how the borrower had performed over the past five years. The committee could only ask what the plant would do over the next 20, and who had promised what.

The committee's question, what the plant would do and who had promised what, is the one every project lender asks. \term{Project finance} is financing in which lenders are repaid from the cash flows of a single ring-fenced project and secured on its assets and contracts, with limited or no recourse to the sponsors. Each of its three features changes how a lender works. Because repayment comes from one project's cash flows, the lender studies the project, not the owner: its output, its revenue contract, its costs. Because the security is the project's assets and contracts, the lender's protection on default is the right to take over a working plant together with the contracts that make it earn money. Because recourse to the owners is limited or absent, the lender cannot fall back on a parent company's balance sheet, the statement of what it owns and owes (\cref{ssec:7.4.1}), when the forecasts prove wrong.

The legal device that makes all three features possible is the \term{special purpose vehicle} (SPV): a company created to own and operate one project and to do nothing else, so that the project's assets, contracts, and debts are held apart from its owners'. Its legal form follows the host country's company law. Llano Pardo Solar is a Corredanan \emph{sociedad anónima} (SA). Case P's Bélanou Power is a Kessaran \emph{société anonyme}, also SA. In the United States the usual form is a limited liability company: Sabine Pass Liquefaction, LLC, which borrowed to build the first plant to export liquefied natural gas (LNG) from the lower 48 states (\cref{ssec:2.3.3}), is one. In England it is a private limited company. Whatever the form, the company's liability must be separate from its shareholders', its shares must be capable of being pledged to lenders, and it must be able to hold the licenses and sign the contracts the project needs. Once it holds a project, the SPV is the \term{project company}: the special purpose vehicle that owns the project, signs its contracts, and borrows its debt.

The owners of the project company are its sponsors. A \term{sponsor} is a company that develops a project and owns equity in its project company, carrying the first loss. Llano Pardo had two: Tallisford Energy Partners, a London fund manager, with 60\%, and Grupo Arismendi, a Corredanan family group, with 40\%. The sponsors' equity is paid in before the lenders lose a dollar and is repaid only after the lenders have received what is due, so the sponsors absorb any shortfall first. \Cref{ch:4} sorts sponsors into strategic, financial, and developer types; the definition here needs only that first-loss position.

Grupo Arismendi already had companies that could have signed the power purchase agreement (PPA) with ELNACOR, and every party at the table had a reason to want a new one instead. The lenders wanted a borrower whose only assets and liabilities were the project's, so that the cash they forecast would be the cash they received and no unrelated creditor would stand beside them. The sponsors wanted their other businesses protected if the plant failed, and they wanted to be able to sell the project later by selling shares instead of transferring dozens of contracts and licenses one by one. Tallisford, a fund with investors of its own, needed a holding it could value and eventually sell. ELNACOR and the Ministry of Energy and Mines wanted a counterparty whose sole business was delivering the plant they had tendered for, so that its obligations under the PPA would be easier to enforce and the plant easier to keep running if a sponsor failed.

\subsection{What the ring-fence keeps in and keeps out}\label{ssec:2.1.2}

A new company is not enough on its own. Nothing in Corredanan company law stops Llano Pardo Solar SA from borrowing more money, guaranteeing a sponsor's bank loan, buying a second site, or paying its cash into an account Grupo Arismendi also uses. Each of those acts would let other creditors or other businesses reach the cash that the lenders sized their loan against, so the finance documents add a set of promises that close those routes.

\term{Ring-fencing} is the legal and contractual separation that keeps a project's assets and cash flows apart from its sponsors' other businesses, protecting each from the other's failure. It works in two directions. A lender that confirms only that the sponsors are protected from the project can forget to confirm that the project is protected from the sponsors' creditors.

The first direction protects the sponsors from the project. If Llano Pardo had failed, Sterrenberg Bank and the Atlantic Basin Development Bank (ABDB) could have enforced their security over the plant, its contracts, its accounts, and the shares in the project company, and nothing else. Tallisford's other funds and Grupo Arismendi's agribusiness were beyond their reach. The sponsors' maximum loss was the equity they had put in.

The second direction protects the project's lenders from the sponsors. If Grupo Arismendi's agribusiness had collapsed in 2020, its creditors could have claimed its assets, including its 40\% shareholding in Llano Pardo Solar SA. Those shares were already pledged to the project lenders, so the agribusiness creditors would have taken them subject to the pledge, and the value they could extract was the shares' value after the project debt: in practice, the right to future distributions once the lenders had been paid. They could not seize the plant, the PPA receivables (the money ELNACOR owed for power already delivered), or the cash in the project accounts, because none of those belonged to Grupo Arismendi. \Cref{exh:2.1} draws both directions on Llano Pardo's parties.

The promises that build the ring-fence usually sit together in the covenant section of the common terms agreement, the master finance document that all the senior lenders sign (\cref{ch:51}). The \term{single-purpose undertaking} is a covenant by the project company to carry on no business other than the project. A \term{separateness undertaking} is a covenant by the project company to keep its own books, bank accounts, and decisions separate from its owners' and to deal with them only on arm's-length terms. A limit on other financial debt and on guarantees keeps new creditors from standing beside the lenders, and a ban on mergers, acquisitions, and subsidiaries stops the project company from becoming something else.

Sponsors and lenders usually add one more layer. Each sponsor holds its shares through a single-purpose holding company of its own (\cref{ssec:67.2.1}), so the lenders can take their share pledge over a clean entity and, on default, enforce it by selling the shares, while the sponsor can sell or borrow against its stake at that level without touching the project company (\cref{ssec:31.3.1}).

\Cref{cl:2.1} shows how the promises read in a contract. It is drafted under English law for the common terms agreement of a project that the rest of this chapter follows: Parque Eólico Alto Huelén, a 240~MW wind farm in Chile's Biobío region that Generadora Litoral Andino SA (GLA), a generator listed on the Santiago Stock Exchange, was preparing to finance in 2024. Words with capital letters, such as ``Borrower'' and ``Project Documents,'' are defined terms: a definitions clause at the front of the agreement gives each one an exact meaning, and \cref{ch:10} and \cref{ch:51} teach the convention.

\begin{clause}{Single-purpose and separateness undertaking, common terms agreement \illustrative}\label{cl:2.1}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Business.} The Borrower shall not carry on any business other than the development, ownership, construction, operation, maintenance and financing of the Project in accordance with the Project Documents, and activities reasonably incidental to it.
\item \emph{Financial Indebtedness and guarantees.} The Borrower shall not incur or permit to subsist any Financial Indebtedness, or give any guarantee or indemnity in respect of the obligations of any person, other than Permitted Financial Indebtedness.
\item \emph{Separateness.} The Borrower shall: (i) maintain its own books, records and bank accounts, separate from those of any Sponsor or Affiliate; (ii) not commingle its funds with those of any other person; (iii) hold itself out as a separate legal entity and conduct business in its own name; and (iv) enter into any transaction with a Sponsor or Affiliate only on arm's length terms, in writing, and, where the value of the transaction exceeds USD 250,000 in any Financial Year, with the prior consent of the Facility Agent.
\item \emph{Corporate structure.} The Borrower shall not enter into any amalgamation, demerger, merger or corporate reconstruction, acquire any company, business or undertaking, or establish or acquire any Subsidiary.
\end{enumerate}
\end{clause}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Paragraph (a). ``Project Documents'' is the defined list of the project's contracts. The words ``reasonably incidental'' carry the negotiation: sponsors want them wide enough to cover grid services or a co-located battery without asking; lenders want them narrow, because each new activity brings risks the model did not test. In Latin American renewable financings closed 2020 to 2025 the usual landing was a narrow clause plus a consent process (indicative).
\item Paragraph (b). An indemnity is a promise to make good another person's loss. ``Permitted Financial Indebtedness'' is a defined list, typically the senior facilities, hedging under the hedging policy, shareholder loans that are subordinated (repaid only after the senior lenders have been paid), and a small working-capital basket, meaning a capped amount allowed as an exception. The guarantee ban protects the second direction of the ring-fence: without it, a sponsor could ask the project company to guarantee the sponsor's own corporate loan.
\item Paragraph (c). An ``Affiliate'' is any company in the same group as a Sponsor, and the ``Facility Agent'' is the bank that administers the loan for all the lenders. Separateness is what persuades a court in a sponsor's insolvency that the project company is a different person. A project company that shared bank accounts with its parent, paid the parent's bills, and let the parent sign for it invites a creditor's argument that the two should be treated as one. The consent threshold in sub-paragraph (iv) aims at a common way value leaks out of a ring-fenced project: an engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) or operations and maintenance (O\&M) contract with a sponsor's affiliate on soft terms.
\item Paragraph (d). A project company that merged with another company would import that company's creditors. A ``Subsidiary'' is a company the Borrower controls. Lenders sometimes allow one for a specific purpose, such as holding a transmission line that must later be transferred to a utility, and list it as an exception.
\end{enumerate}

Whether the ring-fence holds in the end depends on the insolvency law of the sponsor's country, not on the clause. US bankruptcy courts can, in rare cases, order the substantive consolidation of a parent and a subsidiary that were run as one business: the court treats the two as a single debtor, so the creditors of each can reach the assets of both. English courts almost never disregard a company's separate personality; the Supreme Court confined that power to cases where a company is interposed to evade, or frustrate the enforcement of, an existing obligation or restriction of its controller (Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd 2013). French law can extend a debtor's insolvency proceedings to another company, such as a subsidiary, whose assets are commingled with the debtor's or which is a sham (France 2022). In each system the clause matters less than the conduct behind it (\cref{ch:64} follows a ring-fenced project through its sponsor's insolvency): a project company that has actually kept its own accounts, signed its own contracts, and dealt with its owners at arm's length gives a court little reason to treat it as part of its sponsor.

\subsection{SunEdison, TerraForm, and a ring-fence under test}\label{ssec:2.1.3}

SunEdison's collapse tested the second direction of the ring-fence, the protection of projects and their lenders from a failing sponsor. The debt and the assets stayed separate; the governance did not.

The structure had three layers. At the bottom sat individual wind and solar projects, many with their own non-recourse project debt. In the middle sat TerraForm Power, a listed company that SunEdison floated on NASDAQ in July 2014 to buy operating projects. At the top sat SunEdison, which developed new projects, sold finished ones into TerraForm Power, and kept control through high-vote Class~B shares and incentive distribution rights, a claim to a rising share of TerraForm Power's distributions. This listed structure is called a yieldco (\cref{sec:32.7}). TerraForm Power borrowed on its own account through notes, which are bonds sold to investors (\cref{ssec:6.7.1}): in 2015 its subsidiary Terra Operating LLC issued USD~950 million of 5.875\% unsecured senior notes due 2023 (USD~800 million in January and USD~150 million in June) and USD~300 million of 6.125\% unsecured senior notes due 2025, guaranteed by Terra LLC, to pay for acquisitions. Each issue was governed by an indenture, the bond's contract, which required TerraForm Power to file its accounts with the US securities regulator on time (TerraForm Power 2016).

The separation of debt and assets held. Neither TerraForm company filed for bankruptcy, neither was substantively consolidated with SunEdison, and the project-level lenders kept their claims on their own projects. In March 2017 Brookfield agreed to become TerraForm Power's sponsor and bought into a business that had stayed whole; it completed the purchase of TerraForm Global in December 2017 (TerraForm Power 2018; TerraForm Global 2017).

Control was never separated, by design. By January 2016 SunEdison held 84.1\% of TerraForm Power's votes against 34.5\% of its economic interest; the board changes of November 2015 showed what that control could do. SunEdison had also pledged its Class~B shares and its incentive distribution rights to its own lenders, so control of TerraForm Power had become collateral for SunEdison's creditors (\cref{ssec:63.7.3}). TerraForm Power's 2015 accounts, filed only on December 5, 2016, carried a going-concern note, the auditor's warning that a company may not be able to keep operating for the next year (\cref{ssec:7.11.1}). The note cited two risks: that a bankruptcy court might order substantive consolidation with SunEdison, and that defaults could occur under the project-level financings; the annual report also listed the loss of SunEdison's services among TerraForm Power's risks. The late filing put the reporting covenants in the note indentures and TerraForm Power's NASDAQ listing at risk. In November 2016 SunEdison's creditors' committee, which represents the unsecured creditors in a bankruptcy, sought the court's permission to sue to unwind payments and asset transfers between SunEdison and TerraForm Power. Those claims were released only in TerraForm Power's March 2017 settlement with the SunEdison debtors, signed alongside the Brookfield agreement (TerraForm Power 2016; TerraForm Power 2018).

A ring-fence protects exactly what its contracts and corporate separations cover, and the TerraForm story returns when sponsor credit risk is analyzed (\cref{ssec:14.18.1}). At TerraForm the debt and the assets were separated, while votes, management, services, and the pipeline of new projects were tied to the sponsor, and the sponsor's failure traveled through every one of those ties. When you review a structure, ask three questions beyond the debt: who appoints the board, who provides the services the company cannot run without, and which agreements fall away if the sponsor fails.

\section{What lenders can reach}\label{sec:2.2}

\subsection{Non-recourse, limited recourse, full recourse}\label{ssec:2.2.1}

``Recourse'' is a lender's right to claim against someone other than the borrower. Every lender has full claims against the borrower itself; the question in project finance is what the lenders can claim from the sponsors when the project company cannot pay. The answers sit on a scale with three named points.

\term{Non-recourse debt} is debt whose lenders can look only to the project company's assets and cash flows, never to the sponsors. Llano Pardo's loan was non-recourse from financial close. The sponsors committed to contribute their share of the project's cost as equity, USD~16.3 million in total, and gave nothing else: no guarantee of the loan, no promise to fund overruns, no undertaking to complete the plant. Had ELNACOR stopped paying in 2019 and the plant's revenue collapsed, Tallisford and Grupo Arismendi would have lost their USD~16.3 million and owed the lenders nothing more. Strictly, the equity commitment is itself an obligation the sponsors owe until it is paid in, so even the purest non-recourse deal involves the sponsors' money; the label means that once the agreed equity is in, the lenders' only remaining claim is on the project.

\term{Full recourse debt} is debt for which the borrower's owner is liable without limit. A parent company that borrows in its own name and spends the money on a project gives full recourse, because its whole balance sheet stands behind the loan. So does a parent that guarantees its subsidiary's loan in full and for the loan's whole life.

\term{Limited recourse debt} is debt for which the sponsors give defined, capped support, usually until completion, beyond their equity. Most construction-stage financings in which the sponsors keep technology or overrun risk sit here. The sponsors promise something more than their equity for a defined period or up to a defined amount; the promise falls away when the risk it covers has passed, and the loan becomes non-recourse for the rest of its life. The amount and the period are what the parties negotiate, and \cref{ssec:2.2.2} gives them a framework.

The scale matters well beyond the sponsors and the project lenders. A sponsor's own lenders, and the credit rating agencies that grade its debt, count full recourse debt as the sponsor's debt, ignore non-recourse debt for most purposes, and argue about limited recourse support. A credit rating is an agency's opinion of how likely a borrower is to repay, expressed on a letter scale. The grades run from AAA at the top through AA, A, and BBB, then BB and below; on the scales of S\&P and Fitch, BBB- is the lowest grade that counts as investment grade, Moody's equivalent is Baa3, and anything below is called speculative grade or, informally, junk (\cref{ssec:30.5.1}). A downgrade raises what a company pays to borrow and can shut it out of some markets, which is why a sponsor's finance director watches every promise made to a project's lenders. An EPC contractor that also owns equity in a project can find that its parent's completion guarantee to the project lenders appears in its own covenant calculations. And when a host government guarantees an offtaker's payments, it is providing recourse of a different kind, to the state instead of the sponsors (\cref{ch:17}).

\subsection{Where limited recourse sits in practice}\label{ssec:2.2.2}

\term{Sponsor support} is any undertaking by the sponsors to provide funds or performance to the project company beyond their base equity, and \cref{ch:26} owns the instruments. A cost-overrun undertaking commits the sponsors to fund construction costs above the budget, up to a cap, usually as subordinated equity. A completion guarantee goes further: until the plant reaches financial completion, the lenders' test of physical, operational, commercial, and financial performance (\cref{ssec:26.5.2}), the sponsors guarantee the senior debt itself, so a project abandoned during construction becomes the sponsors' debt to repay. Contingent equity is an extra equity commitment drawn only if named events occur (\cref{ssec:32.3.3}). Each has a trigger, a cap, and a release date, and a term sheet that asks for ``sponsor support'' without specifying all three is unfinished.

Lenders also ask for credit support behind the base equity itself. An equity letter of credit is a bank's undertaking to pay the sponsor's committed equity if the sponsor does not (\cref{ssec:32.3.2}). It removes the risk that a sponsor cannot pay when called, and it adds nothing to what the sponsor can lose.

\begin{framework}[label={fw:recourse-ladder}]{The recourse ladder}
\begin{enumerate}
\item List every route by which the lenders can reach the sponsors: base equity commitment, contingent equity, cost-overrun undertaking, completion guarantee, debt guarantee.
\item For each route, record its trigger, its cap (in currency), and its release date or event.
\item Compute the sponsor's maximum loss on the project if it fails before completion and if it fails after completion, adding each route that is live at that time.
\item Compare both figures with the sponsor's appetite for the project and with its covenant and rating headroom, and identify the rung on which the sponsor can stand.
\end{enumerate}
Use it whenever a term sheet asks for sponsor support, on either side of the table.
\end{framework}

The \term{recourse ladder} is this book's name for that method. It turns a debate about labels (``is this still non-recourse?'') into a debate about numbers, which is where sponsors and lenders can trade. \Cref{ex:2.1} climbs it.

\begin{example}{Climbing the recourse ladder \illustrative}\label{ex:2.1}
In May 2024 GLA's board is choosing how to fund Alto Huelén, which will cost USD~389.6 million to build. The wind farm will sell 80\% of its expected output for 15 years under a US-dollar PPA with a Chilean copper miner rated BBB-, and the rest at market prices. The power does not travel to the mine. Chile's wholesale market sets a separate price at each point, or node, of the grid (\cref{ssec:11.11.1}), and the PPA is pay-as-produced and settled through that market: the miner pays the contract price for 80\% of whatever the wind farm produces and settles any difference between the market price at the wind farm's node and at the mine's. GLA keeps the risk that the system operator, the body that runs the grid and decides which plants run, curtails the wind farm. If project financed, the project company would borrow 75\% of the cost and GLA would contribute the rest as equity:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Debt} = 0.75 \times 389.6 = 292.2, \qquad \text{Equity} = 389.6 - 292.2 = 97.4 \quad \text{(USD m)}.
\end{equation*}
The turbines come under a fixed-price turbine supply agreement, but the foundations, roads, and cabling sit in a separate balance-of-plant contract, and the interface between the two contracts is a frequent source of overruns on multi-contract wind farms (\cref{ch:23}). Suppose that before completion a foundation redesign raises the cost by 14\%, an overrun of $0.14 \times 389.6 = $ USD~54.5 million, and that whoever is obliged to fund it does so. GLA can stand on any of four rungs.

On the full-recourse rung, GLA borrows the whole USD~389.6 million in its own name (the corporate route of \cref{ex:2.3}) and funds the overrun the same way. If the wind farm becomes worthless, at any date, GLA still owes what it borrowed: a maximum loss of $389.6 + 54.5 = $ USD~444.1 million.

Under a completion guarantee, the project company borrows USD~292.2 million and GLA guarantees that debt until financial completion. If the project fails before completion, GLA loses its equity, the overrun it funded, and the guaranteed debt: $97.4 + 54.5 + 292.2 = $ USD~444.1 million, the same as full recourse. At completion the guarantee is released, and if the project fails afterward GLA loses only the money it has put in, $97.4 + 54.5 = $ USD~151.9 million.

A capped cost-overrun undertaking, the rung the lenders' first term sheet asks for, commits GLA to fund overruns up to 15\% of the cost, $0.15 \times 389.6 = $ USD~58.4 million, before completion. The USD~54.5 million overrun is inside the cap, so GLA funds all of it, and its maximum loss is USD~151.9 million whether the project fails before or after completion. Completion releases the promise to fund further overruns, not the money already spent. On the day the loan was signed, before any overrun was known, GLA's exposure on this rung was $97.4 + 58.4 = $ USD~155.8 million; had no overrun occurred, completion would have cut it to the USD~97.4 million of base equity.

On the non-recourse rung GLA commits its USD~97.4 million of equity and nothing else. It may choose to fund the overrun to protect its equity, but it cannot be made to, so its maximum loss is USD~97.4 million and the lenders own the overrun problem. In practice they would not wait for it: lenders to a non-recourse construction loan size a contingency inside the budget and often a standby facility, extra debt committed at financial close for overruns up to an amount the independent engineer sets (\cref{sec:31.7}). The ladder shows who pays beyond those buffers.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}L rr@{}}
\toprule
Rung (USD m) & Fails before completion & Fails after completion\\
\midrule
Full recourse & 444.1 & 444.1\\
Completion guarantee & 444.1 & 151.9\\
Capped cost-overrun undertaking & 151.9 & 151.9\\
Non-recourse & 97.4 & 97.4\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The gaps between rungs show what each request is worth. Moving from the completion guarantee to the capped undertaking removes USD~292.2 million of pre-completion exposure, the whole senior loan; moving from the capped undertaking to non-recourse removes the USD~54.5 million overrun, and with it the balance-of-plant interface risk the lenders most want the sponsor to carry. Release dates matter as much as caps, because every rung but full recourse falls back to the money already invested once the lenders' completion test is passed (\cref{ch:61}). Whether GLA can afford even the capped rung is a separate question, which its covenants answer in \cref{ex:2.3}.
\end{example}

\section{The contractual web}\label{sec:2.3}

\subsection{Contracts in place of a balance sheet}\label{ssec:2.3.1}

A lender to an established company takes comfort from its balance sheet: many assets, many customers, retained earnings, a history of surviving bad years. Llano Pardo Solar SA had none of that. It had a set of signed contracts, each of which took one uncertainty about the project's future and turned it into a promise from a named party (\cref{exh:1.1}).

The PPA fixed the revenue. ELNACOR agreed to buy every megawatt-hour the plant produced for 20 years at USD~58.20/MWh, to pay for energy it could not take because of grid curtailment above 50 hours a year, and to back its payments with a standby letter of credit for three months of expected revenue. The EPC contract fixed the construction cost and date: Montajes Cordillera would deliver a working plant for USD~49.1 million by December 31, 2017, or pay delay damages of USD~28,700 a day. The O\&M agreement fixed the largest single operating cost, a fee of USD~0.41 million a year, for five years. The 30-year land lease with the farmers' cooperative fixed the site. The Ministry of Finance's letter of support, which is not a guarantee, gave the lenders the host government's stated backing for ELNACOR's obligations.

Two of those contracts appear in almost every project financing. A \term{power purchase agreement} (PPA) is a long-term contract under which a buyer, usually a utility, pays a generator for capacity, energy, or both at prices set by formula (\cref{sec:18.1}). A capacity payment is a fixed monthly amount for keeping the plant available whether or not the buyer takes its output; an energy payment is paid for each megawatt-hour delivered. Llano Pardo's PPA paid for energy only, while gas-fired plants are usually paid both (\cref{ssec:18.1.1}). An \term{engineering, procurement, and construction contract} (EPC contract) is a contract under which one contractor designs, procures, and builds the plant, usually for a fixed price and a fixed completion date, with liquidated damages, sums fixed in advance, for delay and underperformance (\cref{ch:22}). The buyer under the PPA is the \term{offtaker}: the party that buys the project's output under a long-term contract. A utility under a PPA, an LNG buyer under a sale and purchase agreement, a government paying for a road's availability, and a technology company leasing a data center are all offtakers.

Together the contracts form what this book calls the \term{contractual web}: the set of interlocking contracts that fixes a project's revenues, costs, and risks and so takes the place of a corporate balance sheet as the lenders' protection. The PPA's latest permitted commercial operation date has to fall after the EPC contract's guaranteed completion date, with a margin. The delay damages the EPC contractor pays have to cover what the project company loses each day the plant is late. Where the pieces fail to interlock, the gap belongs to the sponsors, and Llano Pardo found one in its first operating year: dry-season dust on the modules and a tracker-controller firmware fault cut output 7.5\% below forecast and the sponsors' distribution by USD~0.44 million (\cref{ex:1.9}). The O\&M operator's availability guarantee protected inverter and tracker availability, not the cleanliness of the modules, so no contract moved the soiling loss away from the sponsors (\cref{ch:28} shows how to scan a web for such gaps before signing).

\subsection{Giving each risk to the party best able to manage it}\label{ssec:2.3.2}

Every project financier repeats the rule that each risk should go to the party best able to manage it, and \cref{ch:15} turns it into a working method. Repeated without tests, the rule justifies whatever the stronger party wanted. The \term{risk allocation principle} is the rule that each risk should be borne by the party best able to control it, price it, and absorb its loss.

Control asks who can change the probability or size of the loss. The EPC contractor controls the construction schedule; the offtaker controls whether it takes the energy; nobody controls the wind. Pricing asks who can estimate the risk and charge for it with the least uncertainty, because a party that cannot price a risk will either refuse it or charge a premium for its ignorance: an EPC contractor that has built 410~MW of solar plants prices schedule risk better than a fund manager in London. Capacity to absorb asks whether the party can pay the loss when it happens without failing. A contractor whose net worth is smaller than the delay damages it has promised has taken the risk only up to its net worth, and beyond that the loss returns to the project company. When all three tests point to the same party, the allocation is rarely disputed; \cref{ex:2.2} shows what happens when they diverge.

\begin{example}{Assigning the 2018 soiling loss at Llano Pardo \illustrative}\label{ex:2.2}
In Llano Pardo's first operating year, 2018, output fell 7.5\% short of the P50 forecast, the output level expected to be beaten in half of all years. Revenue came in at USD~7.25 million against a forecast USD~7.84 million, a shortfall of USD~0.59 million, and the sponsors' distribution fell from USD~1.82 million to USD~1.38 million (\cref{ex:1.9}). Two causes combined: dry-season dust settled on the modules faster than four cleaning cycles a year could remove it, and a firmware fault parked 11\% of the trackers flat for 23 days in March. The firmware fault was a defect in the tracker supplier's equipment, a separate risk with a separate owner. \Cref{ch:1} does not split the shortfall between the causes, but soiling was most of it. Who should have carried the soiling?

Start with control. Cordillera Servicios, the O\&M operator, decides how often the modules are washed. ELNACOR controls nothing about module cleanliness. Montajes Cordillera, the EPC contractor, handed over a plant that passed its performance test at 81.4\% against a guaranteed 81.0\%, and dust in the first dry season is not a construction defect. The sponsors control the O\&M agreement but not the dust. Control points to the O\&M operator.

Pricing points the same way. The O\&M operator knows what an extra cleaning cycle costs in water, labor, and truck hours, and how much output it recovers, so it can quote a price for keeping the modules to a cleanliness standard. The sponsors would have to hire someone to learn it.

Capacity to absorb points away from the O\&M operator. Its whole fee was USD~0.41 million a year (\cref{exh:1.3}). A 2018-sized revenue shortfall, even if only most of it was soiling, is of the same order as a full year's fee, and a dust year twice as bad would threaten the O\&M operator's solvency and, with it, the plant's maintenance.

Put together, the tests give a specific allocation. The O\&M operator should guarantee a soiling standard, or a performance ratio measured net of weather, and pay for its failures up to a cap. Utility-scale solar O\&M contracts in Latin America and Europe from 2015 to 2025 commonly capped such liabilities at between half and all of one year's fee (indicative), USD~0.21 million to USD~0.41 million here. Because Cordillera Servicios is Montajes Cordillera's operations affiliate, the lenders would also ask Montajes Cordillera to guarantee its affiliate's payments. The O\&M operator recovers the cost of extra cleaning through a higher fee, and the sponsors keep the weather-driven variation above the cap, which no party controls. The renegotiation of 2019 came close to that answer: the O\&M operator moved from four to seven cleaning cycles a year under a renegotiated fee (\cref{ex:1.9}).
\end{example}

The commonest failure of the principle is a counterparty too small to absorb the risk it controls, as in \cref{ex:2.2}: the allocation looks right on paper and evaporates in a bad year. Lenders respond by capping the risk at what the party can bear and asking for a parent guarantee, a bank guarantee (in construction usually called a performance bond, \cref{ch:22}), or insurance behind it. A transfer can also be priced above the risk's value. An EPC contractor asked to take ground-condition risk on a tunnel it has not been allowed to investigate will price the unknown at its worst case, and the sponsors, and through them the host government or the toll payers, pay that price whether or not bad ground appears; leaving that risk with the project company, or with the host government that holds the geological data, can be cheaper. Some risks no party controls at all: a hurricane, a pandemic, a change in the world price of steel. They go to whoever is paid to hold them. Insurers take insurable events; the offtaker takes some events through force majeure clauses, which excuse a party from obligations it is prevented from performing by events beyond its control (\cref{ssec:10.5.1}), and through deemed payments, which pay for output the plant could have delivered as if it had been; the host government takes some political events; and the sponsors' equity takes the rest.

The parties' readings of the principle collide on almost every clause. Take the grid-curtailment protection in a PPA like Llano Pardo's. The sponsors argue that ELNACOR controls the grid and should pay for every hour it curtails the plant. ELNACOR answers that it cannot price congestion on a network it is still building and offers nothing for the first years. The lenders care only about size: a few dozen curtailed hours a year barely move debt service, a few hundred would, and they will not lend against output the offtaker can switch off for free. The EPC contractor, asked to guarantee output, insists that its guarantee exclude curtailment entirely, which leaves the risk with the project company unless the PPA catches it. The usual compromise, and Llano Pardo's, is a threshold: the plant absorbs the first hours each year and the offtaker pays deemed energy above them, 50 hours at Llano Pardo. The same collision over currency risk on a dollar tariff ends less tidily, because host governments refuse a risk they cannot control and sponsors cannot absorb (\cref{ch:59}).

\subsection{How contracts made Sabine Pass financeable}\label{ssec:2.3.3}

Sabine Pass in Cameron Parish, Louisiana, was built by Cheniere as a terminal to import LNG, natural gas cooled to a liquid so it can be shipped (\cref{sec:12.2}). Half of its capacity was reserved by Total and Chevron under 20-year agreements paying approximately USD~125 million a year each, used or not, and the other half by a Cheniere affiliate. US shale gas then turned the country from a likely importer of gas into a potential exporter, and Cheniere set out to add liquefaction trains, the processing lines that cool gas to liquid for export, next to the existing tanks and berths through its subsidiary Sabine Pass Liquefaction, LLC. The sponsor was a development-stage company with a weak balance sheet, and a liquefaction plant costs billions of dollars and sells into a volatile world market (Cheniere Energy Partners 2013).

Between October 2011 and January 2012 the project company signed four 20-year sale and purchase agreements (\cref{ch:21} dissects the contract), with BG, Gas Natural Fenosa, GAIL of India, and KOGAS of Korea, each for approximately 3.5 million tonnes of LNG a year. Each offtaker pays two prices. One is approximately 115\% of the Henry Hub price, the benchmark US gas price set at a Louisiana trading point, a multiplier meant to cover the feed gas and the gas the plant consumes as fuel. The other is a fixed fee of USD~2.25/MMBtu to USD~3.00/MMBtu, where an MMBtu (million British thermal units) is the standard unit of gas energy (\cref{ssec:11.2.1}); the fixed fee pays for the liquefaction capacity itself. An offtaker may cancel cargoes with notice, but it still pays the fixed fee on volumes it does not take (Sabine Pass Liquefaction 2014). An obligation to pay for a contracted quantity whether or not it is taken is called take-or-pay (\cref{ssec:18.4.1}). Once BG had added volumes to its contract, the fixed fees came to approximately USD~2.3 billion a year for the first four trains, from offtakers that were investment-grade or guaranteed by investment-grade parents. Like a capacity payment, they did not depend on gas prices, on LNG prices in Asia or Europe, or on whether the offtaker wanted the cargo.

On July 31, 2012, the project company closed a USD~3.6 billion credit facility for the first two trains from 21 banks, which the sponsor's chairman described as the largest non-recourse debt financing in the US in a decade (Cheniere Energy Partners 2012). A lump-sum turnkey contract with Bechtel, a single fixed price for a plant handed over ready to run (\cref{ssec:22.1.1}), fixed the construction cost and date, and Cheniere reported that every train was completed ahead of its guaranteed date and within budget (Cheniere Energy 2022). The contracts had moved the gas-price risk to the offtakers, who carried the Henry Hub price and their destination markets, while the project company earned a fee for turning gas into LNG.

The test came in 2020. When COVID-19 collapsed world gas prices, offtakers canceled many US cargoes. Cheniere's revenue for the first nine months of 2020 included USD~932 million from cargoes its customers chose not to take, recognized because the fixed fee was still payable (Cheniere Energy 2020). That figure covers Cheniere's whole business, including its Corpus Christi terminal, but the mechanism is the one the Sabine Pass contracts created: the fixed fee was payable whether or not a cargo was lifted, and the lenders had sized their debt to that fee.

\section{Project finance and its neighbors}\label{sec:2.4}

Project finance shares tools with six other kinds of financing, and practitioners borrow freely across the borders. What separates each from project finance is the answer to four questions: what repays the debt, what secures it, whether the lender can reach the owner, and how the debt is sized and repaid. \Cref{exh:2.2} at the end of this section sets the answers side by side.

\subsection{Corporate finance}\label{ssec:2.4.1}

GLA can fund a new plant from its existing bank facilities within weeks, with nothing more than a drawdown request, and change its plans afterward without asking anyone. Borrowing of that kind, on the strength of a company's whole balance sheet and all its cash flows, is \term{corporate finance}, also called balance-sheet financing. GLA's lenders are repaid from the cash of every plant it owns and every contract it has signed. They study its history, its rating, and its ratio of debt to earnings, and they protect themselves with financial covenants (promises written into the loan) that cap that ratio across the whole group, not with security over any one asset. Diversification does much of their risk management, because a failure at one plant is absorbed by the cash of the others, and the debt is sized to the group's earnings and usually repaid in large amounts at maturity, from new borrowing (\cref{ch:8}).

The same diversification is the weakness for large projects. A project that fails hits every creditor of the company, and a project large enough relative to the company can take the company down with it. A corporate lender also learns little about any single project, because it is lending against the company, so it cannot charge a risky project more than a safe one.

\subsection{Asset finance and leasing}\label{ssec:2.4.2}

\term{Asset finance} is debt or leasing secured on a specific movable asset and repaid by the user's general credit. An airline that borrows against two new turboprops, or a copper miner in Chile's Antofagasta region that finances 18 haul trucks, is using asset finance. The lender's protection is the asset and its resale market: if the borrower stops paying, the lender repossesses the aircraft or trucks and sells or re-leases them. Repayment, though, comes from the borrower's whole business, not from the asset's own earnings, and the loan usually amortizes, meaning it is repaid in installments, over the asset's useful life.

A lease achieves the same thing through ownership. The lessor owns the asset and the user, the lessee, pays rent for its use. A \term{finance lease} transfers substantially all the risks and rewards of owning the asset to the lessee: the lease runs for most of the asset's life, and the rent repays the lessor's cost with a return, so economically it is a secured loan in another legal form. An \term{operating lease} leaves the main risks and rewards of ownership with the lessor, which takes the risk that the asset is worth less than expected when the lease ends and a new lessee must be found. The accounting difference matters to sponsors because an offtake contract can itself contain a lease (\cref{ch:66}).

The difference from project finance lies in what makes the asset valuable. A turboprop is worth roughly the same in Lima as in Lisbon. A solar plant on the Llano Pardo plateau is worth what its PPA, its land lease, and its grid connection make it worth; detached from those contracts and moved elsewhere it is worth little more than scrap. Project lenders therefore take security over contracts and shares as well as equipment, and they plan to enforce by taking over a working plant, not by repossessing it.

\subsection{Reserve-based lending compared with project finance}\label{ssec:2.4.3}

Reserve-based lending is the oil and gas producer's version of a borrowing base (\cref{sec:75.3}), descended from the production payments of the mid-twentieth century (\cref{ch:3}). Lenders value the borrower's reserves: in US practice, proved reserves at the banks' own price assumptions; in the international market, the net present value of an agreed cash-flow model for each field, which can give credit to probable reserves and to fields still being developed. They set the maximum loan, the borrowing base, as a fraction of that value and redetermine it, usually twice a year, as prices, production, and reserves change. The facility is usually revolving, so the borrower draws and repays as its needs change. Like project finance, it lends against the cash defined assets will produce and takes security over them. Unlike project finance, it typically spans several fields, often has recourse to the producing company as a whole, and resizes the debt to the assets' value instead of fixing a repayment schedule at financial close. A falling oil price shrinks the borrowing base and forces the borrower to repay, or pledge more fields, within a cure period measured in weeks or a few months, which no project financing would allow. 

\subsection{Acquisition finance}\label{ssec:2.4.4}

\term{Acquisition finance} is debt raised to buy a company or asset, repaid from the target's cash flows and often the buyer's. When an infrastructure fund buys a portfolio of operating wind farms in Spain (\cref{ch:47} teaches such acquisitions), it typically borrows part of the price at a holding company above the farms. The lenders are repaid from the dividends the farms pay up after servicing their own project debt, and they take security over the holding company's shares in the farms (\cref{sec:31.3}).

Acquisition finance on infrastructure looks like project finance, but its lenders face a different risk. It funds no construction, so they bear none of the risks that dominate a new project. They do sit behind the project debt: when the farms' own lenders block distributions after a bad year, the holding company receives nothing, and its lenders depend on the reserves and covenants of their own loan. That position, behind the creditors of the companies that own the assets, is called structural subordination, and holding-company lenders charge a higher margin than the project lenders for it. The debt is also sized differently. Project debt is sized to the contracted cash flow and fully repaid inside the life of the contracts; acquisition debt is sized to a multiple of the target's earnings and is often a single repayment at maturity, such as a term loan B (\cref{ssec:31.3.3}), which relies on being refinanced.

\subsection{Securitization and structured finance}\label{ssec:2.4.5}

\term{Securitization} is financing by selling or pledging a pool of many similar receivables to a vehicle that issues debt repaid from the pool. A rooftop solar company with 40,000 household leases, a tower company with thousands of tenant contracts, or a bank with a book of car loans can each sell the receivables to a special purpose company that issues notes. Investors rely on statistics: they ask how many of the 40,000 households will stop paying, not whether one particular household will. The vehicle has no operating business and builds nothing; it collects and pays. Project finance relies on a few large contracts with a few named counterparties, whose individual credit and performance the lenders investigate one by one.

\term{Structured finance} is the wider family: financing that uses vehicles, tranching, and credit enhancement to shape risk for investors, not tied to a single operating project. Tranching splits the debt into slices repaid in order of seniority; credit enhancement is support, such as a guarantee, extra collateral, or a junior slice that absorbs losses first, that improves the senior investors' position (\cref{ssec:16.9.1}). The family includes securitization; whole-business-style financings, such as the bankruptcy-remote structure (one designed so that the company cannot easily be pulled into its own or its owners' insolvency, the ring-fence idea of \cref{ssec:2.1.2}) of the company that built London's Thames Tideway Tunnel, which secure the debt on an entire licensed business, one that operates under a government license and is regulated as a utility, instead of a pool of receivables or one new project (Bazalgette Tunnel Limited 2026); and the hybrid structures that technology companies have begun to use for data centers.

Meta's Hyperion data center in Richland Parish, Louisiana, shows how a structure can use the vocabulary of project finance and still be something else. In October 2025 Meta formed a joint venture with funds managed by Blue Owl Capital, which hold 80\% to Meta's 20\%, to develop the campus at a total development cost then estimated at approximately USD~27 billion. Part of the Blue Owl side's capital came from debt sold privately to PIMCO and other bond investors (Meta Platforms 2025); fund holdings filings describe the notes as senior secured and maturing in 2049 (PIMCO Variable Insurance Trust 2025). Meta manages construction and operations, and from 2029 it will lease the buildings under initial four-year terms, renewable to as long as 20 years, with an aggregate initial lease commitment of approximately USD~12.31 billion. Behind the short leases sits a residual value guarantee: for the first 16 years, if Meta does not renew a lease and certain conditions are met, Meta pays any shortfall between the property's value and a threshold that starts at approximately USD~28 billion and declines over time (Meta Platforms 2026a).

The noteholders did not underwrite a 20-year contract from a creditworthy offtaker, which is what a project lender would have asked for. They underwrote Meta's willingness to renew four-year leases on specialized buildings, backed by Meta's promise to make good a fall in the buildings' value. Meta does not guarantee the notes, and it does not consolidate the venture in its accounts, because it does not direct the decisions that most affect the venture's economics. It does disclose a maximum exposure to loss of USD~46.03 billion at June 30, 2026, against an equity investment carried at approximately USD~2.9 billion (Meta Platforms 2026b). The venture sits off Meta's balance sheet while Meta keeps a large economic exposure, a combination of vehicle, short lease, and value guarantee that is structured finance with project features (\cref{sec:82.9}).

\section{What sponsors, lenders, and governments gain from project finance}\label{sec:2.5}

\subsection{Risk isolation and debt capacity}\label{ssec:2.5.1}

A sponsor uses project finance first to keep one project's failure from becoming the company's failure, and closely tied to that, for debt capacity in its everyday sense: how much more a company can borrow before its lenders' covenants or its rating stop it. (\Cref{ssec:36.1.1} gives the term its narrower project finance meaning, the debt a project's own cash flows can support.) Both turn on one ratio that corporate lenders and rating agencies watch. \term{Net debt to EBITDA} is a leverage ratio: borrowings less cash, divided by annual earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA (\cref{ssec:7.3.1}) is roughly the cash a business's operations generate in a year before paying its lenders and the tax authority, so dividing debt by it tells a lender how many years of operating cash the debt represents.

\begin{example}{Two ways to fund a wind farm \illustrative}\label{ex:2.3}
In May 2024 GLA has EBITDA of USD~182.4 million and net debt of USD~510.6 million, a ratio of $510.6 / 182.4 = 2.80$\x. Its bank facilities cap net debt at 3.50\x{} EBITDA. Its board must decide by July 2024 how to fund the USD~389.6 million Alto Huelén wind farm (\cref{ex:2.1}), which will earn EBITDA of USD~41.3 million a year once running.

If GLA takes the corporate route, it borrows the full cost in its own name. Once the cost is fully drawn, just before the wind farm starts operating, net debt is $510.6 + 389.6 = $ USD~900.2 million, while EBITDA is still USD~182.4 million because the wind farm earns nothing yet:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{900.2}{182.4} = 4.94\text{x}.
\end{equation*}
After the commercial operation date (COD), EBITDA rises to $182.4 + 41.3 = $ USD~223.7 million and the ratio falls to $900.2 / 223.7 = 4.02$\x. The covenant is breached in both periods. The most GLA can add to its net debt without breaching is
\begin{equation*}
3.50 \times 182.4 - 510.6 = 127.8 \quad \text{(USD m)},
\end{equation*}
about a third of the wind farm's cost.

If GLA takes the project finance route, the project company borrows 75\% of the cost, USD~292.2 million, without recourse to GLA. GLA contributes the USD~97.4 million of equity, and suppose it borrows all of that at the parent. GLA's net debt rises to $510.6 + 97.4 = $ USD~608.0 million, and
\begin{equation*}
\frac{608.0}{182.4} = 3.33\text{x},
\end{equation*}
inside the covenant with USD~30.4 million of headroom left ($127.8 - 97.4$). The project debt is excluded because GLA's facility agreements, like many, leave the debt of non-recourse project subsidiaries out of net debt and count only the dividends those subsidiaries pay up, not their EBITDA. A rating agency may still add the project debt back if it judges that GLA would support the wind farm in trouble (\cref{ch:30}).

The headroom does not survive the overrun of \cref{ex:2.1}. If GLA funds the USD~54.5 million overrun under its cost-overrun undertaking with parent debt, net debt becomes $608.0 + 54.5 = $ USD~662.5 million and leverage $662.5 / 182.4 = 3.63$\x, a breach by USD~24.1 million. If the lenders' full cap of USD~58.4 million were called, leverage would reach $666.4 / 182.4 = 3.65$\x. The USD~30.4 million of headroom covers only 52\% of the cap. Funding the call from cash does not help, because net debt rises by the same amount.

Now suppose instead that two years after COD, serial gearbox failures across the turbine fleet make the wind farm worthless to its equity. Under project finance, GLA loses its USD~97.4 million (plus any overrun it funded), its covenant leverage stays where it was, and the project lenders bear the rest of the loss through their claim on a damaged wind farm and its warranties. Under the corporate route, GLA still owes the USD~389.6 million it borrowed, with no asset earning anything behind it. Its leverage returns to 4.94\x{} and stays there until it repays debt from other cash.
\end{example}

The example separates the two benefits. Debt capacity is about the covenant: the project finance route lets GLA build a wind farm costing three times its corporate headroom ($389.6 / 127.8 = 3.0$), provided the overrun support is sized to the headroom that is left, which the board paper in \cref{sec:2.8} has to solve. Risk isolation is about the failure case: the corporate route converts a project problem into a company problem. A sponsor values isolation more as the project's technology or resource risk grows relative to the sponsor's size.

Gearing, the share of a project's cost funded with debt (\cref{ssec:8.2.1}), lets GLA own a USD~389.6 million wind farm with USD~97.4 million of its own money, a quarter of the cost, and as long as the wind farm earns more on its assets than the debt costs, the return on that smaller equity investment rises; \cref{ch:8} works through the arithmetic. And project finance lets a sponsor borrow on the credit of the project's contracts and offtaker instead of its own. Cheniere's balance sheet could not have carried Sabine Pass, but four investment-grade offtakers paying fixed fees could (\cref{ssec:2.3.3}). For a sponsor with weak credit and a strong offtaker, project finance can be the cheaper debt, or the only debt available.

\term{Off-balance-sheet financing} is financing that does not count as the sponsor's debt for covenant, rating, or (where the tests are met) accounting purposes. Finance directors routinely confuse those senses. GLA would own 100\% of the project company and control it, so the project company's debt would appear in GLA's consolidated accounts under the accounting standards most listed companies use: off balance sheet here means outside the covenant calculation and, if the agencies agree, outside the rating, but still in the group accounts. Taking a project out of the group accounts requires the sponsor to give up control, as Meta did at Hyperion (\cref{ssec:2.4.5}) and as Case P's Kilnworth did when it sold part of its stake in 2026 (\cref{ch:66}).


\subsection{Governance and agency costs}\label{ssec:2.5.2}

A company that generates more cash than its good investment opportunities need is tempted to spend the surplus on bad ones: an acquisition the chief executive wants, a new business the board finds appealing, a headquarters building. An \term{agency cost} is the loss of value that arises when managers or one set of owners act in their own interest at the expense of other investors (Jensen and Meckling 1976). Michael Jensen later argued that debt reduces the agency cost of surplus cash, because fixed debt service takes the cash away from managers before they can spend it (Jensen 1986).

Project finance applies that argument with unusual force. Each December 31 at Llano Pardo (\cref{exh:1.10}), revenue from ELNACOR lands in an account administered by the facility agent and leaves it in a fixed order: operating costs and taxes, interest, principal, the top-up of the debt service reserve account, and only then, if the distribution test is passed, distributions to the sponsors. The project company's managers cannot divert a dollar to a second solar plant or a new venture, because the single-purpose undertaking forbids it and the accounts structure makes it impossible. Every dollar that passes the distribution test reaches the sponsors.

The waterfall also protects the sponsors from each other. Tallisford's fund wanted a steady cash yield for its investors. Grupo Arismendi, a family group with agribusiness and logistics interests, might have been tempted to send the project company's trucking work to its own logistics arm, or to keep cash in Corredana for its own use. Partners who would not trust each other with a joint bank account trust a waterfall that neither of them controls, administered by the facility agent on the lenders' behalf (\cref{ch:52}).

A project lender's exposure does not depend on whether the sponsor's other businesses prosper, and its information is far more complete than a corporate lender's: it has seen every contract, every cost estimate, and every assumption in the model. Moody's study of project finance bank loans to 10,452 projects originated between 1983 and 2021, drawn from a consortium of lenders covering approximately 67\% of the project financings originated worldwide in that period, shows what the discipline is worth to lenders (Moody's Investors Service 2023). The 10-year cumulative default rate, the share of loans that defaulted at some point within 10 years of origination, was 4.8\% on the Basel definition, the bank regulators' test under which a loan counts as defaulted once a payment is 90 days late or full repayment is judged unlikely (\cref{ch:68}). That is the same rate Moody's reports for corporate borrowers rated Baa3. The average ultimate recovery, the share of a defaulted loan that lenders eventually got back, was 79.5\%, and lenders recovered everything in 62\% of defaults. The shape of the default curve matters more for pricing than its total. Annual default rates in the first three years resembled those of borrowers at the high end of speculative grade, around BB, and fell toward those of borrowers rated A by about year seven, and defaults during construction recovered less on average than defaults in operation, 72.5\% against 80.8\%. That record is why lenders charge more for construction and early operations, and why sponsors refinance once a project has a few years of operating history.

Lenders get that record from four things: transparency (regular reports on a single asset they understand), control (consents over every material change, and the right to step in on default), security over everything the project company owns, and a price set for each project's own risk.

\subsection{Partnering and political deterrence}\label{ssec:2.5.3}

Project finance lets unequal partners invest together. Grupo Arismendi found the Llano Pardo site, signed the land option with the cooperative, and knew the ministries, but it could not have raised USD~65.1 million alone. Tallisford had capital and solar experience but no presence in Corredana. A project company with its own debt let each partner invest a fixed share of a defined project without exposing its other businesses to the other's, and without either partner's corporate lenders having a say. Larger projects stretch the same logic further. At financing in 2009, PNG LNG in Papua New Guinea was owned by ExxonMobil (33.2\%), Oil Search (29.0\%), Papua New Guinea state participants (19.6\%), Santos (13.5\%), and Nippon Oil (4.7\%), each holding its share of an unincorporated joint venture, a partnership created by contract rather than a company, in which each partner owns its share of the assets directly. A jointly owned company, PNG LNG Global Company, played the borrowing role a project company plays at Llano Pardo, and the co-venturers guaranteed the debt until financial completion in 2015 (PNG LNG Global Company and ExxonMobil 2010).

The lenders' identity also changes how a host government behaves. Half of the Llano Pardo loan came from the ABDB, a development bank whose shareholders include governments that lend to Corredana. A host government tempted to cut ELNACOR's tariff or delay its payments would be harming a lender its own finance ministry depends on, and would risk its standing with the bank. Practitioners call the deterrent effect of a development finance institution's or an export credit agency's presence a ``halo.'' The deterrent has limits, which \cref{ch:60} traces through the cases where it worked and where it failed.

\subsection{What the host government gets}\label{ssec:2.5.4}

Corredana's Ministry of Energy and Mines wanted 200~MW of solar power without borrowing to build it: four plants at Llano Pardo's USD~65.1 million each come to about USD~260 million. Private capital paid for the plants instead, with the cost recovered over 20 years through the tariff. Competition priced the power: four 50~MW lots were tendered in 2015, bidders priced against each other, and Llano Pardo won its lot at USD~58.20/MWh. And risk moved to private parties: if the plant had been late, over budget, or below its performance guarantee, the project company and its EPC contractor would have paid, not ELNACOR or the treasury.

What remained with the host government is less visible. ELNACOR, a state company, owed 20 years of payments; if it could not pay, the pressure to support it would fall on the Ministry of Finance, whose letter of support carried political weight even without legal force. PPAs of this kind usually contain termination provisions under which the offtaker must buy the plant for a sum that covers the outstanding debt if the agreement ends through the offtaker's or the host government's default (\cref{ch:17}). The termination payment and the implicit support for ELNACOR are contingent liabilities, obligations that become payable only if something goes wrong, and they appear in no budget until they do (\cref{ch:57}).

\section{What project finance costs}\label{sec:2.6}

\subsection{Transaction costs and time to close}\label{ssec:2.6.1}

Lenders who rely on one project's contracts and forecasts have to examine them, and the project company pays for the examination. Lenders' counsel reviews every contract and drafts the finance documents; an independent engineer reviews the design, the budget, the schedule, and the energy forecast; a model auditor checks the financial model; an insurance advisor reviews the insurance program. The sponsors need their own counsel and advisors to answer them. The lenders also charge an upfront fee for committing to a loan that, in \cref{ex:2.4}, takes more than a year to close. A corporate bond needs a prospectus, the offering document sold to investors, and a rating, and little else.

\subsection{Rigidity, information, and control}\label{ssec:2.6.2}

The larger costs of project finance never appear in a fee letter, the side letter that sets the arrangers' fees (\cref{ssec:51.1.2}): they are what the sponsors give up for the life of the loan. In 2024 Llano Pardo's owners looked at adding a 10~MW battery behind the plant's grid connection, to store midday output and sell it into ELNACOR's evening peak. On a corporate balance sheet the decision would have taken a board meeting. Inside the ring-fence, the first gate was ELNACOR: the PPA bought energy as produced at a flat price, so selling stored energy at a different hour needed a PPA amendment with a time-shifted price or dispatch rights, and probably the Ministry of Energy and Mines' agreement, since it had run the tender. The lenders would condition their own consent on that amendment. The single-purpose undertaking defined the business as a 50~MW solar plant selling under one PPA, so a battery was a new business. The limit on other debt barred the battery's financing unless the lenders agreed to permit it, and the lenders would want the battery added to their security. The base-case model had to be rebuilt with the battery's costs, revenues, and degradation, and re-audited. The independent engineer had to give a new opinion on the battery's technology, its integration with the plant's inverters, and its effect on the plant's performance under the PPA. Sterrenberg Bank and the ABDB each had to take the request through its own credit process, with the loan's USD~32.9 million outstanding at the end of 2024 (\cref{exh:1.4}) as their only exposure, before any battery could be ordered.

Consents are only the most visible control. Reporting obliges the project company to send monthly or quarterly operating reports, annual audited accounts, budgets, compliance certificates, and prompt notice of anything that could affect the lenders, so its management reports to the lenders as well as to its board. Distribution tests decide whether the sponsors receive cash at all: if the debt service cover ratio falls below the lock-up level, 1.15\x{} at Llano Pardo, cash stays in the project company until the ratio recovers (\cref{sec:37.4}). A sponsor used to moving cash around a group to wherever it earns most finds that cash trapped in a project company earns the deposit rate, for as long as the lenders' test says so (\cref{ch:62}).

\section{When project finance is the wrong tool}\label{sec:2.7}

Most of the costs in \cref{ex:2.4} are fixed. An independent engineer's review of a 24~MW wind farm costs not much less than one of a 240~MW wind farm, and lenders' counsel reads a short PPA as carefully as a long one. A project that is small relative to those costs cannot carry them, whatever its risk; a project whose cash flows cannot be separated or forecast cannot be financed this way at any size.

\begin{example}{Too small for project finance \illustrative}\label{ex:2.5}
In March 2025 Mvuli Paa Energy Ltd, a Nairobi developer, owns 214 rooftop solar systems on supermarkets and warehouses around Nairobi, with a combined capacity of 11.8~MWp (megawatts of panel capacity) and a total cost of USD~18.6 million. Each system sells its output to the building's owner under its own 15-year contract, priced and paid in Kenyan shillings. The average system is $11.8 / 214 = 0.055$~MWp, or 55~kWp, and cost about USD~86,900.

Mvuli Paa asks a bank to arrange a project financing of the portfolio. The lenders would need counsel to review 214 offtake contracts and 214 roof leases under Kenyan law, an independent engineer to sample the installations and review the energy forecasts, a model auditor, an insurance review, and credit checks on 214 building owners, and they would charge their own fees. The arranger estimates the fixed transaction costs at about USD~2.9 million, which is $2.9 / 18.6 = 15.6\%$ of the portfolio's cost, against 3.55\% on Alto Huelén (\cref{ex:2.4}). The portfolio's risk would pass; its size would not.

A corporate facility to Mvuli Paa, secured on its portfolio and repaid from its whole cash flow, costs a fraction as much to arrange. A warehouse facility lends against systems as they are installed, using standardized contracts and eligibility criteria instead of contract-by-contract review, until the portfolio is large enough to refinance. Because the contracts pay in shillings, either loan should be in shillings or hedged. Once the portfolio holds thousands of standardized contracts, a securitization can sell their receivables to a vehicle that issues notes, with investors relying on the statistics of the pool (\cref{ssec:2.4.5}), as tower and fiber owners already do (\cref{ssec:82.7.2}).
\end{example}

A project can fail the other test, forecastable cash flow, at any size. Northvolt AB, a Swedish battery cell manufacturer founded in 2016, financed its first gigafactory, Northvolt Ett in Skellefteå, as a project. On July 28, 2020, the project company, Northvolt Ett AB, signed first-lien and second-lien facilities of approximately USD~1.6 billion, called the Snowball facilities; first-lien lenders are paid from the security before second-lien lenders. The lenders included the European Investment Bank, commercial banks, the Export-Import Bank of Korea, and lenders whose loans were covered by export credit agencies, government agencies that lend or insure to support their country's exports (\cref{ssec:4.3.2}), in Germany, Japan, and France. The facilities were secured on substantially all of Northvolt Ett AB's assets and on pledges of its shares; the project company had to hedge most of its interest-rate exposure and held a debt service reserve account (Millar 2024).

Northvolt Ett had no contracted revenue. Northvolt reported USD~55 billion of customer orders, but orders for battery cells are not take-or-pay obligations: a customer pays for cells delivered on specification and on time, and Northvolt Ett could not deliver them. The plant had been intended to be fully operational by 2023; in November 2024 it could make cells with a combined storage capacity of 16~GWh a year against a 60~GWh target, and the company relied on Chinese suppliers for some critical materials and machinery. Net losses widened from approximately USD~285 million in 2022 to USD~1.2 billion in 2023. An expansion package with approximately USD~4.5 billion of debt commitments, signed in December 2023, was never drawn and was unwound in November 2024. Northvolt filed for Chapter~11 in the Southern District of Texas on November 21, 2024, with approximately USD~30 million of cash, and its main Swedish companies filed for bankruptcy in Sweden on March 12, 2025 (Millar 2024; Northvolt 2025).

No project finance structure turns manufacturing ramp-up risk into contracted cash flow. A power plant's EPC contractor guarantees output and efficiency against a test it has passed many times before; a first-of-a-kind gigafactory's yield depends on learning that nobody can guarantee, as \cref{ch:77} shows in detail. A PPA pays for energy the plant can produce; a supply agreement for cells pays only for cells that meet the customer's specification.

\begin{framework}[label={fw:pf-fit-test}]{The project finance fit test}
Ask six questions in this order. The first two decide whether project finance is the right tool at all; the other four decide what it will cost to make it work.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Is the asset legally and physically separable from the sponsor's other businesses, with revenues of its own?
\item Is the revenue either contracted with a counterparty creditworthy enough to pay for longer than the debt will be outstanding, or predictable enough for lenders to forecast it for that long; and, whether contracted or merchant, is it earned in the currency of the debt or hedged into it?
\item Can construction risk be fixed in price and date with a creditworthy contractor?
\item Is the deal large enough to carry the fixed transaction costs? Compute the ratio of fixed costs to project cost and compare it with the benefit.
\item Can the sponsors accept the lenders' control and information demands for the life of the loan?
\item Can the host country's legal system give lenders enforceable security over the assets, the contracts, and the shares, and the right to step in?
\end{enumerate}
A ``no'' to question 1 or 2 means another financing tool. A ``no'' to questions 3 to 6 means a fix or a price: a sponsor guarantee, a different contract structure, a portfolio, a waiver, or a higher margin. Use the test at the first screen of any project, before anyone spends money on advisors.
\end{framework}

Llano Pardo passes the \term{project finance fit test} on every question: a separate plant on leased land with its own grid connection; a 20-year US-dollar PPA with a state utility, in a dollarized country, backed by a letter of credit; a fixed-price EPC contract with an experienced contractor; a cost of USD~65.1 million that carried its advisors' fees; sponsors who accepted the controls; and Corredanan security law that allowed pledges of the shares, the accounts, and the contracts and a mortgage of the land lease. The Nairobi rooftops pass questions 1, 2, 3, and 6, with shilling revenue matched by shilling debt, and fail question 4 by a margin no fee or price could close, so the fix is structural: a different tool until the portfolio grows. Northvolt Ett passed question 1 and the size test easily but strained question 3, because no contractor could fix a gigafactory's ramp-up, and question 2, because customer orders gave no take-or-pay protection once the plant could not deliver.

\section{Walkthrough: a board paper choosing between corporate debt and project finance}\label{sec:2.8}

A board decides how to fund a project from a short paper, and a good one fits on two pages. GLA's chief financial officer sent this one to the board on June 10, 2024, for its meeting on Thursday, June 20, drawing on \cref{ex:2.1,ex:2.3,ex:2.4}. Each step below describes what one section of the paper does. Replace the numbers and the same paper works for any sponsor.

\begin{enumerate}
\item State the decision and the deadline. The paper opens with one sentence asking the board to approve a limited recourse project financing for Alto Huelén of USD~292.2 million of senior debt plus a USD~29.2 million standby facility, USD~321.4 million committed in all, with GLA providing USD~97.4 million of equity and a cost-overrun undertaking capped at USD~29.2 million, 7.5\% of the cost. The second sentence gives the deadline: the turbine supplier's reservation fee falls due on July 15, 2024, and the supplier will release the turbine slots to another buyer if GLA has not committed by then. A director will ask what happens if the board decides nothing, so the paper says it first: the slots are lost, and the next delivery window delays commercial operation by about a year.

\item Present the leverage arithmetic, including the finding the board would rather not hear. The corporate route takes net debt to EBITDA to 4.94\x{} during construction and 4.02\x{} after, against a covenant of 3.50\x; project finance leaves the parent at 3.33\x. That 3.33\x{} is already a thin cushion: GLA's EBITDA would only have to fall 4.8\%, to USD~173.7 million ($608.0 / 3.50$), for the covenant to break, and a Chilean generator's EBITDA can move by more than that in an ordinary year with rainfall, which drives hydro output, and with spot prices. The lenders' first term sheet asked for the overrun undertaking of \cref{ex:2.1}, capped at USD~58.4 million, and a full call funded with parent debt would take leverage to 3.65\x. Even a cap of USD~29.2 million, 7.5\% of cost and just inside the USD~30.4 million the headroom allows, would leave USD~1.2 million to spare after a full call, a cushion that a 0.2\% fall in EBITDA would erase.

The paper therefore applies GLA's treasury policy, an illustrative one set by its board: in a joint stress, with the overrun cap called in full while EBITDA is down, EBITDA must still be able to fall about 5\% before the covenant breaks, and before any call the cushion must be about twice that. The board will propose to the 2025 ordinary shareholders' meeting a dividend of half the usual USD~67.2 million. The usual dividend is about 65\% of GLA's net profit of USD~103.4 million, so the halved USD~33.6 million is 32.5\% of net profit and stays above the minimum of 30\% that Chilean law requires an open corporation to distribute unless every share votes otherwise (Chile 1981). The paper drafts the message to investors and rating agencies that goes with it: a one-year retention to fund Alto Huelén, not a change of dividend policy. The retained cash brings parent net debt after the equity to $608.0 - 33.6 = $ USD~574.4 million, or 3.15\x, a cushion of 10.0\%. With the cap set at USD~29.2 million, a full call takes net debt to USD~603.6 million and leverage to 3.31\x, a breach only if EBITDA fell 5.5\%, to USD~172.5 million. Because the covenant is tested each quarter on trailing twelve-month EBITDA, the EBITDA of the most recent four quarters, and GLA's own capital spending and cash flow will move the headroom by more than these static figures show, the paper attaches a quarterly forecast of the covenant through construction. It records the alternatives it rejected: a letter of credit behind the undertaking keeps the call off GLA's balance sheet only until it is drawn; a covenant carve-out for sponsor-support payments would need a majority of GLA's own lenders; a share issue would take months and dilute shareholders.

Finally, the paper asks the lenders to provide the remaining USD~29.2 million of overrun funding themselves ($58.4 - 29.2$), through a standby facility inside the project financing, the buffer of \cref{ex:2.1} here sized to replace sponsor support. It is sized with the independent engineer so that the base loan and the fully drawn standby together still pass the lenders' cover-ratio and gearing tests (\cref{sec:31.7}). For the lenders the standby cuts both ways: it ensures the overrun is funded, but it is more senior debt, drawn exactly when the project is in trouble, so they treat it as a concession to the sponsor. The order of drawing (here, GLA's capped payment first, then the standby, after the budget contingency) is a negotiated term, and lenders often prefer the two drawn side by side in a fixed ratio. The standby will carry an upfront fee, about USD~0.66 million at the 2.25\% rate of \cref{ex:2.4}, a commitment fee, and possibly a higher margin. If the lenders refuse, the fallback is an equity-funded overrun reserve or a letter of credit, either of which uses the covenant headroom the cap was cut to save. \Cref{exh:2.3} carries the numbers.

\item Present the cost arithmetic. The paper states the cost of \cref{ex:2.4} in both units the board understands: about 104\bps{} a year more before the agency fee, or about USD~3.0 million a year, before the standby facility's fees, and about 11 months longer to first funds. The question it must answer is what GLA buys for USD~3 million a year, and the answer is in the steps on either side: covenant headroom, and a cap on what the wind farm can cost GLA.

\item Present the risk arithmetic. If a 14\% overrun is followed by failure before completion, the corporate route loses USD~444.1 million and the proposed project financing USD~126.6 million, GLA's equity plus its capped overrun payment ($97.4 + 29.2$); the standby facility funds the remaining USD~25.3 million of the overrun. If the standby is drawn, project debt rises to $292.2 + 25.3 = $ USD~317.5 million, which lowers the project's cover ratios and the distributions GLA can expect, and the paper says so. It explains why failure is not fanciful: the chosen turbine model has a record of gearbox replacements on other sites, the kind of technology risk a ring-fence exists to contain. It also states what the lenders are lending against: the 15-year US-dollar PPA of \cref{ex:2.1}, with GLA keeping curtailment risk, and the other 20\% of output sold at market prices that the lenders give little credit.

\item List what the company gives up: lender consents for any change to the wind farm or its contracts; quarterly reporting and compliance certificates; a distribution test that can trap cash in the project company after a poor wind year; the lenders' security over the wind farm and GLA's shares in it. A director will ask whether the lenders could stop GLA selling the wind farm. They could, through the change-of-control clause, unless the buyer meets criteria the term sheet sets, and the paper proposes those criteria.

\item State the accounting treatment. GLA will control the project company, so its group accounts will show the wind farm and its USD~292.2 million of debt (up to USD~321.4 million if the standby facility is drawn in full); the paper says that ``off balance sheet'' means outside the covenant calculation and, if the agencies agree, outside the rating (\cref{ssec:2.5.1}). It anticipates the comparison with Hyperion by noting that taking the wind farm out of the group accounts would require giving up control, which the board has not been asked to do.

\item Recommend, and say what would reverse the recommendation. The paper recommends project finance and lists the conditions under which the corporate route would be better: a share issue that restores covenant headroom; a material rise in project finance margins relative to GLA's bond spread, or a fall in GLA's own spread; or a rating agency that announces it will count the project debt as GLA's anyway. A director's last question is which of those is most likely and how GLA would know, and the paper answers it in one line: the likeliest is the rating agency, whose credit opinion after GLA's annual results is where any decision to add the project debt to GLA's leverage would appear, so that opinion is the trigger to revisit.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Case P: Kilnworth decides how to fund Bélanou}\label{sec:2.9}

Five and a half months after the phone call that ends \cref{ch:1}, Tomasz Wierzbicki presented Bélanou to Kilnworth Power International's investment committee in London on Thursday, September 17, 2015. In June he had signed a co-development agreement with Groupe Talmé, the Dabakro family conglomerate whose deputy chief executive, Mariama Talmé, had made that call. Its second phase, in which Kilnworth would pay 70\% of development costs and Groupe Talmé 30\%, could begin only if this committee approved a full development budget by September 30. The two partners' shares in the project would follow the same split. Kessara had announced a competitive tender for a 450~MW to 650~MW gas-fired plant at Bélanou in March, under its Emergency Power Plan; the public-private partnership (PPP) unit in Kessara's finance ministry, which would run the tender, had not yet begun the procurement (\cref{ssec:57.6.1}).

\begin{casescene}{Case P}{Kilnworth Power International, London, September 2015}
Tomasz had reached the third slide, the site plan, when Niall Brannigan stopped him.

``Before the plant. Why are we paying bankers to finance it?'' Niall had built more of Kilnworth's fleet than anyone else in the room. ``We've four-point-one gigawatts running. One more plant goes on the balance sheet, we fund it off the revolver and a bond, and we're building a year earlier.''

Devesh Raval had the rating agency's latest report on Kilnworth open in front of him. ``A year earlier, and a downgrade later. We're triple-B-minus. One notch from junk, Niall.''

``We're one notch from junk with or without Kessara.''

``Show me the downside first.'' Devesh turned to Tomasz. ``The Kessaran government stops paying the utility's bills. What happens to Kilnworth?''

``The tariff is in dollars,'' Tomasz said. ``The government guarantees SEKA's payments under the PPA. If SEKA doesn't pay, the guarantee pays. The lenders will want the PPA and the guarantee, and once they have them they'll be comfortable.''

``Pays in what?''

``In dollars. It's a dollar tariff.'' Tomasz moved to the next slide. ``If we're worried about the guarantee, the question is what it costs us per kilowatt-month to cover it, and I can get you that number.''

Devesh let it go, and wrote something down. ``Then non-recourse. If Kessara goes wrong, we lose our equity and nothing else.''

``And a year to the lawyers,'' Niall said.

``We lose the year anyway,'' Tomasz said. ``The tender won't be out before next year. The lawyers can work while the PPP unit writes the documents.''

Philippa Carrow, chairing, cut across them. ``What would make us walk away?''

Tomasz had the list ready: a tender that tilted toward the state utility's own plants, a gas field that slipped, a tariff below the committee's return. Philippa heard him out and went to the budget slide.

``Twelve million for development. That gets you to a bid.''

``It gets us to a bid,'' Tomasz said. ``It doesn't get us to close. The gas studies, the environmental and resettlement work, two sets of lawyers, the bid security. Fourteen-point-eight.''

``Fourteen-point-eight, and you come back to this committee before you commit the last two million.''

``Agreed.''

Devesh was still writing. ``Seventy-five percent debt as the target. Project level. Nothing guaranteed by us beyond what the lenders force.''

The committee approved both.
\end{casescene}

Niall's ``revolver'' was Kilnworth's revolving corporate credit line, which it could draw and repay at will, and Tomasz's ``per kilowatt-month'' is the unit in which capacity payments for gas plants are usually quoted (\cref{ch:18}). The approval set a target gearing of 75\%, meaning debt of 75\% of the total funding (\cref{ssec:8.2.1}), and a development budget of USD~14.8 million, up from the USD~12 million Philippa Carrow first proposed, a budget whose fate \cref{ch:4} follows through development.

Run the project finance fit test (\cref{fw:pf-fit-test}) on Bélanou as it stood that September, and the decision holds, but with three conditions, ranked by how much each could hurt the deal. The largest sat inside question 2. The revenue would come from a PPA with SEKA, the state utility, backed by a government guarantee, and its value depended on two things the committee could not yet see: whether SEKA and the Republic could pay, and whether they could pay in dollars that Kessara's central bank would let leave the country. A dollar tariff answers the second question only if dollars are available to convert into; Devesh's ``Pays in what?'' was the right question, and the committee let Tomasz's answer about the tariff's currency stand in for an answer about convertibility (\cref{ch:59}). Next came security: Kessara's civil-law system recognizes pledges of shares, receivables, and bank accounts and a pledge of a business as a going concern, but not the trust. A trust is the common-law device that lets one security agent hold security for every lender, so lenders can change without the security being retaken. Without it, the lenders would need the substitute mechanics of \cref{ch:52}. Construction risk was the smallest of the three: a combined-cycle plant of this size is a proven technology that experienced contractors build under fixed-price, date-certain EPC contracts, though the price would have to be found in the market.

The other three answers were clean. Bélanou would be a new plant on a government-allocated site, owned by a new Kessaran project company with its own grid connection and its own gas supply point. A plant of several hundred megawatts spreads its transaction costs over a cost base dozens of times larger than the Nairobi portfolio's, so the ratio would fall to a few percent, the range Alto Huelén showed. And Kilnworth wanted non-recourse debt and accepted the consents and reporting that come with it.

\section{Practitioner's notebook}\label{sec:2.10}

\subsection*{Checklist}
Before accepting that a structure is ring-fenced, confirm each of the following in the documents, not in the term sheet.
\begin{itemize}
\item The project company's constitutional documents limit its objects to the project, so that a later board cannot widen its business without amending them.
\item The project company has given no guarantee or security for any affiliate's obligations, because one such guarantee lets the affiliate's creditors reach the project's cash.
\item The project company holds its own bank accounts in its own name, with no pooling or sweeping into a group treasury, because commingled cash is the first thing a sponsor's insolvency administrator will claim.
\item Where the host country's law or the lenders require it, an independent director or a list of reserved matters prevents the sponsors from filing the project company for insolvency or changing its business on their own.
\item Every contract with an affiliate (EPC, O\&M, management services, fuel) is in writing, on terms the lenders' advisors have tested against the market.
\item Every sponsor support document is listed, with its cap, its trigger, and its expiry, and the total fits inside what the sponsors' boards approved and what their own covenants allow.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Red flags}
\begin{itemize}
\item An affiliate EPC or O\&M contract with weak liquidated damages or a low liability cap, because the sponsor is then on both sides of the risk and has no reason to enforce it.
\item A sponsor that needs the project's distributions to service its own debt, because it will press for distributions the lenders should trap and will resist every reserve.
\item ``Non-recourse'' debt accompanied by a long list of sponsor undertakings, because together they can add up to recourse; run the recourse ladder before agreeing.
\item A sponsor-support cap larger than the sponsor's covenant headroom, because a call would put the sponsor in breach at the moment the project most needs it to pay.
\item A cross-default from the project company's debt into the sponsor's corporate facilities, because the sponsor's lenders have then made the project's failure their default, and the sponsor will negotiate any project problem with its own lenders watching.
\item Revenue described by order volumes or memoranda of understanding instead of by what the buyer must pay if the plant underperforms; if the answer is nothing, the revenue is not contracted in the sense the fit test requires.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Rules of thumb and their limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item Transaction costs above about 5\% of capital cost make project finance hard to justify. The limit: the threshold comes from the examples in this chapter, not from market data, and fixed costs vary with jurisdiction, lender type, and how standardized the documents are; a government program with standard contracts can cut them sharply.
\item Sponsor support should end at completion. The limit: technology risk on a first-of-a-kind plant or resource risk on a field with little production history can justify support that runs into operations, such as a guarantee that falls away only after two or three years of proven output.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Common mistakes}
\begin{itemize}
\item Confusing off-balance-sheet with off the consolidated accounts. A controlled project company's debt appears in the group accounts; what it can stay out of is the covenant calculation and, sometimes, the rating.
\item Treating the risk allocation principle as ``give the risk to whoever will sign for it.'' A party that cannot control, price, or pay for a risk returns the loss to the project company as soon as the loss exceeds what it can pay.
\item Ignoring a counterparty's capacity to absorb the risk it controls, as in \cref{ex:2.2}: allocate up to what the party can pay, and decide in advance who carries the rest.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Questions experts ask}
\begin{itemize}
\item ``If the sponsor went bankrupt tomorrow, what would the project company stop receiving?'' The answer lists every service, contract, and right that crosses the ring-fence.
\item ``Which contract replaces the balance sheet for this risk?'' If no contract does, the risk sits with the project company and the sponsors' equity.
\item ``What does the sponsor lose if this fails, in dollars, before and after completion, and can it pay that without breaching its own covenants?'' The answer is the sponsor's two numbers from the recourse ladder, checked against its headroom.
\end{itemize}

\section{Judgment drill}\label{sec:2.11}

\subsection*{The situation}
You are GLA's chief financial officer, in Santiago, on Monday, June 17, 2024. The board meets on Thursday to decide how to fund the Alto Huelén wind farm, and your paper (\cref{sec:2.8}) recommends project finance with a USD~29.2 million overrun cap. This morning your relationship bank, which leads GLA's syndicated corporate facilities, has made a new offer: a corporate term loan of USD~389.6 million at a margin of 160\bps, and an offer to lead a request to GLA's lending syndicate to relax the net debt to EBITDA covenant from 3.50\x{} to 4.25\x{} for three years, for a waiver fee of 0.25\% of the loan. The relationship manager says the offer is good until Friday. The inputs are \cref{ex:2.1,ex:2.3,ex:2.4}. Do you change your recommendation?

\subsection*{Reasoning it through}
Start with whether the offer works as stated. During construction the corporate route takes leverage to 4.94\x, above even the relaxed 4.25\x, so the offer fails its first test unless the covenant is lifted further, GLA raises equity, or GLA phases the spending so that leverage peaks lower, and none of those is in the offer. The relaxation itself is not the bank's to give: a syndicated facility's covenant changes need the consent of a majority of its lenders, often two-thirds by commitment, so nothing will be certain by Friday. After commercial operation leverage would be 4.02\x, inside 4.25\x{} but with only $4.25 \times 223.7 - 900.2 = $ USD~50.5 million of headroom, and the relaxation expires after three years, when GLA must be back under 3.50\x{} or ask again. GLA would have spent its borrowing capacity on one wind farm, with nothing left for the next project.

Then the price. The bank's all-in cost is the 160\bps{} margin plus the waiver fee of $0.0025 \times 389.6 = $ USD~0.97 million, which over a 9.4-year average life is $0.974 / (389.6 \times 9.4) = 2.7$\bps{} a year: about 163\bps. Project finance costs about 248\bps{} (\cref{ex:2.4}), before the standby facility's fees. The corporate offer is cheaper by about 85\bps{} a year, roughly USD~2.5 million on USD~292.2 million, or 1.4\% of GLA's EBITDA, and about 11 months faster.

Then the risk. The chosen turbine model has a record of gearbox replacements elsewhere. If the wind farm fails, the corporate route leaves GLA owing USD~389.6 million or more against no asset, at leverage of 4.94\x{} with a covenant relaxation that ends. Project finance also uses up GLA's construction-period headroom: at the lenders' first ask the overrun support alone would have breached the covenant, and the recommended structure needs a halved 2025 dividend, proposed to the shareholders, to keep a 5.5\% EBITDA cushion after a full call. But that use of headroom is capped at USD~29.2 million and temporary: at completion the cap falls away and GLA's capacity for the next project returns, while under the corporate route the whole USD~389.6 million stays on GLA's balance sheet. The ring-fence is worth most where the asset carries a technology or resource risk the parent cannot afford, and this asset does.

An experienced chief financial officer keeps the recommendation for project finance on this asset and uses the offer to sharpen the board paper. The paper should tell the board that the corporate offer is cheaper by about USD~2.5 million a year, depends on a syndicate vote, and still fails the construction-period covenant, so that the board sees the trade-off and is not surprised later. The relationship bank can be kept close by offering it a role as one of the arrangers of the project loan, where its balance sheet earns fees on this deal without stretching GLA's covenants.

\subsection*{What would change the answer}
\begin{itemize}
\item A share issue that restores headroom. If GLA raised enough equity to keep corporate leverage under the covenant through construction, the cheaper and faster corporate route would win on any asset without unusual technology risk.
\item A project that fits easily within existing headroom. If the wind farm's cost fitted within GLA's USD~127.8 million of headroom with room for the next project, and its failure would not move GLA's rating, risk isolation would be worth little and the fixed costs of project finance would weigh more.
\item A market where project finance terms are unavailable. If banks were not lending to Chilean wind on non-recourse terms at an acceptable margin, the comparison would collapse to corporate debt or equity.
\item A rating agency that counts the project debt anyway. If the agencies treated the USD~292.2 million as GLA's debt, project finance would keep the covenant benefit but lose the rating benefit, and the case for paying 85\bps{} more would weaken.
\end{itemize}

\section{The structure is old; its rules were learned one failure at a time}\label{sec:2.12}

A project financing replaces the borrower a lender would normally study with substitutes: a company that does nothing but the project, a ring-fence of promises that keeps other creditors out and caps what the sponsors can lose, and a web of contracts that does the work a balance sheet would do. Each substitute is only as good as the parties behind its promises, which is why every risk has to pass the tests of control, pricing, and capacity before it is placed, and why an investment-grade sponsor pays a price in margin and time for the result. Where the cash flows cannot be pinned down, or the deal is too small to carry the fixed costs, the substitutes cost more than they protect.

None of those rules was settled when the Channel Tunnel's banks underwrote it. On February 12, 1986, the British and French governments agreed in the Treaty of Canterbury that the Channel Tunnel would be financed without government funds or government guarantees (Grant 1997). In 1987 a group of approximately 50 banks underwrote a GBP~5~billion facility on the strength of forecasts of traffic through a tunnel that did not yet exist. By the time the tunnel opened in 1994 its debt was approximately GBP~8~billion, and in September 1995 the company suspended interest payments, leaving the lenders of a ring-fenced, contract-backed structure to decide what their security over a working tunnel with too little traffic was worth (\cref{ch:3}).

\section{Solutions to exercises}\label{sec:2.14}

\subsection*{Tier 1: Concept checks}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.1}
Project finance is financing in which lenders are repaid from the cash flows of a single ring-fenced project and secured on its assets and contracts, with limited or no recourse to the sponsors. Its three features are therefore repayment from one project's cash flows, security over that project's assets and contracts, and limited or no recourse to the owners. Check: each feature changes the lender's work (\cref{ssec:2.1.1}); a definition that omits recourse describes asset finance, and one that omits the single project describes corporate secured lending.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.2}
The first direction protects the sponsors from the project: if Llano Pardo had failed, Sterrenberg Bank and the ABDB could have enforced only against the plant, its contracts, its accounts, and the pledged shares, so Tallisford and Grupo Arismendi could lose no more than their USD~16.3 million of equity. The second direction protects the project's lenders from the sponsors: if Grupo Arismendi's agribusiness had failed, its creditors could have claimed its 40\% shareholding, but only subject to the lenders' pledge, and could not reach the plant, the PPA receivables, or the project accounts. Check against \cref{exh:2.1}: each dashed arrow is one direction.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.3}
The tests are control (who can change the probability or size of the loss), pricing (who can estimate and charge for the risk with least uncertainty), and capacity to absorb (who can pay the loss without failing). Soiling at Llano Pardo is one case where they diverge: control and pricing point to the O\&M operator, but a loss of the order of its USD~0.41 million annual fee exceeds its capacity, so the O\&M operator should carry it up to a cap and the sponsors above it (\cref{ex:2.2}). Ground conditions on a tunnel are another: the tunneling contractor controls the tunneling method, the host government may hold the geological data, and no one controls the rock, so the risk is often shared through a geotechnical baseline report, which describes the ground the contractor prices for; worse ground is the owner's risk (\cref{ssec:72.3.2}).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.4}
Llano Pardo's loan was non-recourse because, from financial close, the sponsors owed only their committed equity of USD~16.3 million; once that was paid in, the lenders could look only to the project company. Nothing in the term sheet obliged the sponsors to fund overruns, guarantee the loan, or complete the plant. The loan would have been limited recourse if the term sheet had required any capped, time-limited support beyond the equity: for example, a completion guarantee of the USD~48.8 million loan until financial completion, or a cost-overrun undertaking capped at a share of the EPC price and released at completion. Check: limited recourse needs all three of a trigger, a cap, and a release date (\cref{ssec:2.2.2}).
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.5}
Against securitization, the distinguishing feature is reliance on a few large, individually examined contracts and an operating asset, rather than on the statistics of a pool of many small receivables. Against reserve-based lending, it is that project debt is sized once and repaid on a fixed schedule from one ring-fenced project, usually without recourse, whereas a reserve-based loan's available amount is redetermined periodically from the value of several fields and often has recourse to the producer.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.8}
Before completion: full recourse USD~444.1 million, completion guarantee USD~444.1 million, capped undertaking USD~151.9 million, non-recourse USD~97.4 million. After completion: USD~444.1 million, USD~151.9 million, USD~151.9 million, and USD~97.4 million.

The overrun is $0.14 \times 389.6 = 54.5$ and the cap $0.15 \times 389.6 = 58.4$, so the overrun is inside the cap. Full recourse: $389.6 + 54.5 = 444.1$ at any date, because GLA owes what it borrowed. Completion guarantee: before completion GLA loses equity, overrun, and guaranteed debt, $97.4 + 54.5 + 292.2 = 444.1$; after completion the guarantee is released and it loses only what it put in, $97.4 + 54.5 = 151.9$. Capped undertaking: $97.4 + 54.5 = 151.9$ both before and after. Non-recourse: 97.4, because GLA need not fund the overrun.

Completion releases the contingent part of the support, the promise to fund future overruns, not money already contributed. Had no overrun occurred, the capped undertaking's live exposure would have been $97.4 + 58.4 = 155.8$ from signing to completion and 97.4 afterward. The most common wrong answer gives 97.4 after completion on the capped rung even though the overrun was funded; release at completion cannot return the USD~54.5 million already spent.
\end{solution}

& 13.83/(292.2 \times 9.4) = 13.83/2{,}746.7 = 0.504\% = 50.4\text{ bps}\\
\text{Bond costs:}\quad & 0.0045 \times 389.6 + 0.60 = 1.75 + 0.60 = 2.35\\
& 2.35/(389.6 \times 9.4) = 2.35/3{,}662.2 = 0.064\% = 6.4\text{ bps}\\
\text{Difference:}\quad & (195 + 50.4) - (135 + 6.4) = 245.4 - 141.4 = 104.0\text{ bps}\\
& 0.0104 \times 292.2 = 3.04 \quad \text{(USD m a year)}
\end{align*}
Check: with the agency fee of 2.6\bps, the difference is 106.6\bps, USD~3.1 million a year. The most common wrong answer divides the project finance costs by the full USD~389.6 million instead of the USD~292.2 million of project debt, giving 37.8\bps{} and understating the cost by a quarter.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.10}
A model answer, drafted under English law to match the governing law of the Llano Pardo loan agreement:
\begin{clause}{Single-purpose and separateness undertaking, Llano Pardo common terms agreement \illustrative}\label{cl:2.2}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Business.} The Borrower shall not carry on any business other than: (i) the development, construction, ownership, operation and maintenance of the 50.0 MWac photovoltaic generating facility at Llano Pardo and the sale of its output to ELNACOR under the PPA; (ii) the construction of the Grid Connection Assets and their transfer to ELNACOR in accordance with the PPA; and (iii) the financing of those activities under the Finance Documents.
\item \emph{Financial Indebtedness, guarantees and disposals.} The Borrower shall not incur any Financial Indebtedness other than Permitted Financial Indebtedness, give any guarantee or indemnity for the obligations of any person, or dispose of any asset other than in the ordinary course of operating the Project, except that the transfer of the Grid Connection Assets to ELNACOR on or about the Commercial Operation Date is permitted.
\item \emph{Separateness and Sponsor Group transactions.} The Borrower shall maintain its own books, records and bank accounts, shall not commingle its funds with those of any member of a Sponsor Group, and shall not enter into any contract or arrangement with a member of a Sponsor Group (including any agribusiness, logistics or land-holding company in the Grupo Arismendi group) unless it is in writing, on arm's length terms, and, where its value exceeds USD 50,000 in any Financial Year, approved in advance by the Facility Agent.
\item \emph{Corporate structure.} The Borrower shall not enter into any merger, amalgamation or corporate reconstruction, acquire any interest in any company or business, or establish any Subsidiary.
\end{enumerate}
\end{clause}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Paragraph (a). Sub-paragraph (ii) makes the grid connection part of the permitted business, so building the 14~km line and substation for transfer cannot be argued to breach the single-purpose undertaking.
\item Paragraph (b). The transfer of the Grid Connection Assets is the one disposal of a major asset the deal requires; naming it avoids a waiver request at the commercial operation date. Lenders would also want the PPA to oblige ELNACOR to accept the transfer and maintain the line.
\item Paragraph (c). ``Sponsor Group'' must be defined to include every company each sponsor controls, so that Grupo Arismendi's trucks, its land interests and its farming companies are caught. The threshold is lower than in \cref{cl:2.1} because the whole operating budget is about USD~1.1 million a year.
\item Paragraph (d). Llano Pardo needs no subsidiary because the grid assets are transferred, not held, so the prohibition can be absolute.
\end{enumerate}
The most common weak answer copies \cref{cl:2.1}, which would make the grid transfer a technical breach of paragraph (b) and leave Grupo Arismendi's group companies outside a narrowly defined ``Affiliate.''
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.11}
Do not arrange a stand-alone project financing now; arrange a shilling corporate or warehouse facility and revisit a securitization once the portfolio holds thousands of standardized contracts. On the fit test, question 1 (separable, own revenues) passes, since each system has its own contract. Question 2 passes for a shilling loan: the building owners are established businesses, Nairobi's solar resource is predictable, and the contracts pay in Kenyan shillings, so the debt must be in shillings or hedged. Question 3 (construction fixed) passes, since the systems are built. Question 4 (size) fails: fixed costs are $2.9/18.6 = 15.6\%$ of cost against 3.55\% on Alto Huelén. Question 5 (control) is acceptable. Question 6 (security under Kenyan law) passes in principle, though registering the lenders' security over 214 separate roof leases, the step that makes it effective against third parties (\cref{ssec:52.3.1}), is costly. The structure: a corporate facility to Mvuli Paa secured on the portfolio, or a warehouse facility advancing a fixed share of each new system's cost against standardized contracts and eligibility criteria. Revisit once the fixed costs of a term financing or securitization fall to a few percent of the amount raised, which on this cost base means a portfolio many times today's USD~18.6 million. The most common wrong answer prices the problem away with a higher margin. Spread over a 9.4-year average life, USD~2.9 million on a 75\% loan of USD~14.0 million adds $2.9 / (0.75 \times 18.6 \times 9.4) = 2.2\%$, about 220\bps{} a year before any credit margin, more than the whole margin on Alto Huelén.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.13}
A strong set of five questions, each tied to Kilnworth's balance sheet:
\begin{enumerate}
\item ``If SEKA stops paying, what do we lose, in dollars, before and after completion?'' The answer fixes Kilnworth's place on the recourse ladder and therefore its rating exposure.
\item ``Which sponsor support will the lenders ask for, with what cap and release date?'' A completion guarantee on a plant this size could exceed what a BBB- balance sheet can carry.
\item ``Who guarantees SEKA's payments, and can the guarantor pay in dollars that can leave Kessara?'' A dollar tariff is worth only what the central bank allows to be converted and transferred.
\item ``What happens to the USD~14.8 million if we lose the tender?'' The development budget is written off in full if the bid fails, and the committee should approve it as money at risk.
\item ``Will the project company be consolidated in our accounts, and can we deconsolidate later?'' As the 70\% partner Kilnworth would consolidate the project company and its debt, which matters for the group's reported leverage even if the rating agencies exclude the debt.
\end{enumerate}
The committee failed to pursue question 3. Devesh asked ``Pays in what?'' and accepted Tomasz's answer about the currency of the tariff, which addresses denomination, not convertibility or transfer (\cref{ch:59}). The most common weak answer asks only about the tariff and returns, which are the sponsor's upside questions; a chief financial officer's job at this meeting is the downside.
\end{solution}

\begin{solution}{exr:2.14}
B18 = 4.94\x; B19 = 4.02\x; B20 = 292.2; B21 = 97.4; B22 = 3.33\x; B23 = 50.4\bps; B24 = 6.4\bps; B25 = 103.9\bps; B26 = 257.2; B27 = 215.9; B28 = 33.5 (USD m where not stated). B25 shows 103.9 rather than 104.0 because the spreadsheet does not round B23 and B24 before subtracting ($245.37 - 141.43 = 103.94$).

Steps for the threshold: under the corporate route, net debt after the commercial operation date is USD~900.2 million. For net debt to EBITDA to equal 3.50\x, total EBITDA must be $900.2/3.50 = 257.2$. The wind farm contributes 41.3, so the parent's other businesses must earn $257.2 - 41.3 = 215.9$, which is $215.9 - 182.4 = 33.5$ more than today. B26 to B28 tell the board that the corporate route works after commercial operation only if the rest of GLA grows its EBITDA by USD~33.5 million, about 18\%, and that even then the construction period remains in breach, since B18 is measured before the wind farm earns anything. \Cref{ch:13} shows how to find the same threshold with a data table or goal seek.

Check: $257.2 \times 3.50 = 900.2$. The most common wrong answer is $3.50 \times 182.4 - 510.6 = 127.8$, which is headroom measured in debt, not the EBITDA at which the corporate route stops breaching.
\end{solution}
