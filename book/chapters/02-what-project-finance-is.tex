\chapter{What project finance is, and when to use it}\label{ch:2}

On April 21, 2016, SunEdison, Inc.\ filed for Chapter~11, the US bankruptcy procedure under which a company keeps operating while a court supervises the restructuring of its debts. Seven months earlier its consolidated balance sheet, the statement of what a company and every company it controls own and owe (\cref{ssec:7.4.1}), had shown total liabilities of USD~16.1~billion, of which USD~11.7~billion was debt (SunEdison 2015). SunEdison filed with commitments for up to USD~300~million of new financing to keep its business running in bankruptcy (SunEdison 2016).

Two companies that SunEdison controlled were missing from the list of debtors. TerraForm Power and TerraForm Global were listed on NASDAQ and owned operating wind and solar plants, many of them built or bought by SunEdison. About USD~3.8~billion of SunEdison's consolidated debt had been borrowed by the two TerraForm companies and the projects beneath them. Much of the rest had been borrowed by project companies and warehouse vehicles (companies that hold new projects until they are sold or refinanced) that SunEdison owned directly, without recourse to SunEdison itself (SunEdison 2015). Lenders to those borrowers could look only to them and to their projects. Neither TerraForm company was pulled into the bankruptcy.

Their governance was pulled in anyway. On November 20, 2015, five months before its own filing, SunEdison had used its voting control of TerraForm Power to remove that company's chief executive and chief financial officer (TerraForm Power 2016).

The Llano Pardo deal of \cref{ch:1} used the same structure and named its parts in passing: a company that owned one plant, lenders who could look only to that plant, and owners who could lose only what they had put in. The TerraForm companies had all three, and their sponsor's bankruptcy still reached them.

\section{The project company and its ring-fence}\label{sec:2.1}

\subsection{A company with one asset and no history}\label{ssec:2.1.1}

When Sterrenberg Bank's credit committee approved its half of the Llano Pardo loan in 2016, Llano Pardo Solar SA had no audited accounts, no revenue, no employees of note, and no track record. It had been incorporated in Corredana for one purpose: to build and own a 50~MW solar plant on 152 hectares of leased plateau and sell its output to the state utility, ELNACOR, for 20 years. A lender to an established company would have asked how the borrower had performed over the past five years. The committee could only ask what the plant would do over the next 20, and who had promised what.

Lending against what an asset will do and who has promised what, instead of against a borrower's history, is what defines the discipline. \term{Project finance} is financing in which lenders are repaid from the cash flows of a single ring-fenced project and secured on its assets and contracts, with limited or no recourse to the sponsors. Each of its three features changes how a lender works. Because repayment comes from one project's cash flows, the lender studies the project, not the owner: its output, its revenue contract, its costs. Because the security is the project's assets and contracts, the lender's protection on default is the right to take over a working plant together with the contracts that make it earn money. Because recourse to the owners is limited or absent, the lender cannot fall back on a parent company's balance sheet when the forecasts prove wrong.

The legal device that makes all three features possible is the \term{special purpose vehicle} (SPV): a company created to own and operate one project and to do nothing else, so that the project's assets, contracts, and debts are held apart from its owners'. Its legal form follows the host country's company law. Llano Pardo Solar is a Corredanan \emph{sociedad anónima} (SA). Case P's Bélanou Power is a Kessaran \emph{société anonyme}, also SA. In the United States the usual form is a limited liability company: Sabine Pass Liquefaction, LLC, which borrowed to build the first plant to export liquefied natural gas (LNG) from the lower 48 states (\cref{ssec:2.3.3}), is one. In England it is a private limited company. Whatever the form, the company's liability must be separate from its shareholders', its shares must be capable of being pledged to lenders, and it must be able to hold the licenses and sign the contracts the project needs. Once it holds a project, the SPV is the \term{project company}: the special purpose vehicle that owns the project, signs its contracts, and borrows its debt.

The owners of the project company are its sponsors. A \term{sponsor} is a company that develops a project and owns equity in its project company, carrying the first loss. Llano Pardo had two: Tallisford Energy Partners, a London fund manager, with 60\%, and Grupo Arismendi, a Corredanan family group, with 40\%. The sponsors' equity is paid in before the lenders lose a dollar and is repaid only after the lenders have received what is due, so the sponsors absorb any shortfall first. \Cref{ch:4} sorts sponsors into strategic, financial, and developer types; the definition here needs only that first-loss position.

Grupo Arismendi already had companies that could have signed the power purchase agreement (PPA) with ELNACOR, and every party at the table had a reason to want a new one instead. The lenders wanted a borrower whose only assets and liabilities were the project's, so that the cash they forecast would be the cash they received and no unrelated creditor would stand beside them. The sponsors wanted their other businesses protected if the plant failed, and they wanted to be able to sell the project later by selling shares instead of transferring dozens of contracts and licenses one by one. Tallisford, a fund with investors of its own, needed a holding it could value and eventually sell. ELNACOR and the Ministry of Energy and Mines wanted a counterparty whose sole business was delivering the plant they had tendered for, so that its obligations under the PPA would be easier to enforce and the plant easier to keep running if a sponsor failed.

\subsection{What the ring-fence keeps in and keeps out}\label{ssec:2.1.2}

A new company is not enough on its own. Nothing in Corredanan company law stops Llano Pardo Solar SA from borrowing more money, guaranteeing a sponsor's bank loan, buying a second site, or paying its cash into an account Grupo Arismendi also uses. Each of those acts would let other creditors or other businesses reach the cash that the lenders sized their loan against, so the finance documents add a set of promises that close those routes.

\term{Ring-fencing} is the legal and contractual separation that keeps a project's assets and cash flows apart from its sponsors' other businesses, protecting each from the other's failure. It works in two directions. A lender that checks only that it cannot be made to chase the sponsors can forget to check that the sponsors' creditors cannot reach the project.

The first direction protects the sponsors from the project. If Llano Pardo had failed, Sterrenberg Bank and the Atlantic Basin Development Bank (ABDB) could have enforced their security over the plant, its contracts, its accounts, and the shares in the project company, and nothing else. Tallisford's other funds and Grupo Arismendi's agribusiness were beyond their reach. The sponsors' maximum loss was the equity they had put in.

The second direction protects the project's lenders from the sponsors. If Grupo Arismendi's agribusiness had collapsed in 2020, its creditors could have claimed its assets, including its 40\% shareholding in Llano Pardo Solar SA. Those shares were already pledged to the project lenders, so the agribusiness creditors would have taken them subject to the pledge, and the value they could extract was the shares' value after the project debt: in practice, the right to future distributions once the lenders had been paid. They could not seize the plant, the PPA receivables (the money ELNACOR owed for power already delivered), or the cash in the project accounts, because none of those belonged to Grupo Arismendi. \Cref{exh:2.1} draws both directions on Llano Pardo's parties.

The promises that build the ring-fence usually sit together in the covenant section of the common terms agreement, the master finance document that all the senior lenders sign (\cref{ch:51}). The \term{single-purpose undertaking} is a covenant by the project company to carry on no business other than the project. A \term{separateness undertaking} is a covenant by the project company to keep its own books, bank accounts, and decisions separate from its owners' and to deal with them only on arm's-length terms. A limit on other financial debt and on guarantees keeps new creditors from standing beside the lenders, and a ban on mergers, acquisitions, and subsidiaries stops the project company from becoming something else.

Sponsors and lenders usually add one more layer. Each sponsor holds its shares through a single-purpose holding company of its own (\cref{ssec:67.2.1}), so the lenders can take their share pledge over a clean entity and, on default, enforce it by selling the shares, while the sponsor can sell or borrow against its stake at that level without touching the project company (\cref{ssec:31.3.1}).

\Cref{cl:2.1} shows how the promises read in a contract. It is drafted under English law for the common terms agreement of a project that the rest of this chapter follows: Parque Eólico Alto Huelén, a 240~MW wind farm in Chile's Biobío region that Generadora Litoral Andino SA (GLA), a generator listed on the Santiago Stock Exchange, was preparing to finance in 2024. Words with capital letters, such as ``Borrower'' and ``Project Documents,'' are defined terms: a definitions clause at the front of the agreement gives each one an exact meaning, and \cref{ch:10} and \cref{ch:51} teach the convention.

\begin{clause}{Single-purpose and separateness undertaking, common terms agreement \illustrative}\label{cl:2.1}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Business.} The Borrower shall not carry on any business other than the development, ownership, construction, operation, maintenance and financing of the Project in accordance with the Project Documents, and activities reasonably incidental to it.
\item \emph{Financial Indebtedness and guarantees.} The Borrower shall not incur or permit to subsist any Financial Indebtedness, or give any guarantee or indemnity in respect of the obligations of any person, other than Permitted Financial Indebtedness.
\item \emph{Separateness.} The Borrower shall: (i) maintain its own books, records and bank accounts, separate from those of any Sponsor or Affiliate; (ii) not commingle its funds with those of any other person; (iii) hold itself out as a separate legal entity and conduct business in its own name; and (iv) enter into any transaction with a Sponsor or Affiliate only on arm's length terms, in writing, and, where the value of the transaction exceeds USD 250,000 in any Financial Year, with the prior consent of the Facility Agent.
\item \emph{Corporate structure.} The Borrower shall not enter into any amalgamation, demerger, merger or corporate reconstruction, acquire any company, business or undertaking, or establish or acquire any Subsidiary.
\end{enumerate}
\end{clause}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Paragraph (a). ``Project Documents'' is the defined list of the project's contracts. The words ``reasonably incidental'' carry the negotiation: sponsors want them wide enough to cover grid services or a co-located battery without asking; lenders want them narrow, because each new activity brings risks the model did not test. In Latin American renewable financings closed 2020 to 2025 the usual landing was a narrow clause plus a consent process (indicative).
\item Paragraph (b). An indemnity is a promise to make good another person's loss. ``Permitted Financial Indebtedness'' is a defined list, typically the senior facilities, hedging under the hedging policy, shareholder loans that are subordinated (repaid only after the senior lenders have been paid), and a small working-capital basket, meaning a capped amount allowed as an exception. The guarantee ban protects the second direction of the ring-fence: without it, a sponsor could ask the project company to guarantee the sponsor's own corporate loan.
\item Paragraph (c). An ``Affiliate'' is any company in the same group as a Sponsor, and the ``Facility Agent'' is the bank that administers the loan for all the lenders. Separateness is what persuades a court in a sponsor's insolvency that the project company is a different person. A project company that shared bank accounts with its parent, paid the parent's bills, and let the parent sign for it invites a creditor's argument that the two should be treated as one. The consent threshold in sub-paragraph (iv) aims at a common way value leaks out of a ring-fenced project: an engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) or operations and maintenance (O\&M) contract with a sponsor's affiliate on soft terms.
\item Paragraph (d). A project company that merged with another company would import that company's creditors. A ``Subsidiary'' is a company the Borrower controls. Lenders sometimes allow one for a specific purpose, such as holding a transmission line that must later be transferred to a utility, and list it as an exception.
\end{enumerate}

Whether the ring-fence holds in the end depends on the insolvency law of the sponsor's country, not on the clause. US bankruptcy courts can, in rare cases, order the substantive consolidation of a parent and a subsidiary that were run as one business: the court treats the two as a single debtor, so the creditors of each can reach the assets of both. English courts almost never disregard a company's separate personality; the Supreme Court confined that power to cases where a company is used to evade an obligation its controller already owes (\emph{Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd} 2013). French law reaches results close to substantive consolidation by extending a parent's insolvency proceedings to a subsidiary whose assets were commingled with the parent's. In each system the clause matters less than the conduct behind it (\cref{ch:64} follows a ring-fenced project through its sponsor's insolvency): a project company that has actually kept its own accounts, signed its own contracts, and dealt with its owners at arm's length gives a court little reason to treat it as part of its sponsor.

\subsection{SunEdison, TerraForm, and a ring-fence under test}\label{ssec:2.1.3}

SunEdison's collapse tested the second direction of the ring-fence, the protection of projects and their lenders from a failing sponsor. The debt and the assets stayed separate; the governance did not.

The structure had three layers. At the bottom sat individual wind and solar projects, many with their own non-recourse project debt. In the middle sat TerraForm Power, a listed company that SunEdison floated on NASDAQ in July 2014 to buy operating projects. TerraForm Power borrowed on its own account through notes, which are bonds: loans divided into tradable pieces sold to investors, paying interest at a fixed rate and repaying their face value at maturity (\cref{ssec:6.7.1}). In 2015 its subsidiary Terra Operating LLC issued USD~950~million of 5.875\% senior notes due 2023 (USD~800~million in January and USD~150~million in June) and USD~300~million of 6.125\% senior notes due 2025 to pay for acquisitions. ``Senior'' means the notes ranked ahead of the company's other unsecured debts; they were not secured on particular projects but were guaranteed by Terra LLC and certain of its subsidiaries. Each issue was governed by an indenture, the bond's contract, which required TerraForm Power to file its accounts with the US securities regulator on time (TerraForm Power 2016). At the top sat SunEdison, which developed new projects, sold finished ones into TerraForm Power, and kept control through high-vote Class~B shares and incentive distribution rights, a claim to a rising share of TerraForm Power's distributions. This listed structure is called a yieldco, and \cref{sec:32.7} teaches it.

The separation of debt and assets held. Neither TerraForm company filed for bankruptcy, neither was consolidated with SunEdison, and the project-level lenders kept their claims on their own projects. In March 2017 Brookfield agreed to become TerraForm Power's sponsor and bought into a business that had stayed whole; it completed the purchase of TerraForm Global in December 2017 (TerraForm Power 2018; TerraForm Global 2017).

Control was never separated, by design. By January 2016 SunEdison held 84.1\% of TerraForm Power's votes against 34.5\% of its economic interest, and the board changes of November 2015 were its first use of them. SunEdison had also pledged its Class~B shares and its incentive distribution rights to its own lenders, so control of TerraForm Power had become collateral for SunEdison's creditors (\cref{ssec:63.7.3}). TerraForm Power's 2015 accounts, filed only on December 5, 2016, carried a going-concern note, the auditor's warning that a company may not be able to keep operating for the next year (\cref{ssec:7.11.1}). The note cited two risks: that a bankruptcy court might order substantive consolidation with SunEdison, and that defaults could occur under the project-level financings; the annual report also listed the loss of SunEdison's services among TerraForm Power's risks. The late filing put the reporting covenants in the note indentures and TerraForm Power's NASDAQ listing at risk. In November 2016 SunEdison's creditors' committee, which represents the unsecured creditors in a bankruptcy, sought the court's permission to sue to unwind payments and asset transfers between SunEdison and TerraForm Power. Those claims were released only in TerraForm Power's March 2017 settlement with the SunEdison debtors, signed alongside the Brookfield agreement (TerraForm Power 2016; TerraForm Power 2018).

A ring-fence protects exactly what its contracts and corporate separations cover. At TerraForm the debt and the assets were separated, while votes, management, services, and the pipeline of new projects were tied to the sponsor, and the sponsor's failure travelled through every one of those ties. When you review a structure, ask three questions beyond the debt: who appoints the board, who provides the services the company cannot run without, and which agreements fall away if the sponsor fails. \Cref{ssec:14.18.1} returns to SunEdison as a case of sponsor credit risk.
