\chapter{What project finance is, and when to use it}\label{ch:2}

On April 21, 2016, SunEdison, Inc.\ filed for Chapter~11 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York. Seven months earlier its consolidated balance sheet had shown total liabilities of USD~16.1~billion, of which USD~11.7~billion was debt (SunEdison 2015). It filed with commitments for up to USD~300~million of new financing to keep the business running in bankruptcy (SunEdison 2016).

Two companies that SunEdison controlled were missing from the list of debtors. TerraForm Power and TerraForm Global were listed on NASDAQ, owned operating wind and solar plants that SunEdison had built or bought, and paid their shareholders dividends from those plants' cash. SunEdison held most of their votes. Much of the USD~11.7~billion of debt on SunEdison's consolidated balance sheet was not SunEdison's to repay: it had been borrowed by the TerraForm companies and by the individual projects beneath them, and the lenders who provided it could look only to those companies and those projects. Neither TerraForm company was pulled into the bankruptcy, and in 2017 Brookfield bought control of TerraForm Power and all of TerraForm Global as going concerns (TerraForm Power 2018).

The separation did not hold cleanly. TerraForm Power's audited 2015 accounts carried a going-concern explanatory note, partly because a bankruptcy court might consolidate it with SunEdison. Its 2015 annual report was not filed until December 5, 2016, which put its bond indentures and its stock exchange listing at risk. Five months before the filing, SunEdison had used its voting control to remove TerraForm Power's chief executive and chief financial officer (TerraForm Power 2016). The plants kept generating electricity. The companies that owned them spent two years fighting their own sponsor.

Chapter~1's Llano Pardo deal used the same structure without naming it. What kept the TerraForm plants and their lenders out of SunEdison's bankruptcy, and why the companies' governance was dragged in anyway, are the first things a project financier needs to understand about it.

\section{The project company and its ring-fence}\label{sec:2.1}

\subsection{A company with one asset and no history}\label{ssec:2.1.1}

When Sterrenberg Bank's credit committee approved its half of the Llano Pardo loan in 2016 (\cref{ch:1}), the borrower had no audited accounts, no revenue, no employees of note, and no track record. Llano Pardo Solar SA had been incorporated in Corredana for one purpose: to build and own a 50~MW solar plant on 152 hectares of leased plateau and sell its output to the state utility, ELNACOR, for 20 years. A corporate lender would have asked how the borrower had performed over the past five years. The committee could only ask what the plant would do over the next twenty, and who had promised what.

That shift in the question defines the discipline. \term{Project finance} is financing in which lenders are repaid from the cash flows of a single ring-fenced project and secured on its assets and contracts, with limited or no recourse to the sponsors. Each of its three features changes how a lender works. Because repayment comes from one project's cash flows, the lender studies the project, not the owner: its output, its revenue contract, its costs. Because the security is the project's assets and contracts, the lender's protection on default is the ability to take over a working plant and its contracts, not the ability to sell a pile of steel. Because recourse to the owners is limited or absent, the lender cannot fall back on a parent company's balance sheet when the forecasts prove wrong.

The legal device that makes all three features possible is the \term{special purpose vehicle} (SPV): a company created to own and operate one project and to do nothing else, so that the project's assets, contracts, and debts are held apart from its owners'. The legal form follows the host country's company law. Llano Pardo Solar is a Corredanan \emph{sociedad anónima} (SA). Case P's Bélanou Power is a Kessaran \emph{société anonyme}, also SA. In the United States the usual form is a limited liability company: Sabine Pass Liquefaction, LLC, which borrowed for the first LNG export trains in Louisiana (\cref{ssec:2.3.3}), is one. In England it is a private limited company. The form matters less than three properties every form must have. The company's liability must be separate from its shareholders', its shares must be capable of being pledged to lenders, and it must be able to hold the licenses and sign the contracts the project needs. From here on the book calls the SPV the \term{project company}: the special purpose vehicle that owns the project, signs its contracts, and borrows its debt.

The owners of the project company are its sponsors. A \term{sponsor} is a company that develops a project and owns equity in its project company, carrying the first loss. Llano Pardo had two: Tallisford Energy Partners, a London fund manager, with 60\%, and Grupo Arismendi, a Corredanan family group, with 40\%. \Cref{ch:4} sorts sponsors into strategic, financial, and developer types and shows how each behaves; for now the definition needs only its last clause. The sponsors' equity is paid in before the lenders lose a dollar and is repaid only after the lenders have been paid what is due, so the sponsors are the first to absorb any shortfall.

Why form a new company at all, when Grupo Arismendi already had companies that could have signed the PPA? Each reason comes from a different chair at the table. The lenders want a borrower whose only assets and liabilities are the project's, so that the cash they forecast is the cash they will get, and so that no unrelated creditor stands beside them. The sponsors want their other businesses protected if the plant fails, and they want to be able to sell the project later by selling shares rather than by assigning dozens of contracts and licenses one by one. Tallisford, a fund with investors of its own, needed a holding it could value and eventually exit. ELNACOR and the Ministry of Energy and Mines wanted a counterparty whose sole business was delivering the plant they had tendered for, which made the PPA's performance obligations easier to enforce and the plant easier to keep running if a sponsor failed.

\subsection{What the ring-fence keeps in and keeps out}\label{ssec:2.1.2}

A new company is not enough on its own. Nothing in Corredanan company law stops Llano Pardo Solar SA from borrowing more money, guaranteeing a sponsor's bank loan, buying a second site, or paying its cash into an account Grupo Arismendi also uses. Each of those acts would let other creditors or other businesses reach the cash that the lenders sized their loan against. The finance documents therefore build a fence around the project company with promises.

\term{Ring-fencing} is the legal and contractual separation that keeps a project's assets and cash flows apart from its sponsors' other businesses, protecting each from the other's failure. It works in two directions, and practitioners who remember only one of them misjudge deals.

The first direction protects the sponsors from the project. If Llano Pardo had failed, Sterrenberg Bank and the Atlantic Basin Development Bank (ABDB) could have enforced their security over the plant, its contracts, its accounts, and the shares in the project company, and nothing else. Tallisford's other funds and Grupo Arismendi's agribusiness were beyond their reach. The sponsors' maximum loss was the equity they had put in.

The second direction protects the project's lenders from the sponsors. If Grupo Arismendi's agribusiness had collapsed in 2020, its creditors could have claimed its assets, including its 40\% shareholding in Llano Pardo Solar SA. Those shares were already pledged to the project lenders, so the agribusiness creditors would have taken them subject to the pledge, and the value they could extract was the shares' value after the project debt: in practice, the right to future distributions once the lenders had been paid. They could not seize the plant, the PPA receivables, or the cash in the project accounts, because none of those belonged to Grupo Arismendi. \Cref{exh:2.1} draws both directions on Llano Pardo's parties.

Four promises in the finance documents build the fence, and they usually sit together in the covenant section of the common terms agreement, the master finance document that all the senior lenders sign (\cref{ch:51} teaches the document set). The \term{single-purpose undertaking} is a covenant by the project company to carry on no business other than the project. A separateness undertaking, which the book uses without a formal definition, is the project company's promise to behave as a separate company: its own name, books, bank accounts, and decisions, and arm's-length terms with its owners. A limit on other financial debt and guarantees keeps new creditors from standing beside the lenders. A ban on mergers, acquisitions, and subsidiaries stops the project company from becoming something else. \Cref{cl:2.1} is an illustrative version drafted under English law for a 2024 common terms agreement for a wind farm in Chile's Biobío region, the deal that \cref{ex:2.1,ex:2.3,ex:2.4} follow.

\begin{clause}{Single-purpose and separateness undertaking, common terms agreement \illustrative}\label{cl:2.1}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Business.} The Borrower shall not carry on any business other than the development, ownership, construction, operation, maintenance and financing of the Project in accordance with the Project Documents, and activities reasonably incidental to it.
\item \emph{Financial Indebtedness and guarantees.} The Borrower shall not incur or permit to subsist any Financial Indebtedness, or give any guarantee or indemnity in respect of the obligations of any person, other than Permitted Financial Indebtedness.
\item \emph{Separateness.} The Borrower shall: (i) maintain its own books, records and bank accounts, separate from those of any Sponsor or Affiliate; (ii) not commingle its funds with those of any other person; (iii) hold itself out as a separate legal entity and conduct business in its own name; and (iv) enter into any transaction with a Sponsor or Affiliate only on arm's length terms, in writing, and, where the value of the transaction exceeds USD 250,000 in any Financial Year, with the prior consent of the Intercreditor Agent.
\item \emph{Corporate structure.} The Borrower shall not enter into any amalgamation, demerger, merger or corporate reconstruction, acquire any company, business or undertaking, or establish or acquire any Subsidiary.
\end{enumerate}
\end{clause}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Paragraph (a). The words ``reasonably incidental'' carry the negotiation. Sponsors want them wide enough to cover future grid services or a co-located battery without asking; lenders want them narrow, because each new activity brings new risks the model did not test. The usual landing is a narrow clause plus a consent process.
\item Paragraph (b). ``Permitted Financial Indebtedness'' is a defined list (typically the senior facilities, hedging under the hedging policy, subordinated shareholder loans, and a small working-capital basket). The guarantee ban protects the second direction of the ring-fence: without it, a sponsor could ask the project company to guarantee the sponsor's own corporate loan.
\item Paragraph (c). Separateness is what persuades a court in a sponsor's insolvency that the project company is a different person. A project company that shared bank accounts with its parent, paid the parent's bills, and let the parent sign for it invites a creditor's argument that the two should be treated as one. The consent threshold in sub-paragraph (iv) aims at the affiliate EPC or O\&M contract on soft terms, the most common way value leaks out of a fenced project.
\item Paragraph (d). A project company that merged with another company would import that company's creditors. Lenders sometimes allow a subsidiary for a specific purpose, such as holding a transmission line that must later be transferred to a utility, and list it as an exception.
\end{enumerate}

Whether the fence holds in the end is a question for the insolvency law of the sponsor's country, not for the clause. Common-law courts can, in rare cases, consolidate a parent and a subsidiary that were run as one business; civil-law systems reach similar results through other doctrines. The clause cannot prevent that, but a project company that has actually behaved separately gives a court little reason to ignore its separateness. \Cref{ch:52} shows how the security package and the accounts structure reinforce the fence, and \cref{ch:64} what happens to it in a sponsor's insolvency.

\subsection{SunEdison, TerraForm, and a ring-fence under test}\label{ssec:2.1.3}

SunEdison's collapse tested both directions of the ring-fence at once, and the result was mixed in a way that teaches more than a clean success would have.

The structure had three layers. At the bottom sat individual wind and solar projects, many with their own non-recourse project debt. In the middle sat TerraForm Power, a listed company that SunEdison had floated on NASDAQ in July 2014 to buy operating projects from it. TerraForm Power borrowed on its own account: in 2015 its subsidiary Terra Operating LLC issued USD~800~million of 5.875\% senior notes due 2023 and USD~300~million of 6.125\% senior notes due 2025 to pay for acquisitions. At the top sat SunEdison, which developed new projects, dropped finished ones into TerraForm Power, and kept control through high-vote Class~B shares and incentive distribution rights, a claim to a rising share of TerraForm Power's distributions (TerraForm Power 2016). \Cref{ch:32} teaches this listed-vehicle structure, known as a yieldco, in full; here only its fence matters.

The fence around the projects and around TerraForm Power's cash held. Neither TerraForm company filed for bankruptcy, neither was consolidated into SunEdison's case, and the project-level lenders kept their claims on their own projects. When Brookfield agreed in March 2017 to become TerraForm Power's sponsor, it bought into a business that had stayed whole.

The fence around governance did not hold, because SunEdison's control rights crossed it by design. By January 2016 SunEdison held 84.1\% of TerraForm Power's votes against 34.5\% of its economic interest. On November 20, 2015, it used that control to remove TerraForm Power's chief executive and chief financial officer and to replace members of the committee meant to police conflicts with SunEdison; three directors resigned in protest. SunEdison had also pledged its Class~B shares and its incentive distribution rights to its own lenders, so in bankruptcy SunEdison's creditors held the levers of control over TerraForm Power. TerraForm Power's 2015 accounts were filed on December 5, 2016, with a going-concern note citing the risk of substantive consolidation, and its late filings put the reporting covenants in its note indentures and its NASDAQ listing at risk. SunEdison's creditors' committee pursued claims to unwind payments and asset transfers between SunEdison and TerraForm Power, released only in the 2017 settlement with Brookfield (TerraForm Power 2016; TerraForm Power 2018).

The mechanism to carry away is that a ring-fence is a set of contracts and corporate separations, and it protects exactly what those contracts and separations cover. Debt and assets were fenced. Votes, management, services, and the pipeline of new projects were deliberately connected to the sponsor, so the sponsor's failure flowed through them. When you review a structure, ask three questions beyond the debt: who appoints the board, who provides the services the company cannot run without, and which agreements fall away if the sponsor fails. \Cref{ssec:14.18.1} returns to SunEdison when it teaches sponsor credit risk.

\section{What lenders can reach}\label{sec:2.2}

\subsection{Non-recourse, limited recourse, full recourse}\label{ssec:2.2.1}

``Recourse'' is the lender's right to claim against someone other than the borrower. Every lender has full claims against the borrower itself; the question in project finance is what the lenders can claim from the sponsors when the project company cannot pay. The answer sits on a scale with three named points.

\term{Non-recourse debt} is debt whose lenders can look only to the project company's assets and cash flows, never to the sponsors. Llano Pardo's loan was non-recourse from financial close. The sponsors committed to contribute their share of the project's cost as equity, USD~16.3 million in total, and gave nothing else: no guarantee of the loan, no promise to fund overruns, no undertaking to complete the plant. Had the plant been washed off its plateau in 2019, Tallisford and Grupo Arismendi would have lost their USD~16.3 million and owed the lenders nothing more. Strictly, the equity commitment itself is an obligation the sponsors owe until it is paid in, so even the purest non-recourse deal involves the sponsors' money; the label means that once the agreed equity is in, the lenders' only remaining claim is on the project.

\term{Full recourse debt} is debt for which the borrower's owner is liable without limit. A parent company that borrows in its own name and spends the money on a project gives full recourse, because its whole balance sheet stands behind the loan. So does a parent that guarantees its subsidiary's loan in full and for its whole life.

\term{Limited recourse debt} is debt for which the sponsors give defined, capped support, usually until completion, beyond their equity. Most project financings in the world sit here. The sponsors promise something more than their equity for a defined period or up to a defined amount, the promise falls away when the risk it covers has passed, and the loan becomes non-recourse for the rest of its life. The defined amount and the defined period are the whole negotiation, which is why the next section gives them a framework.

The distinction matters to three parties beyond the sponsors and lenders. The sponsor's own corporate lenders and rating agencies count full recourse debt as the sponsor's debt, ignore non-recourse debt for most purposes, and argue about limited recourse support (\cref{ssec:2.5.1}). An EPC contractor in a sponsor consortium, such as a construction company that also owns equity, may find that its parent's completion guarantee to the project lenders appears in its own covenant calculations. A host government that guarantees the offtaker's payments is itself providing a form of recourse, to the state rather than the sponsors, and \cref{ch:17} treats that support separately.

\subsection{Where limited recourse sits in practice}\label{ssec:2.2.2}

\term{Sponsor support} is any undertaking by the sponsors to provide funds or performance to the project company beyond their base equity. Four forms account for most of it, and \cref{ch:26} owns the instruments and their drafting.

A cost-overrun undertaking commits the sponsors to fund construction costs above the budget, up to a cap, usually as subordinated equity. A completion guarantee goes further: until the plant reaches financial completion, the lenders' test of physical, operational, and financial performance, the sponsors guarantee the senior debt itself, so a project abandoned during construction becomes the sponsors' debt to repay. Contingent equity is an extra equity commitment that is drawn only if named events occur. The plainest form of all is the base equity commitment backed by a letter of credit from a strong bank, so that the lenders are not exposed to a sponsor that cannot pay its equity when called. Each form has a trigger, a cap, and a release date, and a term sheet that asks for ``sponsor support'' without specifying all three is unfinished.

\begin{framework}[label={fw:recourse-ladder}]{The recourse ladder}
\begin{enumerate}
\item List every route by which the lenders can reach the sponsors: base equity commitment, contingent equity, cost-overrun undertaking, completion guarantee, debt guarantee.
\item For each route, record its trigger, its cap (in currency), and its release date or event.
\item Compute the sponsor's maximum loss on the project if it fails before completion and if it fails after completion, adding each route that is live at that time.
\item Compare both figures with the sponsor's appetite for the project and with its covenant and rating headroom, and identify the rung on which the sponsor can stand.
\end{enumerate}
Use it whenever a term sheet asks for sponsor support, on either side of the table.
\end{framework}

The \term{recourse ladder} is the book's name for this method. It turns a debate about labels (``is this still non-recourse?'') into a debate about numbers, which is where sponsors and lenders can trade. \Cref{ex:2.1} climbs it.

\begin{example}{Climbing the recourse ladder \illustrative}\label{ex:2.1}
In May 2024 the board of Generadora Litoral Andino SA (GLA), a generator listed on the Santiago Stock Exchange and reporting in US dollars, is choosing how to fund Parque Eólico Alto Huelén, a 240~MW wind farm in Chile's Biobío region that will cost USD~389.6 million to build. If project financed, the project company would borrow 75\% of the cost and GLA would contribute the rest as equity:
\begin{equation*}
\text{Debt} = 0.75 \times 389.6 = 292.2, \qquad \text{Equity} = 389.6 - 292.2 = 97.4 \quad \text{(USD m)}.
\end{equation*}
Suppose that before completion the cost rises by 14\%, an overrun of $0.14 \times 389.6 = $ USD~54.5 million, after a turbine foundation redesign, and that whoever is obliged to fund it does so. GLA can stand on any of four rungs.

\emph{Full recourse.} GLA borrows the whole USD~389.6 million in its own name (the corporate route of \cref{ex:2.3}) and funds the overrun the same way. If the wind farm becomes worthless, at any date, GLA still owes the money it borrowed: a maximum loss of $389.6 + 54.5 = $ USD~444.1 million.

\emph{Completion guarantee.} The project company borrows USD~292.2 million, and GLA guarantees that debt until financial completion. If the project fails before completion, GLA loses its equity, the overrun it funded, and the guaranteed debt: $97.4 + 54.5 + 292.2 = $ USD~444.1 million, the same as full recourse. At completion the guarantee is released. If the project fails afterward, GLA loses only the money it has put in, $97.4 + 54.5 = $ USD~151.9 million.

\emph{Capped cost-overrun undertaking.} GLA promises to fund overruns up to 15\% of the cost, $0.15 \times 389.6 = $ USD~58.4 million, before completion. The USD~54.5 million overrun is inside the cap, so GLA funds all of it. Its maximum loss is USD~151.9 million whether the project fails before or after completion. On the day the loan was signed, before any overrun was known, its maximum exposure on this rung was $97.4 + 58.4 = $ USD~155.8 million, and had no overrun occurred, completion would have cut it to the USD~97.4 million of base equity.

\emph{Non-recourse.} GLA commits its USD~97.4 million of equity and nothing else. It may choose to fund the overrun to protect its equity, but it cannot be made to. Its maximum loss is USD~97.4 million. The lenders now own the overrun problem: they must lend more, find another funder, or watch an unfinished wind farm secure their loan.

The ladder shows what the lenders' request is worth to each side. Between the completion guarantee and the capped undertaking lies USD~292.2 million of pre-completion exposure, the whole senior loan. Between the capped undertaking and non-recourse lies USD~54.5 million of overrun, which on a well-run wind farm with a fixed-price turbine contract is a modest risk. A sponsor that can carry USD~155.8 million but not USD~444.1 million should fight for the capped undertaking and give ground elsewhere. Release dates matter as much as caps: the support falls away at completion, which is why \cref{ch:61} spends a chapter on how completion is tested.
\end{example}

\section{The contractual web}\label{sec:2.3}

\subsection{Contracts in place of a balance sheet}\label{ssec:2.3.1}

A corporate lender takes comfort from a balance sheet: many assets, many customers, retained earnings, a history of surviving bad years. Llano Pardo Solar SA had none of that. What it had instead was a set of signed contracts, each of which took one uncertainty about the project's future and turned it into a promise from a named party (\cref{exh:1.1}).

The PPA fixed the revenue. ELNACOR, the state utility, agreed to buy every megawatt-hour the plant produced for 20 years at USD~58.20/MWh, to pay for energy it could not take because of grid curtailment above 50 hours a year, and to back its payments with a standby letter of credit for three months of expected revenue. The EPC contract fixed the construction cost and date: Montajes Cordillera would deliver a working plant for USD~49.1 million by December 31, 2017, or pay delay damages of USD~28,700 a day. The O\&M agreement fixed most of the operating cost for five years at a fee of USD~412 thousand a year. The 30-year land lease with the farmers' cooperative fixed the site. The Ministry of Finance's letter of support, which is not a guarantee, gave the lenders the government's stated backing for ELNACOR's obligations.

Two of those contracts appear in almost every project financing, and each needs a definition here. A \term{power purchase agreement} (PPA) is a long-term contract under which a buyer, usually a utility, pays a generator for capacity, energy, or both at prices set by formula; \cref{sec:18.1} teaches the contract in full. An \term{engineering, procurement, and construction contract} (EPC contract) is a contract under which one contractor designs, procures, and builds the plant, usually for a fixed price and a fixed completion date, with liquidated damages for delay and underperformance; \cref{ch:22} teaches it in full. The buyer under the PPA is the offtaker. An \term{offtaker} is the party that buys the project's output under a long-term contract: a utility under a PPA, an LNG buyer under a sale and purchase agreement, a government paying for a road's availability, or a technology company leasing a data center.

Taken together, the contracts are what this book calls the \term{contractual web}: the set of interlocking contracts that fixes a project's revenues, costs, and risks and so takes the place of a corporate balance sheet as the lenders' protection. ``Interlocking'' is the working word. The PPA's required commercial operation date has to fall after the EPC contract's guaranteed completion date, with a margin. The delay damages the EPC contractor pays have to cover what the project company loses each day the plant is late. The O\&M operator's availability guarantee has to protect the revenue the PPA pays for. When the pieces do not interlock, the gap belongs to the sponsors, and Llano Pardo found one in its first year. Dry-season dust on the modules and a tracker-controller firmware fault cut output 7.5\% below forecast and distributions by USD~441 thousand (\cref{ex:1.9}). The O\&M agreement guaranteed inverter and tracker availability, not cleanliness, so no contract moved the soiling loss away from the sponsors. \Cref{ch:28} teaches how to map the whole web and scan it for gaps before signing.

\subsection{Giving each risk to the party best able to manage it}\label{ssec:2.3.2}

Every project financier repeats the rule that each risk should go to the party best able to manage it. Repeated without its tests, the rule becomes a slogan that justifies whatever the stronger party wanted. The \term{risk allocation principle}, as this book uses it, is the rule that each risk should be borne by the party best able to control it, price it, and absorb its loss. Each of the three tests asks a different question.

Control asks who can change the probability or size of the loss. The EPC contractor controls the construction schedule; the offtaker controls whether it takes the energy; nobody controls the wind. Pricing asks who can estimate the risk and charge for it with the least uncertainty, because a party that cannot price a risk will either refuse it or charge a premium for its ignorance. An EPC contractor that has built 410~MW of solar plants prices schedule risk better than a fund manager in London. Capacity to absorb asks whether the party can pay the loss when it happens without failing. A contractor whose net worth is smaller than the delay damages it has promised has not really taken the risk; it has promised to go bankrupt.

When all three tests point to the same party, the allocation is easy and usually uncontroversial. The work begins when they diverge.

\begin{example}{Assigning the 2018 soiling loss at Llano Pardo \illustrative}\label{ex:2.2}
In Llano Pardo's first operating year (2018), output fell 7.5\% short of the P50 forecast, partly because dry-season dust settled on the modules faster than four cleaning cycles a year could remove it. Combined with a separate tracker firmware fault, the shortfall cut the sponsors' distribution from USD~1,822 thousand to USD~1,381 thousand, a loss of USD~441 thousand (\cref{ex:1.9}). The firmware fault was a defect in the tracker supplier's equipment, a different risk with a different owner. Who should have carried the soiling part?

\emph{Control.} Cordillera Servicios, the O\&M operator, decides how often the modules are washed. ELNACOR controls nothing about module cleanliness. Montajes Cordillera, the EPC contractor, handed over a plant that passed its performance test at 81.4\% against a guaranteed 81.0\%; dust in year one is not a construction defect. The sponsors control the O\&M contract but not the dust. Control points to the O\&M operator.

\emph{Pricing.} The O\&M operator knows what an extra cleaning cycle costs in water, labor, and truck hours, and how much output it recovers. It can quote a price for cleaning to a soiling standard. The sponsors would have to hire someone to learn it. Pricing points to the O\&M operator.

\emph{Capacity to absorb.} The O\&M operator's whole fee was USD~412 thousand a year (\cref{exh:1.3}), and its balance sheet runs to a few million dollars. A loss of the size seen in 2018 would exceed a full year's fee. A dust year twice as bad would threaten the operator's solvency and, with it, the plant's maintenance. Capacity points away from the O\&M operator once the loss passes some fraction of its fee.

The answer the tests give is precise. The O\&M operator should guarantee a soiling standard (or a performance ratio net of weather), pay for its failures up to a cap set as a share of its annual fee, and recover the cost of extra cleaning through a higher fee. The sponsors keep the variation above the cap, which is weather risk that no party controls. That is close to what happened from 2019, when the operator moved from four to seven cleaning cycles a year under a renegotiated fee (\cref{ex:1.9}).
\end{example}

The principle fails in three recognizable ways. The first is a counterparty too small to absorb the risk it controls, as with the soiling loss above: the allocation looks right on paper and evaporates in a bad year. Lenders respond by capping the risk at what the party can bear and by asking for parent guarantees, bonds, or insurance behind it. The second is a transfer priced above the risk's value. An EPC contractor asked to take ground-condition risk on a tunnel it has not been allowed to investigate will price the unknown at its worst case, and the sponsors, and through them the host government or the toll payers, will pay that price whether or not the bad ground appears. Leaving that risk with the project company, or with the government that owns the geological data, can be cheaper. The third is a risk that no party controls at all: a hurricane, a pandemic, a change in the global price of steel. Such risks go to whoever is paid to hold them, which means an insurer for insurable events, the offtaker through force majeure and deemed-payment provisions for some events, the host government for some political events, and the sponsors' equity for the rest. Who pays for each is a negotiation over price, not a question the control test can settle.

Each party at the table reads the principle in its own interest. The sponsor reads it as ``pass everything you can through to the contractors and the offtaker.'' The lender reads it as ``leave nothing with the project company that it cannot pay for.'' The EPC contractor reads ``best able to manage'' as ``able to manage at a price,'' and adds contingency to its bid for every risk it accepts. The host government reads it as a reason to keep the risks it controls (permits, the grid, tariff policy) and to refuse risks it does not, which is why currency risk becomes the hardest negotiation on emerging-market deals. \Cref{ch:15} turns the principle into a method for analyzing, allocating, and pricing each risk.

\subsection{How contracts made Sabine Pass financeable}\label{ssec:2.3.3}

Sabine Pass in Cameron Parish, Louisiana, was built by Cheniere as an LNG import terminal, with most of its capacity reserved by Total and Chevron under 20-year agreements paying about USD~125 million a year each, used or not. US shale gas then turned the country from a likely importer into a potential exporter, and Cheniere set out to add liquefaction trains next to the existing tanks and berths through its subsidiary Sabine Pass Liquefaction, LLC. The sponsor was a development-stage company with a weak balance sheet; a liquefaction train is a multibillion-dollar plant whose output sells into a volatile world market. The sponsor's own credit could not carry such a plant (Cheniere Energy Partners 2013).

The contracts made it financeable. Between October 2011 and January 2012 the project company signed four 20-year sale and purchase agreements, with BG, Gas Natural Fenosa, GAIL of India, and KOGAS of Korea, each for about 3.5 million tonnes a year. Each buyer pays two prices for the gas it lifts at the terminal: about 115\% of the US Henry Hub gas price, which covers the cost of buying and processing the gas, and a fixed fee of USD~2.25 to 3.00 per MMBtu. A buyer may cancel cargoes with notice, but it still pays the fixed fee on the volumes it does not take (Sabine Pass Liquefaction 2014). The fixed fees, about USD~2.3 billion a year for the first four trains from buyers that were investment-grade or guaranteed by investment-grade parents, work like the capacity payment in a power plant's PPA. They do not depend on gas prices, on LNG prices in Asia or Europe, or on whether the buyer wants the cargo.

The contracts moved the commodity risk to the buyers. Each buyer carries the Henry Hub price and the destination market, the project company earns a fee for turning gas into LNG, and the lenders could size their debt to the fees from rated buyers. On July 31, 2012, the project company closed a USD~3.6 billion credit facility for the first two trains from 21 banks, which the sponsor's chairman described as the largest non-recourse debt financing in the US in a decade (Cheniere Energy Partners 2012). A lump-sum turnkey EPC contract with Bechtel fixed the construction cost and date, and Cheniere and Bechtel reported that every train was completed ahead of its guaranteed date and within budget.

The test came eight years later. When COVID-19 collapsed global gas prices in 2020, buyers cancelled many US cargoes. Cheniere's revenue for the first nine months of 2020 included USD~932 million from cargoes its customers chose not to take, recognized because the fixed fee was still payable (Cheniere Energy 2020). That figure covers Cheniere's whole business, including its Corpus Christi terminal, but the mechanism is the one the Sabine Pass contracts created. The plant was paid for capacity the buyers did not use, which is the contractual web doing exactly what the lenders had underwritten. \Cref{ch:21} dissects the sale and purchase agreement and \cref{ch:76} the liquefaction business.

\section{Project finance and its neighbors}\label{sec:2.4}

Project finance shares tools with six other kinds of financing, and practitioners borrow freely across the borders. What distinguishes each from project finance is the answer to three questions: what repays the debt, what secures it, and whether the lender can reach the owner. \Cref{exh:2.2} at the end of this section sets the answers side by side.

\subsection{Corporate finance}\label{ssec:2.4.1}

\term{Corporate finance}, also called balance-sheet financing, is borrowing by a company on the strength of its whole balance sheet and all its cash flows. When GLA, the Chilean generator of \cref{ex:2.1}, borrows from its relationship banks or issues a bond, its lenders are repaid from the cash of every plant it owns and every contract it has signed. They look at its history, its ratings, and its ratio of debt to earnings, and they protect themselves with financial covenants that cap that ratio across the whole group, not with security over any one asset.

Corporate finance has three advantages that project finance cannot match: it is cheap to arrange, fast, and flexible. One loan agreement funds many projects, the company can change its plans without asking anyone, and diversification does the risk management, because a failure at one plant is absorbed by the cash of the others. Its weakness for large projects is the reverse of that diversification. A project that fails hits every creditor of the company, and a project large enough relative to the company can take the company down with it. The corporate lender also learns little about any single project, because it is lending against the company, so it cannot price a risky project differently from a safe one. \Cref{ch:8} teaches how a company chooses its overall mix of debt and equity.

\subsection{Asset finance and leasing}\label{ssec:2.4.2}

\term{Asset finance} is debt or leasing secured on a specific movable asset and repaid by the user's general credit. An airline that borrows against two new turboprops, or a copper miner in Chile's Antofagasta region that finances 18 haul trucks, is using asset finance. The lender's protection is the asset itself and its resale market: if the borrower stops paying, the lender repossesses the aircraft or trucks and sells or re-leases them to someone else. Repayment, though, comes from the borrower's whole business, not from the asset's own earnings.

A lease achieves the same thing through ownership. The lessor owns the asset and the user, the lessee, pays rent for its use. A \term{finance lease} transfers substantially all the risks and rewards of owning the asset to the lessee: the lease runs for most of the asset's life, and the rent repays the lessor's cost with a return, so economically it is a secured loan in another legal form. An \term{operating lease} leaves the main risks and rewards of ownership with the lessor, which takes the risk that the asset is worth less than expected when the lease ends and it must find a new lessee. \Cref{ch:66} teaches how each is accounted for, and why the accounting distinction matters to sponsors whose offtake contracts can contain leases.

The difference from project finance is what makes the asset valuable. A turboprop is worth roughly the same in Lima as in Lisbon. A solar plant on the Llano Pardo plateau is worth what its PPA, its land lease, and its grid connection make it worth; detached from those contracts and moved elsewhere it is worth little more than scrap. Project lenders therefore take security over contracts and shares as well as equipment, and they plan to enforce by taking over a working plant, not by repossessing it.

\subsection{Reserve-based lending compared with project finance}\label{ssec:2.4.3}

Reserve-based lending is the oil and gas producer's version of a borrowing base. Lenders value the borrower's proved oil and gas reserves at their own price assumptions, set the maximum loan, the borrowing base, at a fraction of that value, and redetermine it, usually twice a year, as prices, production, and reserves change; the facility is usually revolving, so the borrower draws and repays as its needs change. Like project finance, it lends against the cash a defined asset will produce and takes security over that asset. Unlike project finance, it typically spans several fields, often has recourse to the producing company as a whole, and adjusts the debt to the asset's value continuously instead of fixing a repayment schedule at financial close. A falling oil price shrinks the borrowing base and forces the borrower to repay or pledge more fields within weeks, which no project financing would allow. \Cref{sec:75.3} teaches the method in full, and \cref{ch:3} traces its ancestry in the production payments of the mid-twentieth century.

\subsection{Acquisition finance}\label{ssec:2.4.4}

\term{Acquisition finance} is debt raised to buy a company or asset, repaid from the target's cash flows and often the buyer's. When an infrastructure fund buys a portfolio of operating wind farms in Spain, it typically borrows part of the price at a holding company above the farms. The lenders are repaid from the dividends the farms pay up after servicing their own project debt, and they take security over the holding company's shares in the farms.

Acquisition finance on infrastructure looks like project finance but carries two differences that change its risk. It funds no construction, so its lenders bear none of the risks that dominate a greenfield project. And it sits behind the project debt: when the farms' own lenders block distributions after a bad year, the holding company receives nothing, and its lenders depend on the reserves and covenants of their own loan. Pricing and structure follow from that structural subordination. \Cref{ch:47} teaches acquisitions and \cref{sec:31.3} holding-company debt.

\subsection{Securitization and structured finance}\label{ssec:2.4.5}

\term{Securitization} is financing by selling or pledging a pool of many similar receivables to a vehicle that issues debt repaid from the pool. A rooftop solar company with 40,000 household leases, a tower company with thousands of tenant contracts, or a bank with a book of car loans can each sell the receivables to a special purpose company that issues notes. Investors rely on statistics, not on any single contract: they ask how many of the 40,000 households will stop paying, not whether one particular household will. The vehicle has no operating business and no construction; it collects and pays. Project finance relies on a few large contracts with a few named counterparties, whose individual credit and performance the lenders investigate one by one.

\term{Structured finance} is the wider family: financing that uses vehicles, tranching, and credit enhancement to shape risk for investors, not tied to a single operating project. Securitization is one member. Others include repackaged loans and the hybrid structures that technology companies have begun to use for data centers.

Meta's Hyperion data center in Richland Parish, Louisiana, shows how a structure can borrow the vocabulary of project finance and still be something else. In October 2025 Meta formed a joint venture with funds managed by Blue Owl Capital, which hold 80\% to Meta's 20\%, to develop the campus at an estimated total cost of about USD~27 billion. The venture raised debt through a private bond offering anchored by PIMCO, maturing in 2049. Meta manages construction and operations, and from 2029 it will lease the buildings under initial four-year terms, renewable to as long as 20 years, with an aggregate initial lease commitment of about USD~12.31 billion. Behind the short leases sits a residual value guarantee: for the first 16 years, if Meta does not renew a lease and certain conditions are met, Meta pays any shortfall between the property's value and a threshold that starts at about USD~28 billion and declines over time (Meta Platforms 2026a).

The bond investors did not underwrite a 20-year contract from a creditworthy offtaker, which is what a project lender would have asked for. They underwrote Meta's willingness to renew four-year leases on specialized buildings, backed by Meta's promise to make good a fall in the buildings' value. Meta does not guarantee the bonds, and it does not consolidate the venture in its accounts, because it does not direct the decisions that most affect the venture's economics. It does, however, disclose a maximum exposure to loss of USD~46.03 billion at June 30, 2026, against an equity investment carried at about USD~2.9 billion (Meta Platforms 2026b). The structure sits off Meta's balance sheet while Meta keeps a large economic exposure. That combination of a vehicle, a short lease, and a value guarantee is structured finance with project features, and \cref{ch:82} teaches how lenders get comfortable with it.

\section{What sponsors, lenders, and governments gain from project finance}\label{sec:2.5}

\subsection{Risk isolation and debt capacity}\label{ssec:2.5.1}

The first reason a sponsor uses project finance is to keep one project's failure from becoming the company's failure. The second, closely tied to it, is debt capacity in its everyday sense: how much more a company can borrow before its lenders' covenants or its rating stop it. (\Cref{ssec:36.1.1} gives the term its narrower project finance meaning, the debt a project's own cash flows can support.) Both reasons turn on one ratio that corporate lenders and rating agencies watch. \term{Net debt to EBITDA} is a leverage ratio: borrowings less cash, divided by annual earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization. EBITDA, which \cref{ssec:7.3.1} defines properly, is roughly the cash a business's operations generate in a year before paying its lenders and the tax authority; dividing debt by it tells a lender how many years of operating cash the debt represents.

\begin{example}{Two ways to fund a wind farm \illustrative}\label{ex:2.3}
In May 2024 GLA (\cref{ex:2.1}) has EBITDA of USD~182.4 million and net debt of USD~510.6 million, a ratio of $510.6 / 182.4 = 2.80$\x. Its bank facilities cap net debt at 3.50\x{} EBITDA. Its board must decide by July 2024 how to fund the USD~389.6 million Alto Huelén wind farm, which will earn EBITDA of USD~41.3 million a year once running.

\emph{Corporate route.} GLA borrows the full cost in its own name. Once the cost is fully drawn, just before the wind farm starts operating, net debt is $510.6 + 389.6 = $ USD~900.2 million, while EBITDA is still USD~182.4 million because the wind farm earns nothing yet:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{900.2}{182.4} = 4.94\text{x}.
\end{equation*}
After the commercial operation date, EBITDA rises to $182.4 + 41.3 = $ USD~223.7 million and the ratio falls to $900.2 / 223.7 = 4.02$\x. The covenant is breached in both periods. The most GLA can add to its net debt without breaching is
\begin{equation*}
3.50 \times 182.4 - 510.6 = 127.8 \quad \text{(USD m)},
\end{equation*}
about a third of the wind farm's cost.

\emph{Project finance route.} The project company borrows 75\% of the cost, USD~292.2 million, without recourse to GLA. GLA contributes the USD~97.4 million of equity, and suppose it borrows all of that at the parent. GLA's net debt rises to $510.6 + 97.4 = $ USD~608.0 million, and
\begin{equation*}
\frac{608.0}{182.4} = 3.33\text{x},
\end{equation*}
inside the covenant with USD~30.4 million of headroom left ($127.8 - 97.4$). The project debt is excluded because GLA's facility agreements, like most, define net debt to leave out non-recourse debt of project subsidiaries. A rating agency may still add it back if it judges that GLA would support the wind farm in trouble, which \cref{ch:30} examines.

\emph{The failure case.} Now suppose that two years after commercial operation, serial gearbox failures across the turbine fleet make the wind farm worthless to its equity. Under project finance, GLA loses its USD~97.4 million, its covenant leverage stays at 3.33\x, and the project lenders bear the rest of the loss through their claim on a damaged wind farm and its warranties. Under the corporate route, GLA still owes the USD~389.6 million it borrowed, with no asset earning anything behind it. Its leverage returns to 4.94\x{} and stays there until it repays debt from other cash.
\end{example}

The example shows the two benefits separately. Debt capacity is about the covenant: the project finance route lets GLA build a wind farm twice the size its corporate headroom allows. Risk isolation is about the failure case: the corporate route converts a project problem into a company problem. A sponsor values the second more as the project's technology or resource risk grows relative to the sponsor's size.

The general idea has a name. \term{Off-balance-sheet financing} is financing that does not count as the sponsor's debt for covenant, rating, or (where the tests are met) accounting purposes. The parenthesis carries a trap that catches finance directors every year. GLA would own 100\% of the project company and control it, so the project company's debt would appear in GLA's consolidated accounts under the accounting standards most listed companies use; ``off balance sheet'' here means off the covenant calculation and, if the agencies agree, off the rating, not off the group accounts. Deconsolidation needs the sponsor to give up control, as Meta did at Hyperion (\cref{ssec:2.4.5}) and as Case P's Kilnworth does when it sells down in 2026. \Cref{ch:66} teaches the accounting tests.

Lenders gain from the same isolation. A project lender's exposure does not depend on whether the sponsor's other businesses prosper, and its information is complete in a way a corporate lender's never is: it has seen every contract, every cost estimate, and every assumption in the model. That is part of why project loans have performed better than their single-asset concentration would suggest.

\subsection{Governance and agency costs}\label{ssec:2.5.2}

A company that generates more cash than its good investment opportunities need is tempted to spend the surplus on bad ones: an acquisition the chief executive wants, a new business the board finds exciting, a headquarters building. Michael Jensen called the loss of value from managers acting in their own interest rather than their investors' an agency cost, and argued that debt reduces it because fixed debt service takes the free cash away from managers before they can spend it (Jensen 1986). An \term{agency cost} is the loss of value that arises when managers or one set of owners act in their own interest at the expense of other investors.

Project finance applies Jensen's argument with unusual force. Look at Llano Pardo's cash waterfall each December 31 (\cref{exh:1.10}). Revenue from ELNACOR lands in an account controlled by the lenders' agent, and leaves it in a fixed order: operating costs and taxes, interest, principal, the top-up of the debt service reserve account, and only then, if the distribution test is passed, distributions to the sponsors. The project company's managers cannot divert a dollar to a second solar plant or a new venture, because the single-purpose undertaking forbids it and the accounts structure makes it impossible. Every dollar of surplus reaches the shareholders.

The waterfall also protects shareholders from each other. Tallisford's fund wanted a steady cash yield for its investors. Grupo Arismendi, a family group with agribusiness and logistics interests, might have been tempted to send the project company's trucking work to its own logistics arm, or to keep cash in Corredana for its own use. Partners who would not trust each other with a joint bank account trust a waterfall that neither of them controls, administered by an agent bank that answers to the lenders. \Cref{ch:52} teaches the accounts structure and the waterfall in full.

Lenders gain more from this discipline than anyone. They get transparency (monthly and quarterly reports on a single asset they understand), control (consents over every material change, and step-in rights on default), security over everything the project company owns, and a price for each project's own risk instead of a blended corporate price. The record suggests the combination works. Moody's study of 10,452 project finance bank loans originated between 1983 and 2021 found a 10-year cumulative default rate of 4.8\% on the Basel definition of default, close to that of corporate borrowers rated Baa3, the lowest investment-grade rating, and an average ultimate recovery of 79.5\%, with lenders recovering everything in 62\% of defaults (Moody's Investors Service 2023). Defaults during construction recovered less on average than defaults in operation, 72.5\% against 80.8\%, which is one reason construction risk costs more than operating risk.

\subsection{Partnering and political deterrence}\label{ssec:2.5.3}

Project finance lets unequal partners invest together. Grupo Arismendi found the Llano Pardo site, signed the land option with the cooperative, and knew the ministries; it could not have raised USD~65.1 million alone. Tallisford had capital and solar experience but no presence in Corredana. A project company with its own debt let each partner invest a fixed share of a defined project without exposing its other businesses to the other's, and without either partner's corporate lenders having a say. Larger projects stretch the same logic further: a mining major, a state oil company, and a Japanese trading house can share one LNG plant through one project company, with each partner's commitment capped at its share of the equity.

The lenders' identity also changes how a host government behaves. Half of the Llano Pardo loan came from the ABDB, a development bank whose shareholders include governments that lend to Corredana. A government tempted to cut ELNACOR's tariff or delay its payments would be harming a lender its own finance ministry depends on, and would risk its standing with the bank. Practitioners call the deterrent effect of a development finance institution's or export credit agency's presence a ``halo.'' The deterrent has limits, and \cref{ch:60} examines when it has worked and when it has not.

\subsection{What the host government gets}\label{ssec:2.5.4}

Corredana's Ministry of Energy and Mines wanted 200~MW of solar power without borrowing to build it: four plants at Llano Pardo's cost of USD~65.1 million would have cost about USD~260 million. Project finance gave it three things. Private capital paid for the plants, with the cost recovered over 20 years through the tariff. Competition priced the power: four 50~MW lots were tendered in 2015, bidders priced against each other, and Llano Pardo won its lot at USD~58.20/MWh. And risk moved to private parties: if the plant had been late, over budget, or below its performance guarantee, the project company and its EPC contractor would have paid, not ELNACOR or the treasury.

What remained with the government is less visible and matters more than most ministries admit when they sign. ELNACOR, a state company, owed 20 years of payments; if it could not pay, the pressure to support it would fall on the Ministry of Finance, whose letter of support carried political weight even without legal force. PPAs of this kind usually contain termination provisions, which \cref{ch:17} teaches, under which the offtaker must buy the plant for a sum that covers the outstanding debt if the agreement ends through the offtaker's or the government's default. These are contingent liabilities, obligations that become payable only if something goes wrong, and they appear in no budget until they do. \Cref{ch:57} teaches how governments measure them and how statistical agencies decide whether the plant sits on or off the public balance sheet.
