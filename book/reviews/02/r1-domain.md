# Chapter 2 review, round 1: domain expert (veteran test, standards §12.3; completeness §12.1)

Reviewer role: domain. File: `chapters/02-what-project-finance-is.tex`. Inputs read: standards.md, decisions.md (D-011, D-013), brief u01 Chapter 2 and spec 1.A, writer notes, fact sheets `sunedison-terraform`, `sabine-pass`, `hyperion-meta`, `northvolt`, `t-ratings-2`, `eurotunnel`, `t-rbl`, Case Bible Annex P (Phase 2, reserved matters, Government Guarantee), glossary canon (homes for referenced terms).

## Verdict: FAIL

The chapter's frame is strong: the recourse ladder, the two-way ring-fence, the board paper and the fit test are tools a practitioner would use. But it has errors a senior banker or sponsor CFO would catch at once. The worst is that the recommended structure breaches GLA's covenant in the chapter's own overrun scenario, and the walkthrough and drill never notice. Several market descriptions are stated too broadly or are wrong: substantive consolidation, RBL valuation, Sabine Pass capacity and price, and the transaction costs for Bélanou. The fit test has no question on counterparty credit or currency. One brief item is missing: leverage as a reason sponsors use project finance. The chapter also generalizes illustrative pricing into a market claim.

## Defects

### A. Errors in the chapter's own deal logic

1. **The recommended structure breaches the covenant in the overrun case (Ex 2.3, ssec:2.5.1; walkthrough Steps 2 and 4, sec:2.8; Exhibit 2.3; drill, sec:2.11).** The board paper recommends project finance with a cost-overrun undertaking capped at USD 58.4 million. Under the project finance route GLA has only USD 30.4 million of covenant headroom. If the 14% overrun the paper presents in Step 4 happens and GLA funds its USD 54.5 million, net debt rises to 510.6 + 97.4 + 54.5 = 662.5, and leverage goes to 662.5 / 182.4 = 3.63x, a breach of 3.50x. At the full cap, leverage is 3.65x. Funding from cash on hand does not help, because net debt rises by the same amount. The drill attacks the bank's offer because it fails the construction-period covenant, but the chapter's own recommendation fails the same test in its own downside case. Required fix:
   - (a) In Ex 2.3, add a short paragraph after the project finance route: "If the 14% overrun of Ex 2.1 occurs and GLA funds it with parent debt, net debt becomes 662.5 and leverage 3.63x: the headroom of 30.4 covers only about half (52%) of the 58.4 cap."
   - (b) In Step 2 or Step 4 of sec:2.8, have the paper say how the overrun would be funded without a breach. The options are: negotiate the cap down to the headroom (about 7.8% of cost); provide the undertaking as a bank letter of credit, which is contingent until drawn; pre-agree an equity raise or hybrid; or agree a covenant carve-out for sponsor-support payments with GLA's banks. Name the option the CFO recommends.
   - (c) Add a row to Exhibit 2.3: "Parent leverage if the 14% overrun is funded by GLA: n/a / 3.63 (breach)".
   - (d) In sec:2.11, have the reasoning acknowledge that project finance also strains the covenant in the overrun case, and explain why it is still the better choice: the strain is capped at 58.4, not 444.1.

2. **The walkthrough reverses one of its own conditions (sec:2.8, Step 7).** The paper lists "a material fall in project finance margins relative to GLA's bond spread" as a condition under which the *corporate* route would be better. A fall in project finance margins makes project finance cheaper, so it strengthens the paper's recommendation. Required fix: "a material rise in project finance margins relative to GLA's bond spread (or a fall in GLA's own spread)".

3. **Alto Huelén has no revenue contract (Ex 2.1, 2.3, 2.4; sec:2.8).** The chapter's own fit test (question 2) requires contracted or forecastable revenue. Yet the GLA examples never say what the wind farm sells its power under. A 75% non-recourse gearing on a Chilean wind farm in 2024 is not credible without a long-term PPA, and Chilean renewables in 2024 face curtailment and low midday prices. Required fix: at first mention in Ex 2.1, add one illustrative sentence giving Alto Huelén a long-term USD PPA. For example: "a 15-year USD-indexed PPA for most of its output with a distribution-company auction contract or an investment-grade mining offtaker." Refer back to it once in Step 4 of sec:2.8.

4. **The non-recourse rung assumes the lenders have no overrun protection (Ex 2.1, "Non-recourse").** The text says the lenders "own the overrun problem: they must lend more, find another funder, or watch an unfinished wind farm secure their loan." In practice, lenders in a non-recourse construction financing size a contingency line inside the budget, and often a standby facility, before they close. That way a defined overrun is pre-funded without recourse. Required fix: add one sentence: "In practice lenders would size an owner's contingency and often a standby facility at close, so that an overrun up to an amount the independent engineer sets is pre-funded; the ladder shows what happens beyond those buffers (\cref{sec:31.7} teaches standby facilities)."

5. **The overrun is described inconsistently (Ex 2.1, last paragraph).** The text calls a USD 54.5 million (14%) overrun "on a well-run wind farm with a fixed-price turbine contract ... a modest risk", although the example has just made it happen. It also puts the redesign in the "turbine foundation". Foundations are usually balance-of-plant scope, not turbine supply. Onshore wind in Chile is commonly procured as a turbine supply agreement plus a balance-of-plant contract, with no EPC wrap. That interface is exactly why lenders ask for a cost-overrun undertaking. Required fix: replace the clause with: "which, on a multi-contract wind farm where the turbine supply agreement is fixed-price but foundations and civil works sit in a separate balance-of-plant contract, is the interface risk lenders most often ask sponsors to cover (\cref{ch:22})."

6. **The defined term and its examples contradict each other (ssec:2.2.2).** The paragraph defines sponsor support as support "beyond their base equity". It then gives as its fourth form "the base equity commitment backed by a letter of credit", which is support *for* the base equity, not beyond it. Required fix: present the equity letter of credit separately, as credit support for the base equity commitment (canon term "equity letter of credit", home ssec:32.3.2). It removes the risk that a sponsor cannot pay, and adds nothing to what the sponsor can lose. Then say "Three forms account for most sponsor support" and add the LC in the following sentence. Alternatively, widen the definition, but that would require a canon change, so do not.

7. **The waterfall paragraph overstates (ssec:2.5.2).** "Every dollar of surplus reaches the shareholders" contradicts the lock-up described in ssec:2.6.2 (1.15x) and ignores cash sweeps and reserve top-ups. Required fix: "Every dollar that passes the distribution test reaches the shareholders, and none can be spent on a venture the lenders have not approved."

### B. Market practice and legal accuracy

8. **Substantive consolidation is attributed to "common-law courts" in general (ssec:2.1.2, paragraph after the clause annotations).** Substantive consolidation is a US bankruptcy-court doctrine. English courts almost never disregard separate corporate personality: veil-piercing is confined to evasion of an existing obligation, per *Prest v Petrodel* [2013] UKSC 34. Civil-law systems reach consolidation through doctrines such as France's extension of insolvency proceedings for *confusion des patrimoines* or a fictitious company. Required fix: "US bankruptcy courts can, in rare cases, order the substantive consolidation of a parent and a subsidiary that were run as one business, which is the risk TerraForm Power's auditors cited (\cref{ssec:2.1.3}); English courts almost never disregard a company's separate personality; civil-law systems reach similar results through doctrines such as France's extension of insolvency proceedings to a company whose assets were commingled with its parent's." The facts reviewer should check the case citation; the doctrine is settled law.

9. **The RBL description is US-only and partly wrong for the international market (ssec:2.4.3; Exhibit 2.2 row).** "Lenders value the borrower's proved oil and gas reserves" is true of US RBL. International RBL, under English law in Africa, the North Sea and Asia, values an agreed cash-flow model. It credits probable reserves and fields in development, and it can finance a field before first oil (`t-rbl`: Kosmos/Jubilee). "Within weeks" also overstates the cure. US deficiency cures run 30 days or four monthly instalments (`t-rbl`, item 9). Required fix: "Lenders value the borrower's reserves (in US practice, proved reserves at the banks' price deck; in the international market, the net present value of an agreed field cash-flow model, which can credit probable reserves and fields still in development)", and "forces the borrower to repay, or pledge more fields, within a cure period measured in weeks or a few months".

10. **Sabine Pass capacity and price formula are misdescribed (ssec:2.3.3).**
    - (a) "Most of its capacity reserved by Total and Chevron": each reserved about 1.0 of 4.0 Bcf/d, half in total; the other half was reserved by a Cheniere affiliate (`sabine-pass`, fact 2). Change "most" to "half".
    - (b) "About 115% of the US Henry Hub gas price, which covers the cost of buying and processing the gas" is wrong on the economics. The 115% multiplier covers the gas feedstock plus the gas consumed as fuel and shrinkage. The liquefaction service itself, the "processing", is what the fixed fee pays for. The fact sheet also bars describing 115% as exactly a cost. Required fix: "about 115% of the US Henry Hub gas price, a multiplier meant to cover the feed gas and the gas the plant consumes as fuel, and a fixed fee ... which pays for the liquefaction capacity itself."

11. **Ground-condition risk solution uses a non-standard term (sec:2.14, Solution 2.3).** "Reference ground baseline" is not the market term. Required fix: "geotechnical baseline (\cref{ssec:72.3.2})", the canon term, with "report" as needed.

12. **The Moody's paragraph leaves out the finding a practitioner uses (ssec:2.5.2).** The cumulative 4.8% is quoted correctly. The more useful finding in the same study (`t-ratings-2`, fact 23) is the shape of the default curve. Marginal default rates look like high speculative grade in the first three years and trend toward single-A by about year seven. That is the empirical basis for pricing construction and early operations above later operations, and for refinancing after the ramp-up. Required fix: add one sentence with those two facts after the recovery figures, cited (Moody's Investors Service 2023). Note also that the data come from a lender consortium of unrated bank loans covering about 67% of global originations.

13. **The Bélanou size claim is wrong by an order of magnitude (sec:2.9, "Size: ample").** "The ratio that killed the Nairobi rooftops would be a fraction of a percent here" is not credible for an emerging-market CCGT with ECA and DFI tranches. Upfront fees alone are about 2% of debt. Lenders' and sponsors' advisors on such a deal cost several million dollars each, and ECA premia come on top. All-in transaction costs of a few percent of project cost are normal. Required fix: "a plant of several hundred megawatts spreads its transaction costs over a cost base dozens of times larger than the Nairobi portfolio's, so the ratio falls to a few percent, the range Alto Huelén showed." Keep it qualitative, because the Case P costs belong to Ch 4.

14. **The chapter's illustrative cost becomes a market claim (ssec:2.6.1, paragraph after Ex 2.4; close, sec:2.12; brief purpose echoed).** "A sponsor pays roughly a percentage point a year, and waits roughly a year longer" is derived from numbers Ex 2.4 itself labels "not market data", and the close restates it as general truth. The difference also depends on the sponsor's credit. For an investment-grade sponsor like GLA it is positive. For a weak or unrated sponsor with a creditworthy offtaker, project finance can be the *cheaper* or the only debt. Sabine Pass is the chapter's own proof: Cheniere's credit "could not carry such a plant". Required fix:
    - (a) At ssec:2.6.1 write "in this illustration, an investment-grade sponsor such as GLA pays about a percentage point a year more".
    - (b) In the close, write "an investment-grade sponsor pays a price in margin and time".
    - (c) Add the credit-substitution reason to ssec:2.5.1 (see defect 16).

15. **The Ex 2.4 simplifications are not declared (ssec:2.6.1).** Standards §7 requires every simplification to be stated with its direction. Only the debt-size mismatch is declared. Several cost items that move the answer are left out, and the comparison mixes bases.
    - The project loan's commitment fee on undrawn amounts during construction, typically a share of the margin, is omitted. This raises project finance cost.
    - Hedging cost (swap credit charges) and the cost of funding the DSRA or a DSRA letter of credit are omitted. These also raise project finance cost.
    - Negative carry on bond proceeds raised up front and spent over construction is omitted. This raises corporate cost.
    - A fixed-rate bond's credit spread over Treasuries is being compared with a floating loan margin over a SOFR-type reference rate, and the bullet bond with the amortizing loan at an equal average life.

    Required fix: add one paragraph that names these items, the direction each one moves the gap, and the chapters that price them: \cref{ch:38} for all-in cost, \cref{ch:6} for reference rates and swaps. Then state that the order of magnitude survives.

16. **A brief item is missing: leverage as a sponsor reason (sec:2.5; brief 2.3 "leverage (preview; effect on returns owned by Ch 8)"; coverage map B "leverage").** The section covers debt capacity and isolation, but not the plain reason sponsors seek 70 to 80% debt on a contracted asset: a smaller equity cheque raises the return on the equity invested. It also omits a related point a veteran expects. Project finance lets a sponsor borrow on the credit of the project's contracts and offtaker rather than its own, which is the credit-substitution reason. Required fix: add two or three sentences to ssec:2.5.1, before "The general idea has a name". Use GLA's figures, with no new numbers needed. The project finance route lets GLA own a USD 389.6 million wind farm with USD 97.4 million of equity, and the return on that equity rises as long as the project earns more on its assets than the debt costs. Forward reference \cref{ssec:8.2.1}. Then add one sentence on credit substitution, citing Sabine Pass (\cref{ssec:2.3.3}) as the case where the project's contracts were a better credit than its sponsor.

17. **The project finance fit test has no counterparty-credit or currency question (sec:2.7, `fw:pf-fit-test`; applications in sec:2.7 and sec:2.9).** Offtaker credit and currency convertibility are the most frequent deal-killers in emerging markets. Question 2 asks only whether revenue is "contracted, or predictable enough". That lets Bélanou answer "yes in structure" while the real question, SEKA's credit and convertibility, goes unasked. That is the exact failure the chapter then criticizes the committee for. The Nairobi example also passes question 2 without saying whether the 214 contracts pay in KES or USD. Required fix:
    - (a) Amend question 2 to: "Is the revenue contracted with a counterparty creditworthy enough, and payable in a currency the debt can be serviced in, or else predictable enough for lenders to forecast it for longer than the debt will be outstanding?"
    - (b) Redo the three applications in that light. Bélanou becomes "conditional on SEKA's credit and on convertibility", which makes the committee's miss a failure to apply the test. The Nairobi solution should state the contract currency.

    The six-question structure and the brief's "no to 1 or 2 means another tool" logic survive. Flag to the coordinator as a framework wording change.

18. **The fit test claims an unsupported frequency ranking (sec:2.7, framework lead-in).** "In the order in which they most often kill a deal" claims an empirical frequency the chapter cannot support, and the ranking is doubtful: size is not a more frequent killer than lender control or legal security on most deals. Required fix: "Ask six questions in this order: the first two decide whether project finance is the right tool at all; the other four decide what it will cost to make it work."

19. **The drill's bank offer is unrealistic as framed (sec:2.11).** A relationship bank that "leads GLA's corporate facilities" cannot by itself relax a covenant in a syndicated facility. It can only sponsor a waiver request that needs majority (often two-thirds) lender consent. GLA's bonds may also carry their own incurrence tests. A veteran CFO would also note the refinancing cliff when the three-year relaxation expires. Required fix: in the situation, have the bank offer "to lead a request to GLA's lending syndicate for a relaxation"; in the reasoning, add one sentence that the relaxation needs majority-lender consent and is therefore not in GLA's gift by Friday; and keep the three-year expiry point.

20. **The drill's "small relative to the group" threshold is arbitrary (sec:2.11, "What would change the answer", bullet 2).** "Less than about one year of GLA's EBITDA" has no basis. The test the chapter has already taught is covenant headroom. Required fix: "If the project's cost fitted within GLA's existing covenant headroom (USD 127.8 million) with room for the next project, and its failure would not move GLA's rating ...". Alternatively, label the EBITDA heuristic as a rough screen with its limit.

### C. Depth and lens points a 25-year practitioner would expect

21. **The SunEdison lesson leaves out its sharpest point (ssec:2.1.3).** `sunedison-terraform` fact 15 says the going-concern note cited project-level default risks as well as substantive consolidation. SunEdison supplied services (O&M, asset management) to the TerraForm projects, so its failure threatened the project financings themselves through the services that crossed the fence, not only TerraForm's governance. The chapter's takeaway already asks "who provides the services the company cannot run without", but the narrative never shows it happening. Required fix: add one sentence to the "fence around governance" paragraph, using only the sheet's wording: the going-concern note also cited the risk of defaults under project-level financings, because the projects depended on the sponsor for services. Do not state a number or name the specific projects unless the facts reviewer verifies them from the 10-K.

22. **The holding-company layer is missing from the ring-fence (ssec:2.1.1 or 2.1.2).** Sponsors and lenders routinely put a single-purpose holding company between the sponsor and the project company. That lets lenders take the share pledge over a clean entity and enforce it by selling the shares. It also lets the sponsor sell or finance its stake at holdco level. A practitioner would expect this in the section that defines the ring-fence. Required fix: add two sentences in ssec:2.1.2 after the four promises, with forward references to \cref{ssec:67.2.1} (holding company) and \cref{ssec:31.3.1} (holdco debt). Mention that Exhibit 2.1 omits the holdco for simplicity.

23. **The neighbors section misses the distinction that matters most in practice (sec:2.4, ssec:2.4.4; Exhibit 2.2).** The three questions (what repays, what secures, recourse) leave out *how the debt is sized and repaid*. Project debt is sized to contracted cash flow and fully amortizes inside the contract life. Acquisition and holdco debt is sized to a leverage multiple and is often a bullet or term loan B that relies on refinancing. RBL debt follows a redetermined borrowing base. Separately, whole-business securitization of UK and European infrastructure (airports, water) is the closest hybrid to project finance and is unnamed. Required fix:
    - (a) Add a fourth question, "how the debt is sized and repaid", to the lead paragraph of sec:2.4 and a matching column (or a note) to Exhibit 2.2.
    - (b) In ssec:2.4.4, add one sentence contrasting multiple-based sizing and bullet maturities (term loan B, canon home ssec:31.3.3) with project finance's sculpted amortization.
    - (c) In ssec:2.4.5, add one sentence naming whole-business securitization as a hybrid that secures a whole operating infrastructure business rather than a pool of receivables or a single new project, with a forward reference to the chapter the registry assigns. If none is assigned, raise a coverage request.

24. **The soiling example needs precision on the loss and on the operator's parent (Ex 2.2, ssec:2.3.2).**
    - (a) The O&M operator would compensate lost *revenue*, not lost distributions. From spec 1.A.10, the 2018 revenue shortfall was 7,841 − 7,253 = USD 588 thousand (D-013 arithmetic), and only part of it was soiling. Comparing the 441 distribution loss with the 412 fee mixes the two measures. Required fix: compare the operator's capacity with the revenue shortfall. Say that the soiling share is unknown but that a full dust year could plausibly exceed the annual fee.
    - (b) Cordillera Servicios is the EPC contractor's operations affiliate (spec 1.A.1). The capacity test should therefore ask for a parent guarantee from Montajes Cordillera, the remedy the next paragraph itself names. Add one sentence saying so.
    - (c) Replace "once the loss passes some fraction of its fee" with a labeled indicative market position under D-011: "O&M performance liabilities are commonly capped at between about half and all of one year's fee (indicative; utility-scale solar O&M, Latin America and Europe, 2015–2025)". Domain sign-off is given for that range.

25. **Insured damage is used to illustrate non-recourse (ssec:2.2.1).** "Had the plant been washed off its plateau in 2019" describes an insured physical loss, and the lenders would be loss payees. A practitioner reads it as a poor example of an uninsured equity loss. Required fix: use an uninsured failure, for example "Had ELNACOR stopped paying in 2019 and the plant's revenue collapsed".

26. **The battery episode leaves out the offtaker and government lens (ssec:2.6.2).** Selling stored energy "into ELNACOR's evening peak" under a single-buyer, as-produced PPA needs ELNACOR's agreement to amend the PPA, including a time-shifted price or dispatch rights, and probably the Ministry's, since it ran the tender. That consent is harder to get than the lenders', and the lenders would condition their own on it. Required fix: add one sentence naming the PPA amendment and ELNACOR's consent as the first gate.

27. **Separateness is used without a definition although the concept is owned here (ssec:2.1.2).** Brief 2.3 lists "single-purpose and separateness undertakings" as owned. The text says "a separateness undertaking, which the book uses without a formal definition", which also reads as meta-commentary. Required fix: define it in one sentence, using the writer's proposed canon wording, and drop the meta clause. Note for the coordinator: the canon row is still pending, so bold it only if the canon is amended.

28. **Exercise 2.12's Eurotunnel row puts recourse on the wrong party (sec:2.14, Solution 2.12 table).** The "Recourse to owner" column for Eurotunnel says "None to governments, by treaty". The governments were concession grantors, not owners. The treaty point is about state support, not owner recourse. Required fix: "None to Eurotunnel's shareholders; no government funds or guarantees, by treaty".

29. **The Terra Operating classification ignores the chapter's own taxonomy (sec:2.14, Solution 2.12).** ssec:2.4.4 describes holding-company debt above project-financed assets, raised to fund acquisitions, as acquisition and holdco finance. The Terra Operating notes funded First Wind and Invenergy and sit structurally subordinated above project debt. Classifying them as "corporate finance" with no comment contradicts ssec:2.4.4. Required fix: in the solution, say the notes are holding-company debt raised for acquisitions. Under the exercise's three categories they fall under corporate finance, because they rely on the yieldco's whole portfolio, but they are the acquisition and holdco debt of ssec:2.4.4 (\cref{sec:31.3}).
