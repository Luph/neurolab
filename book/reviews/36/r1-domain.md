## Blocking (Bible/model; route to editor-in-chief and Case P modeler)

**1. Case P's ECA WAL test is measured on the wrong profile. On the contractual profile the case fails it (Exh 36.13, ssec:36.9.2 last paragraph, sec:36.12 narration, Exr 36.13 solution, sec:36.15 "26 days from failing").**
- Defect. The chapter (and P-F09) tests the 2018 cover terms on the "scheduled installments" of all tranches *net of the forecast 50% sweep on the commercial tranche* (538.2 total). The exhibit note even states this as the method ("The ECA tests are measured on the scheduled installments"). That is wrong in principle. An ECA tests WAL, installment share and first repayment on the **covered tranche's own contractual repayment schedule**. Voluntary prepayments and mandatory prepayments of *another* tranche do not count, and a forecast sweep is neither contractual nor certain. Case P's tranches all amortize pro rata on one common profile (Case Bible line 254), so the ECA tranche repays on the unswept sculpted profile. I re-ran `size_fc` on the FC base. The common profile's shares give these values for the ECA tranche itself (USD 189.98 m): **WAL 7.79 years from the May 1, 2021 COD against the 7.25-year limit, a fail by 0.54 years.** Largest installment is 5.6%, 7.7% is repaid within 24 months, and the repayment term is 13.16 years; all three pass. Even counting the forecast sweep as repayment, the all-tranche WAL is 7.41 years, which also fails. The contractual profile rises without a break from 15.5 to a peak of about 35.5 (2033H1, scaled to 633.3). The 2029 peak and decline that Exh 36.13 shows comes from the sweep shrinking the commercial installments.
- Consequences in the chapter. (i) The statement "All four ECA tests pass, the WAL with the least room: 7.18 years … about 26 days" is false for the contractual profile. (ii) The scene ("Seven-eighteen against seven-and-a-quarter." / "Twenty-six days.") and the close repeat it. (iii) Line 813's explanation of the profile's shape ("rises … then falls, because Case P's capacity payments are partly indexed … and because its major maintenance cycle …") is wrong. The fall comes from the sweep alone. (iv) Exr 36.13(c) and its solution teach the faulty method.
- Required fix (editor-in-chief decision, then a model re-run and a ledger update, then a chapter rewrite). Choose one:
  - (a) Re-size Case P with the WAL constraint on the contractual common profile, repairing the profile as in Ex 36.12. This lowers P-F08 and moves P-F09, P-F36, P-F07 and P-F43 downstream across Chapters 29, 35, 36, 37, 39, 42 and later.
  - (b) **Preferred, because it teaches a real technique and keeps the debt close to 633.3.** Give the ECA-covered tranche its own profile inside 7.25 years (for example, equal or front-loaded semiannual installments). The other tranches take the back-ended principal, and their combined debt service is sculpted so that the total stays at 1.35x. This is standard practice when one ECA tranche cannot carry a back-ended sculpt. It requires amending the Case Bible's "all tranches pro rata on the common profile" and the narration's claim that "the other lenders would not have accepted" a separate profile. That claim becomes the scene's real negotiation point.
- In either case: rewrite Exh 36.13 to print the ECA tranche's contractual installments and tests. Replace the exhibit note with the rule: "ECA tests apply to the covered tranche's contractual schedule; prepayments, including sweeps of other tranches, are ignored." Restate the 538.2 reconciliation as a separate cash-basis memo line if kept. Correct the scene line, sec:36.15, Exr 36.13 and its solution, and the profile-shape sentence. Add one sentence of the rule to ssec:36.9.1. Notify Chapters 29, 39 and 42, which may already teach that Case P passes at 7.18.

**2. The description of Case P's COD re-sculpting contradicts the ledger, and the general description of resculpting is mathematically over-determined (ssec:36.2.4, "Resculpting at COD", line 200).**
- Defect. "Many term sheets let the lenders resculpt at … COD … keeping the debt amount and the target" cannot hold in general. With the debt amount and the final maturity fixed, the level DSCR is PV(CFADS)/D, so it equals the old target only if the forecast's present value is unchanged. Actual practice: the debt (and usually the final maturity) is kept, debt service is reshaped to the new CFADS, and the resulting level DSCR is tested against a floor. If it falls short, a mandatory prepayment restores it, typically from performance LDs (buy-down), or completion is not certified. The chapter also says "In Case P's actual history the lenders resculpted at COD to the same 1.35x target". The ledger says otherwise: P-F19 records a re-sculpted constant DSCR of 1.28x before the LD prepayment, and Annex P §1.12 item 8 sets a 1.30x completion floor.
- Required fix. Rewrite the paragraph: "Many term sheets let the lenders resculpt at COD on an updated forecast. The debt and the final maturity stay; the debt service is reshaped to the new CFADS, so the DSCR is level again but at whatever the new forecast supports. If that level falls below a floor set in the completion test, the borrower must prepay, usually with the contractor's performance liquidated damages, until it is restored." For Case P, write: "Case P's lenders resculpted at COD keeping the debt and the June 30, 2034 maturity, with a 1.30x completion floor; \cref{ch:61} follows what the re-forecast showed." Print no P-F19 value, per the brief.

## Accuracy and judgment defects

**3. ssec:36.5.2, the indicative tail judgment (line 409) and the sentence before it (line 405). Not signed off.**
- Defects. (i) "Two real examples bracket the range": Comber (about one year) and the Gulf maturities (debt "close to" 25-year contracts, which per the fact sheet means "within a few years") are both at the *short* end, so they bracket nothing. (ii) "Long tails of five years and more are the price of long tenors in emerging markets" is causally confused, because a longer tenor makes the tail *shorter*. It also contradicts the chapter's own Gulf evidence: emerging-market contracts with state-backed buyers carry short tails. (iii) The real drivers are omitted: what the post-maturity cash depends on (renewal and volume risk), how long a restructuring takes, and the fact that long EM tails are mostly a *by-product of lender tenor caps* (12 years for non-CCSU power plants, 22 years under the CCSU, DFI policy limits) set against 20- to 25-year PPAs, not a cushion lenders ask for. Case P shows this: its 12.4-year tail is the 14-year ECA cap at work, as the scene itself says. (iv) Demand-risk concessions, which need the longest tails, are not distinguished from availability contracts. (v) The D-011 label "contracted power and infrastructure in 2023 to 2026" names no market.
- Required fix. Change line 405's "Two real examples bracket the range" to "Two real examples show the short end". Replace line 409 with text to this effect (I sign off on this substance):
  > The book's view on tail length (indicative judgment for bank-, DFI- and ECA-financed contracted power and infrastructure in North America, Europe, the Gulf and DFI-led emerging markets, 2023 to 2026; not survey data): the length follows what the cash after maturity depends on and how long a restructuring would take, not the region. Where the payer is investment grade or state-backed and the contract carries no volume risk (availability PPPs, Comber's feed-in tariff, the Gulf contracts above), tails of about six months to two years are accepted. Where commercial banks lend against a sub-investment-grade offtaker, or a contract with volume or renewal risk, two to three years is the minimum. Demand-risk concessions need more, at least three years and often five or more, because the usual cure for a traffic shortfall is to extend maturity into the remaining concession (\cref{ssec:36.6.2}). Tails of five to ten years are common in DFI- and ECA-led emerging-market deals, but mostly as the by-product of the lenders' maximum tenors set against 20- to 25-year PPAs (12 years for non-CCSU power plants and up to 22 under the CCSU, \cref{ssec:36.9.1}), not because lenders ask for that much; Case P's 12.4 years is one (\cref{sec:36.12}). Whatever the number, test it in money: the gap between PLCR and LLCR on the downside case (\cref{ssec:35.4.2}) shows how much contracted value the tail holds against the debt service a restructuring might need to defer.
- Conforming edits. Line 813: replace "long, as an emerging-market gas IPP with a sovereign-guaranteed offtaker should have" with "long, as a by-product of the 14-year ECA cap rather than a lender requirement". The cost to the sponsor is debt capacity left unused, visible in the PLCR, and the tail later gave the lenders room in \cref{ch:64}. Line 695 (walkthrough) and line 898 (drill): describe the 7-year tails as "well above the two-to-three-year minimum and typical of a DFI- and ECA-led deal", not as "what an EM sovereign-backed deal should have".

**4. Ex 36.4 (Brazilian wind, lines 223–237, 265): the interest rate is unrealistic, and "high Brazilian real interest rate" is contradicted by the numbers.**
- Defect. An 18-year nominal BRL loan at 8.75% from a development bank and local banks in 2025 is far below that market. Long-term BRL infrastructure debt is IPCA-linked (TLP-based or incentivized debentures), and in 2025 it cost roughly IPCA plus 7% to 8% real, about 12% to 13% nominal with IPCA near 4%. Against the example's own 4.0% indexation, 8.75% is about 4.6% real, which is not "high" for Brazil. Standards §7 requires realistic figures.
- Required fix. Set the all-in rate at about 13.0% nominal and state that it reflects an IPCA-linked loan converted to nominal at 4.0% inflation. Recompute. My check: P90 still binds, at about BRL 516 m, with gearing about 43.5%. The sentence "Lower the rate to 6% and … the same project would bind on gearing" remains true (I get gearing binding at BRL 830.2 m). Keep that sentence, but phrase the contrast as "Brazil's real rates against a hard-currency or European rate". Alternatively, keep 8.75% and describe it explicitly as a concessional development-bank rate. Either way the "high … rate" wording must match the number.

**6. Ex 36.8 option (c) and the discussion (lines 432, 456): "funded at financial close, usually with equity" overstates practice.**
- Defect. A ramp-up or interest reserve is a use of funds in the funding requirement. In bank-financed toll roads it is commonly funded pro rata by debt and equity at the gearing ratio, with all-equity funding as a lender ask, not a norm. If debt funds part of it, the 2,666.6 does not all go to construction, and the gearing test must include the reserve. "The ramp-up risk is carried by equity that funds the reserve at close" is therefore true only in the equity-funded variant.
- Required fix. Say that the reserve sits in the funding requirement and is funded either pro rata at the gearing ratio or, where lenders insist, by equity. Show in one sentence what changes if 75% of the 208.4 is debt-funded: the debt that pays for construction falls by about 156. Restate the lender preference ("lenders prefer it equity-funded because then the ramp-up risk sits with equity") as the negotiated point.

**7. ssec:36.2.4, Circularity (line 198): the most common sculpting circularity is missing.**
- Defect. In a tax-paying project, CFADS is after tax, tax depends on operating-period interest deductions, and those depend on the sculpted profile. That makes debt service depend on itself even with IDC and fees fixed. Modelers meet this loop first, and the paragraph names only IDC, fees, the ECA premium and the DSRA.
- Required fix. Add: "In a tax-paying project the loop also runs through operations: interest is deductible, so the tax in CFADS depends on the profile that CFADS sets; \cref{sec:40.5} resolves it with the same methods."

**17. Judgment drill (sec:36.14, line 900): the case for resisting 1.25x rests on a mechanism that the DSCR cushion barely addresses.**
- Defect. The reasoning says payment risk, not resource, is the main downside, and that a lower target "makes every interruption draw the reserve harder". A missed or delayed payment by a single buyer removes most of CFADS whatever the cushion. Cushion helps only with partial or short delays. The tools for payment risk are liquidity and credit support: DSRA size, an offtaker letter of credit, a PRG liquidity facility, or PRI. A top arranger would say so and trade on them. The "What would change" paragraph half-acknowledges this with the PRG.
- Required fix. Add two sentences: the cushion is the wrong instrument for a payment stop; the arranger's real counter to 1.25x is "1.30x, plus 12 months of payment security (a 12-month DSRA or an offtaker letter of credit)". Explain that this protects against the actual risk at a cost the sponsor can price. Keep the 1.30x and P99 floor answer, and add that the P99 floor is nearly free on a solar resource and so is not a concession the sponsor will value. I otherwise sign off on the drill's answer (grant b, counter a, decline c).

**18. Exr 36.19 model answer: the proposed package is internally inconsistent for a hydro deal.**
- Defect. The "proposed package" includes a stand-alone one-year P99 floor at 1.00x that cuts debt to 52.8% gearing. The solution then argues it away by counting the DSRA. A practitioner would not propose a test and then explain why it should not apply. For run-of-river hydro with DFIs, the one-year extreme-dry test is normally a *liquidity* test with the DSRA and any dry-year facility counted.
- Required fix. Make the package's P99 test "P99 CFADS plus the available DSRA covers debt service". Present the stand-alone 1.00x floor and its USD 120.8 m as the commonest wrong design. The binding constraint then becomes the 70% gearing cap at USD 160.0 m (already computed in the solution).

**19. Four-lens rule (standards §2): the government and contractor lenses are almost absent.**
- Defect. Sizing is a major topic. The procuring authority's or host government's view gets one clause (line 267). The contractor's view is absent, although Case P's own documents tie them together: the completion test's DSCR floor after COD re-sculpting, and the LD prepayment in Annex P §1.12.
- Required fix. (a) In ssec:36.3.2, add a paragraph on the authority's view: in PPPs the authority cares about debt size because termination compensation is usually debt-based, because bid tariffs and affordability move with gearing and target, and because refinancing gains are shared. Authorities therefore cap senior debt or fix bid DSCRs so bids can be compared (cross-reference the PPP chapters). (b) In ssec:36.2.4, after the corrected resculpting paragraph (Defect 2), add a paragraph on the contractor's view: performance LDs are calibrated to buy down debt and restore the sized DSCR, and delay LDs to cover debt service. That is why EPC contractors negotiate LD rates against the lenders' sizing model.

**20. ssec:36.8.2 (merchant-tail treatments): a fourth common treatment is missing.**
- Defect. The chapter lists no tail debt, merchant-ratio debt, or a sweep. It omits the dual-profile structure common in European and Australian renewables and in soft mini-perms: a *target* (sculpted, faster) repayment profile, enforced by cash sweep, alongside a slower *legal* minimum profile. Lenders use it to size merchant-tail debt while keeping a default only on the legal profile.
- Required fix. Add two or three sentences describing it, linked to the soft mini-perm (ssec:36.7.2) and to sec:37.3, with no market-norm figures (none are verified).
