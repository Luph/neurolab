# Master Prompt: The Complete Project Finance Textbook

Oct 3, 2026 · @Lutfullah Bingol

## 1. The assignment

You are going to write the definitive textbook on project finance: one book that takes a complete novice — someone who does not yet know what a bond is, how a power plant earns money, or what “non-recourse” means — and turns them into a practitioner who can originate, structure, model, negotiate, finance, manage, and rescue large projects anywhere in the world, at the level of the best in the field. The reader must never need another book, course, or website to understand anything in it.

The standard is perfection, and here it has a precise meaning:

- Complete. Everything a world-class project financier needs to know and do is covered. Nothing is waved away as “beyond the scope.”
- Fully built. Nothing is sketched, outlined, summarized in place of being taught, or deferred with a placeholder. Every chapter is finished work.
- Taught, not asserted. Every concept is explained from first principles, demonstrated with fully worked examples, shown in real transactions, and practiced through exercises with complete solutions.
- Practical. The reader learns to do the work — build the model, mark up the term sheet, run the diligence, negotiate the clause, write the credit paper — not merely to describe it.
- A page-turner. The prose has the narrative drive to keep the reader going, without losing an ounce of rigor.
- Exhaustive, never padded. Every paragraph must teach, illustrate, or train. Cut repetition, filler, and generalities without mercy; never cut substance.

## 2. Who is writing this book

Write as though the book were produced by the field’s best people speaking with one voice:

- a senior project finance banker who has led, underwritten, and syndicated deals on five continents and sat on credit committees;
- a developer and sponsor-side investor who has taken projects from a patch of land through financial close, operations, and sale;
- a project finance lawyer who has drafted and negotiated concession agreements, offtake contracts, EPC contracts, common terms agreements, and intercreditor agreements under English and New York law and in civil-law jurisdictions;
- a financial modeler who has built and audited hundreds of bankable models;
- an independent engineer, a market advisor, and an insurance advisor;
- an export credit agency and development finance specialist, and a government PPP-unit official;
- a restructuring specialist who has led waivers, workouts, and enforcements;
- a master teacher who knows exactly where novices get lost and how experts actually think;
- a narrative nonfiction writer with the momentum of the best long-form journalism.

The four-lens rule: every major topic must be seen through the eyes of the sponsor, the lender, the host government or public authority, and the contractor or operator. A great project financier’s edge is knowing what every other party needs, fears, and will trade — so the book must teach the reader to sit in every chair.

## 3. The reader and the promise

The reader is intelligent, motivated, and starting from zero. Assume no knowledge of finance, accounting, law, engineering, energy markets, or Excel beyond basic arithmetic and the ability to open a spreadsheet. The book must supply every prerequisite itself. Teach the essentials up front in an engaging foundations part, and teach the rest just in time, at the moment the reader needs it — never a dry primer that delays the real subject.

Early in the book, walk the reader through an entire small deal end to end in simplified form, so every later chapter has a place to hang. Then return to each piece in depth.

A reader who works through the book, including its exercises and model builds, will be able to:

1. Explain what project finance is, when it is the right tool, and what it costs compared with the alternatives — to a novice, a board, or a finance minister.
2. Screen any project in under an hour and identify the handful of risks that will decide whether it is bankable.
3. Read every core project contract — concession, offtake, EPC, O&M and long-term service, supply, land, shareholder, direct agreements, insurance — and spot gaps, mismatches, and misallocated risks.
4. Build a full project finance model from a blank spreadsheet — construction and operations, funding waterfall, interest during construction and its circularity, sculpted debt, reserve accounts, tax, ratios, equity returns, scenarios and sensitivities — and audit someone else’s.
5. Size and structure debt — amount, tenor, repayment profile, ratios, reserves, sweeps, covenants, hedging — and choose among bank loans, bonds, ECA and DFI facilities, mezzanine, holdco debt, and private credit.
6. Draft, mark up, and negotiate a term sheet and the key finance documents, knowing what is market, what is negotiable, and why.
7. Scope and direct due diligence — technical, market, legal, insurance, model audit, tax, environmental and social — and challenge the advisors’ conclusions.
8. Structure cross-border and emerging-market deals: political, currency, and sovereign risk; ECAs, DFIs, guarantees, and political risk insurance; treaty protection.
9. Design, procure, and evaluate PPPs from the government’s side as well as the bidder’s.
10. Take a project through financial close, construction, completion, and operations as lender or owner — drawdowns, completion tests, covenant compliance, waivers, reporting.
11. Spot distress early and lead a waiver, restructuring, or enforcement.
12. Value projects and portfolios, and run or advise on acquisitions, disposals, and refinancings.
13. Work confidently in every major sector, from power and renewables to LNG, mining, transport, social infrastructure, water, digital infrastructure, and new energy technologies.
14. Write a credit paper or investment committee memo that gets approved for the right reasons.
15. Exercise judgment where the rules run out, and know which questions to ask and which expert to call.

Every chapter must visibly build one or more of these capabilities. By the last page, every one must be fully delivered.

Be candid with the reader about one thing: judgment is finally forged by doing deals. The book’s job is to compress as much of that experience as text can — through realistic simulations, decision drills, war stories, and model builds — and to show the reader exactly how to accelerate the rest.

## 4. Coverage map

The list below is the minimum scope. In the blueprint, organize it into the best teaching sequence — restructure, merge, or split as you see fit — and add anything a world-class practitioner would consider missing. Nothing on this list may be dropped.

### A. Foundations for the novice

- Money and time: interest, compounding, discounting, annuities, NPV, IRR and its traps (multiple IRRs, reinvestment assumptions, scale and timing effects), XNPV and XIRR, payback, real versus nominal values, inflation and indexation.
- Debt basics: principal, interest, and fees; bullet, annuity, straight-line, and sculpted repayment; fixed versus floating rates; reference rates (SOFR, SONIA, €STR, EURIBOR) and the LIBOR transition; margins and basis points; swaps at an intuitive level.
- Accounting from zero: the three financial statements and how they link, accruals versus cash, depreciation and amortization, deferred tax, working capital, reading a project company’s accounts.
- Corporate finance essentials: capital structure, leverage and its effect on equity returns, cost of capital, CAPM, WACC versus APV, risk and return.
- Probability for project risk: distributions, expected values, P50/P90/P99 exceedance levels, correlation, and the difference between sensitivity, scenario, and Monte Carlo analysis.
- Law for financiers: how contracts work (representations, warranties, covenants, conditions, indemnities, liability caps, liquidated damages and the rule against penalties, force majeure, frustration and impossibility), common law versus civil law, security and insolvency in principle, governing law, courts versus arbitration.
- How the assets work, at the depth a financier needs: thermal, wind, solar, hydro, nuclear, and battery plants; grids and electricity markets (merit order, marginal pricing, capacity and ancillary markets, nodal versus zonal pricing, curtailment, negative prices); oil, gas, and LNG chains; mines and processing; roads, rail, airports, and ports; hospitals; water plants; fiber networks and data centers.
- Excel to professional standard (the book’s models are built in Excel): layout, timing flags, core functions, avoiding fragile formulas, data tables, goal seek, circularity, error checks.

### B. What project finance is and why it exists

- The essence: a ring-fenced special purpose vehicle repaid from project cash flows; limited and non-recourse; the contractual web; allocating each risk to the party best able to manage it.
- How it differs from corporate finance, asset finance and leasing, reserve-based lending, acquisition finance, securitization, and structured finance.
- Why sponsors, lenders, and governments use it — risk isolation, debt capacity, leverage, governance and agency-cost benefits, partnering, political-risk deterrence — and what it costs: transaction expense, time, rigidity, information burdens, lender control.
- History and evolution: from early resource and production-payment financings through independent power, the PFI and PPP era, the post-2008 and Basel shifts, and the rise of institutional and private credit, to the energy transition and digital infrastructure — and what each era taught.
- The cast: sponsors (strategic, financial, developers), the project company, every type of lender, offtakers, host governments and regulators, EPC contractors and equipment suppliers, operators, input suppliers, insurers, advisors, agents and trustees, rating agencies, hedge providers. How each makes money, what each fears, and how each behaves under stress.
- The lifecycle end to end: origination, development, financing, financial close, construction, completion, operations, refinancing, sale, and decommissioning or handback.

### C. Risk — the heart of the discipline

- The complete taxonomy: development and permitting; construction (cost, time, performance); technology; resource and reserves; demand and volume; price and market; input supply; operating; offtaker and counterparty credit; interface; interest rate; currency (convertibility, transfer, devaluation); inflation; refinancing; liquidity; political (expropriation, political violence, breach of contract, change in law, non-honoring of sovereign obligations); regulatory; legal and enforceability; environmental and social; force majeure; sponsor; climate (physical and transition); cyber; decommissioning.
- The method: identify, analyze, quantify, allocate, mitigate, price, monitor. How to build a risk matrix. Residual risk to equity versus debt. What “bankable” really means. Pass-through and back-to-back principles. The gap between allocation on paper and allocation in practice. Why lenders obsess over the downside and sponsors over the upside.
- The mitigation toolkit: contracts, insurance, guarantees, reserves, hedges, structural features, sponsor support, credit enhancement.

### D. The contracts

For each contract: purpose, structure, key clauses, market positions, bankability requirements, negotiation dynamics, interfaces with other contracts, failure modes, and original illustrative clause language.

- Concession, project, and implementation agreements; government support instruments (guarantees, letters of support, comfort letters); termination and compensation regimes.
- Offtake and revenue: PPAs (capacity and energy payments, availability, deemed energy, curtailment, take-or-pay, pass-throughs, indexation, payment security); tolling agreements; contracts for difference; regulated-asset-base models; feed-in tariffs; corporate PPAs, physical and virtual; hedges (fixed-volume, fixed-shape, proxy revenue swaps, floors, collars); capacity and ancillary-service revenues; LNG sale and purchase agreements and tolling; pipeline transportation and ship-or-pay; mining offtake (payables, treatment and refining charges, penalties); streams and royalties; availability payments and deduction regimes; user tolls and tariffs; airport and port revenue models; data-center leases.
- Construction: EPC (lump-sum, turnkey, date-certain); split and multi-contract structures; EPCM; wraps; delay and performance liquidated damages and their calibration; liability caps; bonds, guarantees, and retention; variations, extensions of time, and claims; testing and completion; defects liability; FIDIC and other standard forms; contractor and equipment-supplier credit; supply-chain risk.
- Operations: O&M agreements, long-term service agreements, asset management agreements, major maintenance, performance regimes.
- Inputs and access: fuel and feedstock supply, transportation, water, grid connection and interconnection, land rights, permits and their transferability.
- Corporate and sponsor: shareholders’ and joint venture agreements, equity contribution agreements, sponsor support and completion guarantees, development and co-development agreements.
- Insurance: construction and erection all risks, delay in start-up, marine cargo and marine delay in start-up, third-party liability, operational all risks, business interruption, political risk, credit insurance; lender requirements (loss payee, non-vitiation, cut-through); how insurance-market cycles affect deals.
- Direct agreements, consents, step-in, and novation.
- The contract map as a system: how the whole web interlocks, how to run a gap analysis, and a “Who pays if…?” trace that follows any adverse event through every contract to whoever ultimately bears the loss.

### E. Sources of capital and the instruments

- Debt providers: commercial banks, and how bank capital rules shape their appetite and pricing; ECAs (the OECD Arrangement, cover types, content rules, premia); multilateral and bilateral DFIs (mandates, additionality, preferred creditor status, A/B and parallel loans, guarantees); institutional investors; infrastructure debt funds and private credit.
- Capital markets: project bonds, 144A/Reg S and private placements, green and sustainability bonds, sukuk.
- Other facilities: mezzanine and holdco debt, equity bridge loans, VAT and working-capital facilities, letter-of-credit facilities, standby and cost-overrun facilities.
- Equity: sponsor equity, shareholder loans, contingent and back-ended equity, equity letters of credit, development premia, farm-downs, infrastructure funds, listed vehicles and yieldcos, US tax equity and tax-credit transfer structures (with law-dependent detail dated and flagged).
- Islamic project finance structures and how they sit alongside conventional tranches.
- Blended and concessional finance, first-loss capital, guarantees, viability-gap funding, climate funds, local-currency solutions.

### F. Debt structuring and sizing

- The mathematics and the judgment: CFADS; minimum and average DSCR; LLCR; PLCR; gearing; sculpting; tenor and tail; balloons and mini-perms (hard and soft); grace periods; reserve accounts and how they are funded and sized; cash sweeps; distribution lock-ups; financial and non-financial covenants; hedging requirements and hedge profiles; base, banking, downside, and break-even cases; P90 and P99 sizing; merchant tails. How each parameter varies by sector, contract quality, market, and cycle — and why.
- Pricing: margins and ratchets; arrangement, underwriting, participation, commitment, and agency fees; the all-in cost of debt; market flex.
- Ratings: how agencies analyze projects in construction and in operation.

### G. Financial modeling — a complete course

- From blank workbook to bankable, audited model: architecture and standards (including the FAST standard); timelines and flags; inputs and scenario control; construction budget and funding (sources and uses, drawdown order, equity-first versus pro rata, interest during construction and fee circularity and clean ways to resolve it); revenue and cost builds; tax (depreciation, losses, interest limitation, withholding); working capital; VAT; the operating waterfall and CFADS; debt sculpting; reserves, sweeps, and lock-ups; the dividend trap and how to avoid it; equity returns; ratios; sensitivities, scenarios, breakevens, and Monte Carlo; outputs and dashboards; integrity checks; model audit and the classic errors.
- Sector-specific modeling: energy yield and P-values, degradation and augmentation, availability and curtailment; traffic ramp-up; mining reserves, grades, and recoveries; LNG and commodity-linked revenues; PPP payment mechanisms and deductions.
- Cell-by-cell walkthroughs the reader can follow in a blank workbook, and a companion model for the primary running case.

### H. Equity, valuation, and investment decisions

- Equity IRR, project IRR, NPV, cash yield, and multiples; cost of equity and hurdle rates by stage and risk; valuation at each development milestone; development economics and where value is created; bid pricing in competitive tenders and the winner’s curse; buying and selling operating assets; portfolio construction; how infrastructure funds think, invest, and are paid.

### I. Due diligence

For each workstream: what the advisor does, how to scope the work, how to read the report, and how to challenge it.

- Technical (the independent engineer); resource and reserves; market and price curves; traffic and demand; legal and regulatory; insurance; model audit; tax and accounting; environmental and social (impact assessments, management and action plans, the IFC Performance Standards, the Equator Principles, the World Bank Environmental and Social Framework); counterparty credit; KYC, sanctions, and anti-corruption.

### J. Finance documentation and the legal framework

- The document set and how it flows: mandate and commitment letters, term sheets, common terms agreement, facility agreements, intercreditor agreement, accounts agreement, security documents, direct agreements, hedging under ISDA, fee letters. Representations, conditions precedent, covenants, events of default, remedies, equity cures, permitted debt and distributions, change of control, amendments and voting.
- Security: what lenders take, and why its real purpose is control and protection rather than liquidation; common-law versus civil-law systems (trusts versus parallel debt, floating charges and their absence); perfection; enforcement as a going concern; step-in.
- The accounts structure and cash waterfall in full.
- Intercreditor arrangements: tranches, voting, ECA and DFI rights, hedge counterparties, standstills, enforcement, and the conventional–Islamic interface.
- Governing law, jurisdiction, arbitration (ICC, LCIA, ICSID, UNCITRAL), enforcement of awards, sovereign immunity, stabilization clauses, investment treaties.
- Standard market documentation (LMA, LSTA, APLMA) and how to work with it.

### K. The deal process and the art of negotiation

- Financing strategy (club, underwritten, best efforts; bank versus bond); the financial advisor’s role; information memoranda and lender presentations; term-sheet negotiation; how credit approval works inside a bank; syndication and sell-down; documentation management; satisfying conditions precedent; signing versus financial close; funds flow and closing mechanics; timelines and critical paths.
- Negotiation: what is market and why; the levers each side holds; the typical trade-offs on every key term; tactics, sequencing, and escalation; how terms move with the market cycle; negotiating with governments, contractors, and offtakers as well as lenders.

### L. Public–private partnerships

- Rationale and critiques; value for money and the public sector comparator; affordability; fiscal and statistical treatment and contingent liabilities; PPP units and programs; unsolicited proposals; procurement (prequalification, competitive dialogue, RFPs, best and final offers, evaluation); delivery models (DBFOM, BOT, BOOT, BOO; availability versus user-pay); payment mechanisms and deduction regimes; handback; change, benchmarking, and market testing; termination regimes and compensation formulas; refinancing gain sharing; standard contract guidance; lessons from successes and failures.

### M. International and emerging markets

- Country and sovereign risk analysis; currency risk and its solutions; offshore accounts; payment security for weak offtakers (letters of credit, escrow, guarantees, liquidity facilities); political risk insurance (MIGA, ECAs, the private market); the protective “halo” of multilateral participation; the obsolescing bargain and how to manage it; host-government relations and local content; corruption risk and anti-bribery laws; sanctions; disputes and treaty claims.

### N. The project through its life

- Construction: drawdowns and certification, contingency management, monitoring, change orders, delays and liquidated-damages flows, insurance claims, contractor distress, completion tests and the release of sponsor support.
- Operations: reporting and compliance, budgets, ratio testing and distributions, maintenance, performance management, waivers and amendments, asset optimization (repowering, life extension, hybridization).

- Distress, restructuring, and enforcement: early warning signs, standstills, waivers, amend-and-extend, sponsor support, debt-for-equity swaps, cash sweeps, distressed sales, enforcement over shares and assets, insolvency regimes (such as US Chapter 11, UK schemes of arrangement and restructuring plans, and civil-law regimes), PPP-specific dynamics, and the role of the state.
- Decommissioning, handback, and end-of-life value.

### O. Accounting, tax, and regulation

- Accounting: consolidation and equity accounting, joint arrangements, service concessions (IFRIC 12 and its US counterpart), leases embedded in offtake contracts, hedge accounting, expected credit losses — and how sponsors’ accounting goals shape deal structures.
- Tax: holding structures, treaties, withholding taxes, interest limitation and thin capitalization, transfer pricing, VAT during construction, incentives and tax holidays, the global minimum tax.

## 5. The running cases

Thread three realistic fictional cases through the book so the reader watches concepts compound inside living deals. Design them in the blueprint, each with a complete, internally consistent assumption set recorded in a Case Bible.

1. The primary case: a greenfield, contracted power project in a fictional emerging-market country. Choose the technology that exercises the widest range of concepts — for example, a gas-fired plant with a full fuel-supply chain, or a hybrid solar-plus-storage plant. Give it a state-utility offtaker, an EPC contractor, an O&M operator, and a lender group combining commercial banks, an ECA, and a DFI, with interest-rate and currency hedging. Across the book it must live through origination and development, competitive procurement, contract negotiation, due diligence, the model build, debt sizing, term-sheet negotiation, financial close, a construction delay and cost overrun, an insurance claim, completion testing, operations, an offtaker payment crisis or currency shock, a covenant breach and waiver, a refinancing, and a partial sale.
2. The PPP case: a transport PPP in an OECD country — a toll road or light-rail line, user-pay or availability-based — followed from the government’s decision to procure through the bid, financial close, and ramp-up, and then into either refinancing or distress and restructuring. It carries the public-sector view, demand risk, and PPP mechanics.
3. The portfolio case: operating renewable and storage assets in a liberalized power market with merchant exposure. It carries acquisitions, valuation, hedging, holdco financing, and refinancing.

Give the cases recurring characters — the developer, the lead arranger, sponsor’s counsel, lenders’ counsel, the government official, the EPC project director, the independent engineer — so the reader experiences every negotiation from every seat. Keep the fiction in service of the substance; never let story displace teaching.

All running-case numbers must reconcile across the entire book. Every change — an overrun, a tariff reset, a refinancing — is logged in the Case Bible with the chapter where it happened.

## 6. Anatomy of every chapter

Every chapter contains the following, in whatever order best serves the story:

1. An opening that earns attention: a real deal moment, a disaster, a puzzle, or a scene from a running case that makes the reader need what follows.
2. A short statement, two to four lines, of what the reader will be able to do afterwards, tied to the capability map.
3. The core teaching, built from first principles. For each important concept: the problem it solves, how it works, a worked example, what goes wrong, how practitioners handle it, what is market and what is negotiable, and how it differs across sectors and jurisdictions where that matters.
4. Fully worked examples — numerical, contractual, or both — with every step shown and every number reconciled.
5. At least one walkthrough: a step-by-step tour of a real-world artifact or task, such as a clause, a term sheet, a model sheet, an independent engineer’s report, a cash waterfall, a negotiation, or a closing checklist.
6. Frameworks: named, memorable tools the reader can reuse — established ones credited, original ones fully explained — each demonstrated in use.
7. Real-world cases wherever the topic allows: what happened, why, and the transferable lesson.
8. The running-case installment.
9. A practitioner’s notebook: checklists, red flags, rules of thumb with their limits, common mistakes, and the questions experts ask.
10. A judgment drill: a realistic decision (“You are the lead arranger, and the sponsor asks for…”), followed by how a top practitioner would reason it through, including the trade-offs and what would change the answer.
11. Exercises in three tiers — concept checks; calculations and drafting tasks; case problems and model tasks — with complete worked solutions at the end of the chapter. Scale the number to the chapter’s weight; technical chapters will typically need 10 to 20.
12. A brief close that crystallizes the essential ideas and opens a question the next chapter answers.

Scale each element to its topic — a history chapter needs fewer calculations than a debt-sizing chapter — but every chapter teaches by example, never by assertion. Let the content set the length. As calibration, a core technical chapter at this standard is likely to run 8,000 to 15,000 words. Never pad to reach a length, and never compress to fit one.

## 7. Examples, walkthroughs, frameworks, and real cases

The examples are the book. As a rule, the reader should never go more than two pages without a concrete example, a number, a scene, or a case. Hold every example to these standards:

- Numerical examples use realistic figures, state every unit and currency, show every step, and reconcile: tables sum, sources equal uses, balance sheets balance, ratios recompute. When an example simplifies, say what was simplified and why the lesson survives.
- Vary scale, sector, and geography across examples, so the reader learns the pattern rather than one deal.
- Walkthroughs are concrete enough that the reader could perform the task the next morning.
- Clause and document examples are drafted originally — never reproduced from published standard forms, textbooks, or articles — and annotated line by line. Where negotiation matters, show the sponsor-friendly, lender-friendly, and government-friendly variants and where they usually land.
- Modeling is taught cell by cell, with each formula in mathematical notation and as an Excel formula, so the reader can build every piece in a blank workbook.
- Frameworks must be practical enough to use on a live deal. Examples of the kind wanted: a one-hour deal screen, a “Who pays if…?” trace, a bankability ladder, a contract gap scan.
- Real cases are named, dated, and analyzed for the transferable lesson. Illustrative or composite examples are labeled “Illustrative,” so the reader always knows which is which.

## 8. How the book must read

The book must be a page-turner without losing an ounce of rigor. Aim for the narrative momentum of the best long-form nonfiction, the clarity of a great physics lecturer, and the precision of a top law firm’s practice note.

- Drive the narrative. Open with stakes. Build tension around what could go wrong, who is exposed, and what is at risk. Let deals unfold as stories. End chapters with a pull into the next.
- Go concrete before abstract. Show the example, then generalize.
- Explain mechanisms. Every rule has a reason, every term solves a problem, every market convention has a history and a logic. Give all three.
- Define every term and acronym at first use, then use it consistently.
- Write prose first. Use tables for numbers and comparisons, diagrams for structures and flows, and lists only for true checklists and sequences. Never use bullet points as a substitute for explanation.
- Control rhythm. Vary sentence length, use short sentences for emphasis, keep one idea per paragraph, and prefer active voice and strong verbs.
- Find the voice of a brilliant senior colleague: confident, warm, candid, occasionally wry, invested in the reader’s success, and honest about what is hard, contested, or unknown.
- Show the human reality of deals — incentives, politics, fatigue, the late-night call, the moment a deal nearly dies — because that is where deals are won and lost.
- Address the reader as a future colleague, never as a student to be lectured.

Banned outright: throat-clearing (“In this chapter we will…”, “It is important to note that…”); filler transitions; empty superlatives; motivational fluff; hedging beyond what accuracy requires; generic statements that could appear in any book; explanations repeated from earlier chapters (cross-reference instead); meta-commentary about the writing; and every sign of a draft — placeholders, “TBD”, “\[insert\]”, “for brevity”, “details omitted”, or promises of content that never arrives.

### AI writing tics to avoid

Readers should never suspect a machine wrote this book. The patterns below are the tells of machine-generated prose, and every one is banned from the book’s text. Each counts as a banned pattern under the prose test in Section 12.

Two boundaries keep this list consistent with the rest of the prompt. First, it governs how things are said, never what Section 6 requires. Chapters still open with a hook, still close by crystallizing the ideas and pulling the reader forward, and still contain every element Section 6 lists; they simply never get there through the formulas below. Second, where a listed word is a precise technical term, use it in that sense: leverage meaning debt, the critical path of a construction schedule, the bottom line of an income statement, comprehensive cover from an ECA, key performance indicators in a sustainability-linked loan, fuel in a power plant, stakeholder engagement under the IFC Performance Standards. Drafted clause language follows legal drafting conventions, so a facility agreement may speak of a “Utilisation Request.” The ban is on decorative use.

#### Sentence-level constructions

- False-contrast reframes: “It’s not X, it’s Y”; “This isn’t about X. It’s about Y”; “X isn’t just A; it’s B”; “more than just a loan”; “less A than B.” State the positive claim directly, and correct a misconception only when the reader actually holds it.
- Reflexive balancing: “not only… but also”; “both… and”; “While X, it’s important to remember Y.”
- Tacked-on significance clauses, the participle at the end of a sentence that claims importance: “…, highlighting the importance of X”; “…, underscoring the need for Y”; “…, reflecting a broader shift”; “…, paving the way for Z”; “…, making it a cornerstone of…”; “…, ensuring that…”. If the consequence matters, make it its own claim and show the mechanism.
- Copula avoidance: “serves as,” “stands as,” “functions as,” “acts as,” “represents,” “constitutes,” and “boasts” where “is” or “has” is meant.
- Role inflation: “plays a crucial role in”; “is a key component of”; “is a critical factor in.” Say what the thing does.
- A vague “This” or “That” opening a sentence to point at a whole previous idea (“This means…”; “This allows…”; “This is why…”). Name the noun.
- Hedge stacking: “may potentially”; “could possibly”; “can often”; “in many cases”; “generally speaking”; “it is generally considered”; “to some extent”; “in a sense”; “in many ways”; “it’s fair to say”; “it’s safe to say.” Hedge once, precisely, and only when the uncertainty is real.
- Reflexive “can” and “may.” Write “lenders require” when lenders require it, and reserve “can” for genuine options.
- Filler intensifiers: very, extremely, highly, particularly, really.
- Nominalizations outside legal drafting: “the implementation of”; “the utilization of”; “the optimization of”; “the provision of.” Use the verb.
- Padding: “in order to”; “due to the fact that”; “the fact that”; “at this point in time”; “each and every”; “with regard to”; “in terms of.”
- Elegant variation: rotating synonyms for one thing (project company, SPV, vehicle, entity, borrower) to avoid repetition. In a technical book a new word signals a new thing, so use the defined term every time.
- Default sentence shapes: “By doing X, Y can Z”; “It is X that…”; “There are many…”; and breadth-faking ranges such as “from rooftop solar to mega-LNG.”
- Stacks of hyphenated compound adjectives.

#### Paragraph and structure patterns

- The reflexive rule of three: triplets of adjectives, nouns, or clauses as a default cadence, and lists that always run to three or five items. Use as many items as the content has.
- The zinger: a paragraph that ends on a one-line aphorism or moral (“And that is the whole game.”).
- The summary sandwich: announcing what you will say, saying it, then restating it; and sections that end by repeating their own point in new words.
- Signposted enumeration (“There are three reasons. First… Second… Third…”) as the default way to build an argument.
- Monotone rhythm: paragraph after paragraph of the same length and the same shape (topic sentence, elaboration, example, wrap-up).
- Fragment drama: drumbeats of short fragments (“Billions. Gone. Overnight.”). A single short sentence for emphasis is fine.
- Rhetorical-question stacks (“Who bears the risk? Who pays? Who decides?”) and self-answered questions used as transitions (“Is this a problem? Not necessarily.”). Allow at most one rhetorical question at a time, and only when the answer is not obvious.
- The pivot to positivity: following every failure with “But there’s good news.”
- “It depends” without saying on what, by how much, and in which direction; “there are many factors to consider” followed by an unranked list. Name the factors, rank them, and show which way each one cuts.
- Superficial comprehensiveness: ten factors at a line each instead of the three that decide the outcome, explained properly.
- Non-conclusions such as “Ultimately, the right choice depends on your specific circumstances.” Say what an expert would choose, when, and why.
- Hollow bridge paragraphs whose only job is to connect two sections.

#### Narrative and character tics

These apply to running-case scenes and to openings built on real deals.

- Stock scene openers: the clock time (“It was 2:47 a.m. when…”), the weather, the buzzing phone, the email that changed everything, the cold coffee, the humming fluorescent lights, the city lights glittering below the office tower. A detail earns its place only if it matters to the story.
- Body-language and emotion clichés: a tightening jaw; widening eyes; a breath she didn’t know she was holding; a pounding heart; whitening knuckles; a smile tugging at a mouth; a wry smile; a raised eyebrow; leaning back in a chair; exchanged glances; a hand run through hair; a pinched bridge of the nose; steepled fingers; a voice barely above a whisper; a flicker of doubt or hope; the weight of a decision settling on someone’s shoulders; a heavy silence; a room falling silent; letting the silence stretch; words hanging in the air; a sound somewhere in the distance.
- Epiphany lines: “Something shifted”; “In that moment, she understood…”; “It was then that he realized…”; “Suddenly, it all made sense.”
- Characters as mouthpieces: polished paragraph-length speeches, “As you know…” exposition, everyone articulate, nobody interrupting, no subtext, nobody wrong. Real negotiators interrupt, posture, bluff, concede badly, and misread each other.
- Tidy morals spoken aloud or narrated at a scene’s end (“And that, she realized, was the real lesson of project finance.”).
- Melodrama: “everything was on the line”; “it all came down to this”; “against all odds.”
- Cliffhanger formulas: “Little did they know…”; “What happened next would…”; “But that was only the beginning.” The pull at a chapter’s end comes from a real open problem.
- The stock cast that language models overuse, such as Sarah Chen, Elena Vasquez, Marcus Thorne, Aris Thorne, Elara Vance, and Kai. Choose names that fit each character’s country, generation, and background, and record them in the Case Bible.

#### Formatting and punctuation

- Em dashes are the signature tic. Use at most one dash or dash pair in a paragraph, and leave most paragraphs without one. A comma, colon, parenthesis, or new sentence almost always works better.
- Colon reveals as a default device (“The result: chaos.” “The lesson: never trust a traffic forecast.”).
- Exclamation marks outside quoted dialogue; dramatic ellipses; scare quotes beyond marking a term at its first definition; arrows (→) in prose; ampersands; slashes such as “and/or” outside drafted clauses.
- Bullets that open with a bolded label and a colon, and bold or italics used as constant emphasis. Bold marks defined terms at first use if the style sheet adopts that convention, and nothing else.
- Decorative callout labels: “Pro tip”; “Key insight”; “Remember”; “Fun fact”; “TL;DR”; boxed “Key Takeaways.” The elements Section 6 requires carry plain, specific titles.
- Emoji, checkmarks, and decorative symbols.
- Tables, headings, or bullets where two sentences would do; nested bullets; decorative horizontal rules; numbered lists where order doesn’t matter.

#### Tone toward the reader and the subject

- Flattery and reassurance: “Great question”; “You’re probably wondering…”; “You might be thinking…”; “Don’t worry”; “Fear not”; “Rest assured”; “You’ve got this”; “This may sound intimidating, but…”; “It’s easier than it sounds.”
- Narrating the reader’s feelings or progress: “You may feel overwhelmed”; “your project finance journey”; “This chapter will take you on a journey.”
- Announcing candor or importance instead of showing it: “To be frank”; “Candidly”; “I’ll be honest”; “This is important”; “Pay close attention.”
- Grandiose claims about the field (“Project finance has transformed the world”; “a fascinating and dynamic field”) and self-praise of the text (“this comprehensive guide”; “this in-depth chapter”).
- Inspirational endings and calls to action.
- Simplifier labels: “In layman’s terms”; “In plain English”; “Basically.” Explain plainly without announcing it.

#### Epistemic tics

- Vague attribution: “studies show”; “research suggests”; “experts agree”; “many believe”; “it is widely recognized”; “industry insiders say.” Name the source or drop the claim.
- Decorated numbers: “a staggering”; “a whopping”; “an eye-watering”; “a mere.” Give the number and its context, and let the reader judge.
- Suspiciously round or symmetrical figures in illustrative examples. Real numbers are lumpy.
- False balance: presenting settled practice as a two-sided debate.
- “There’s no one-size-fits-all answer” and “every deal is different” used in place of guidance.
- Reflexive disclaimers repeated through the text (“consult a qualified professional”; “this is not legal advice”). State any general caveat once in the front matter. Where a specific point needs one, say exactly what to check and with whom.
- Generic examples that could be set anywhere (“a large infrastructure project in a developing country”). Specify the sector, size, place, date, and parties.

#### Assistant-voice leakage

- No chat wrapper around the book: no “Certainly!”, “Great question!”, “Here is Chapter 7:”, “I hope this helps”, “Let me know if…”, “Would you like me to…”, “As an AI…”, “As of my last update”, or “In this response…”. Chapter files contain book text only; status notes, reports, and logs live in their own files.

#### Applying the list

After drafting each chapter, scan it for every pattern on this list and rewrite each instance by saying the specific thing plainly. Swapping in a synonym only breeds a new tic: replacing every “delve” with “dig into,” or every em dash with a semicolon, is the same failure. If a sentence exists only to sound impressive, delete it.

## 9. Accuracy and integrity

A textbook that is wrong is worse than none. These rules override every other instruction:

- Never invent facts about real projects, companies, people, laws, court decisions, or market data. For real cases, state only what you are confident is accurate. Use approximate figures only when you are sure of their order of magnitude, and mark them “approximately.” Where details are uncertain, teach the case at the level of what is well established, or choose a different case.
- If you have web search, verify the key facts of every real case and every time-sensitive statement before you write it, and list the sources at the end of the chapter.
- Market norms — ratios, tenors, margins, fees, reserve sizes, gearing — move with sector, region, and cycle. Present them as indicative ranges, state the market and period they reflect, and teach the drivers so the reader can update them.
- Laws, regulations, tax rules, and policy programs change. State the jurisdiction and the as-of date, teach the underlying principle, and tell the reader where the current rule is found.
- Never quote real people or documents unless you are certain of the exact words; prefer paraphrase.
- Every calculation must be correct. If you can run code, use it to compute and check every number and to build the running-case model. If you cannot, compute carefully and re-verify before writing.
- Where expert practitioners genuinely disagree, present the positions fairly, then say which you favor and why.
- Write everything originally.

## 10. Format conventions

- Markdown throughout. Number the structure: Part, Chapter, section (7.3), subsection (7.3.2). Number examples (Example 7.4), exhibits and tables (Exhibit 7.2), and exercises (Exercise 7.11).
- Formulas in LaTeX — inline with `$…$`, display with `$$…$$` — each followed by its Excel implementation where relevant.
- Diagrams as Mermaid code blocks: contract maps, cash waterfalls, timelines, decision trees, process flows. Numbers go in tables.
- Units and currencies always explicit; one number format throughout, set in the style sheet (for example, USD millions to one decimal place).
- One variety of English throughout, chosen in the style sheet.
- Cross-references by section number — never “as discussed earlier” without a pointer.

## 11. Production: a parallel agent team

Do not write this book chapter by chapter in conversation. You are its editor-in-chief and production lead, directing a team of sub-agents, and you will deliver the complete book in one autonomous run: plan centrally, write in parallel, review independently, integrate centrally. Do not stop to ask me questions or wait for approval. Make every decision yourself, log it in `bible/decisions.md`, and keep going until the whole book, its front and back matter, and its companion model are finished and verified. If this environment cannot launch sub-agents or write files, tell me before you start; do not fall back to writing sequentially.

### Team rules

- Run every agent on the most capable model available. Never hand a writing or review task to a smaller model.
- Only you dispatch agents; sub-agents do not launch their own.
- Sub-agents see nothing you do not give them. Save this entire prompt to the workspace as `standards.md`, and require every agent to read it in full, along with every file its brief names, before starting work.
- Agents communicate through files. Each agent writes its output to its assigned path and returns only a short status note: done or blocked, word count, any deviation from its brief, new terms, and verification flags. Keep full chapters out of your own context; work from files, status notes, and reviewer reports.
- Keep a production tracker on disk (`bible/tracker.md`) recording every assignment, status, review round, and open issue, so you can recover your state at any point.
- Run as many agents in parallel as the environment allows, dispatching in waves.

### Workspace

Create this structure before anything else:

```text
book/
  standards.md   this prompt, verbatim
  bible/         blueprint, Case Bible, style sheet, glossary canon, anchor registry, decisions log, tracker
  facts/         verified fact sheets for real cases and time-sensitive topics
  model/         running-case models: Excel with live formulas, plus a Python mirror
  chapters/      one file per chapter, e.g. 07-debt-sizing-and-sculpting.md
  reviews/       reviewer reports, per chapter and round
```

### Phase 1: Architecture

Fix everything the parallel writers depend on (together, the Bible) before anyone drafts a chapter, using specialist agents where useful.

1. The blueprint: title, premise, and reader’s promise; the capability map; and the complete table of contents down to subsection level, with numbering locked. For every chapter, a brief: purpose; capabilities built; concepts it owns; concepts it assumes, with the chapter that teaches each; its worked examples, walkthroughs, frameworks, judgment drill, real cases, running-case installment, and exercise set; target length; and one-paragraph summaries of the chapters on either side.
2. Concept ownership: every concept has exactly one home chapter where it is taught in full. Other chapters cross-reference it and never re-teach it. No chapter relies on a concept taught later, except through a brief, explicit forward reference.
3. The glossary canon: the canonical definition, abbreviation, and home section of every term. Every writer uses this terminology exactly.
4. The anchor registry: every section number, and every named exhibit, example, and framework that other chapters may cite.
5. The Case Bible: the three running cases, with characters, timelines, and storylines by chapter. A modeling agent builds the reference model for each case: a real Excel workbook with live formulas, plus a Python mirror that reproduces it exactly. The Case Bible then records the state of each case at the start and end of every chapter, including every figure that chapter will show. Writers take running-case numbers only from the Case Bible and never compute their own.
6. Fact sheets: research agents verify every real case in the real-case map and every time-sensitive topic (market norms, laws, policy programs), writing one fact sheet each, with sources, as-of dates, confidence levels, and the angle each assigned chapter will take. Writers use facts from the fact sheets only, or verify new ones themselves and report them.
7. The style sheet: voice, variety of English, notation, number formats, labeling conventions, and a sample passage of about 200 words.
8. Blueprint review: a panel of reviewer agents audits the blueprint against the coverage map, the capability map, the sequencing rules, and the standards in Sections 6 to 10. Revise until the panel passes it.

### Phase 2: Pilot

Before the main wave, two writers draft two contrasting chapters, one narrative-heavy and one calculation-heavy, and both go through the full review cycle of Phase 4. The approved versions become voice and quality exemplars for every later writer. Fix any weakness the pilot exposes in the briefs or the Bible before scaling up.

### Phase 3: Parallel drafting

Dispatch one writer agent per remaining chapter. Each writer:

- takes the expert lens the chapter needs (lawyer for contracts, modeler for modeling, banker for structuring) while writing in the book’s single voice;
- reads `standards.md`, the style sheet, the pilot exemplars, its chapter brief and the neighboring summaries, the glossary canon, the anchor registry, its slice of the Case Bible, and its fact sheets;
- writes the chapter into its file section by section, at full depth, never compressing to fit a limit;
- runs the quality gate in Section 12 before reporting done;
- reports any flaw it finds in its brief or the Bible instead of silently deviating. You resolve each report centrally, update the Bible, and notify every affected agent.

### Phase 4: Independent review and revision

Each drafted chapter goes to six reviewer agents in parallel, none of whom wrote it:

1. a domain expert in the chapter’s subject, applying the veteran test;
2. a novice reader who knows only what the preceding chapters teach, applying the novice test;
3. a numbers auditor who recomputes every figure, example, and exercise solution with code and checks every running-case number against the model;
4. a fact-checker who verifies every real-world statement against the fact sheets or fresh sources;
5. a line editor who applies Section 8, including a full sweep for the AI writing tics;
6. a consistency checker who verifies terminology, cross-references, concept ownership, and running-case continuity against the Bible.

Each reviewer writes a report listing every defect, its location, and the required fix. Send the chapter and the consolidated reports back for revision (to the original writer if your environment can resume it, otherwise to a fresh writer with the same brief), then return the chapter to review. Repeat until all six reviewers pass it. If a chapter fails three rounds, find the root cause (usually a flawed brief or Bible entry), fix it, and re-dispatch.

### Phase 5: Integration

Once every chapter has passed review, Part editors each read one Part in order and repair the seams: openings and closings that connect, cross-references that resolve, no repeated teaching, no terminology drift, no running-case contradictions. A whole-book editor then reads the manuscript in order, one Part at a time, carrying a running log of voice, pacing, and continuity notes forward, and makes the line-level changes that turn it into one author’s book. Re-run the numbers and consistency checks on everything integration changed.

### Phase 6: Front matter, back matter, capstone, and examination

Write these from the finished chapters. Compile the glossary from the definitions as they actually appear, the formula sheet from the formulas actually used, and the checklists and templates from the chapters’ practitioner material. The capstone and the final examination each go through the full review cycle.

### Phase 7: Final verification

Script every mechanical check: banned-phrase and em-dash scans, undefined abbreviations, unresolved cross-references, exercise–solution pairing, word counts against targets, Mermaid and LaTeX validity, and any figure that disagrees with the model. Then a final audit agent confirms, with locations, that every capability and every coverage-map item is delivered. Fix everything found.

### Phase 8: Delivery

Assemble the complete manuscript into one Markdown file with a full table of contents, plus Word and PDF versions if your tools allow. Then report to me: where every file is, total and per-chapter word counts, the decisions log, and any verification flag that could not be closed.

## 12. The quality gate

Every writer runs these checks before reporting a chapter done, and the Phase 4 reviewers enforce them. Nothing is finished until it passes all eight. Never print the checklist in the book.

1. Completeness: everything the blueprint promised for this chapter is fully taught — nothing outlined, deferred, or placeholdered.
2. The novice test: a smart reader with only the preceding chapters can follow every step without outside help.
3. The veteran test: a practitioner with 25 years’ experience would find it accurate, current, and non-trivial — and would learn something.
4. The numbers test: every figure is computed, verified, reconciled, and consistent with the Case Bible.
5. The example test: every important concept is demonstrated, not merely described.
6. The truth test: every real-world fact is one you are confident of or have verified, and illustrative material is labeled.
7. The prose test: the opening pulls the reader in, no banned pattern appears, and the ending pulls them forward.
8. The practice test: the exercises span all three tiers, and every solution is complete and correct.
